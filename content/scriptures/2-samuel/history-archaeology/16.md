## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** None of the events can be checked externally. Shimei's curse, though, is historically revealing: it voices a Saulide accusation that David was a "man of blood" who took Saul's throne. That accusation is exactly what the apologetic sections of Samuel work to refute. Absalom's public taking of his father's concubines reflects a known ancient symbolism of royal succession.

## What the Text Claims

- **Ziba**, Mephibosheth's steward, meets David with provisions and claims that his master hopes to regain Saul's kingdom. David transfers Mephibosheth's property to Ziba (16:1–4).
- At **Bahurim**, **Shimei son of Gera** of Saul's clan curses David: "Out, out, you man of blood… the LORD has avenged on you all the blood of the house of Saul" (16:5–14).
- In Jerusalem, on **Ahithophel's** advice, Absalom goes in to David's ten concubines in a tent on the roof "in the sight of all Israel" (16:15–23).

## External Evidence

- No extra-biblical references to these figures exist. **Bahurim** is a Benjaminite village east of Jerusalem, sometimes located at Ras et-Tmim; its identification is uncertain.
- **Harem and succession:** Taking a predecessor's women as a claim to the throne appears elsewhere in the Bible (Abner and Rizpah, 3:7; Adonijah's request for Abishag, 1 Kgs 2:13–25). Comparable practices are attested more broadly. Herodotus (3.68–88) reports that the Persian usurper and then Darius I took over the previous king's wives.

## Points of Agreement

- **Saulide hostility.** Benjaminite resentment of David runs through 2 Samuel: Shimei here, Sheba in chapter 20, the Gibeonite executions in chapter 21. It is coherent and politically realistic for a dynasty that displaced Saul's house. Historians such as Halpern treat Shimei's charge as an authentic echo of opposition propaganda.
- **Competing loyalties among Saul's former retainers** (Ziba vs. Mephibosheth, see 19:24–30) are typical of dynastic transitions.

## Points of Conflict

- **Which account is true?** Ziba's accusation against Mephibosheth (16:3) is contradicted by Mephibosheth's later defense (19:24–30). The narrator never resolves it. This is a literary ambiguity rather than a historical conflict, but it shows that the Court History records competing versions without adjudicating them.
- **"The blood of the house of Saul":** Shimei's charge is not explained by anything earlier in the narrative, unless it refers to the execution of Saul's seven descendants in chapter 21. That chapter's displacement to the appendix may hide its original chronological position.

## Assessment

Chapter 16 offers no verifiable facts. It is valuable, though, for showing the political opposition that 2 Samuel's apologetic answers. Shimei's curse states the case *against* David, and the narrative preserves it in order to answer it. That is a strong indicator, as McCarter and Halpern argue, that real accusations circulated about David's role in the downfall of Saul's family.
