## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter has no external corroboration. Its two accounts of Saul's death contradict each other, and its main purpose is apologetic: to clear David of any part in Saul's fall. The lament in 1:19–27 may be genuinely old poetry drawn from a lost written source.

## What the Text Claims

- Three days after returning to **Ziklag**, David receives an Amalekite who says he killed the wounded Saul at Saul's request on Mount Gilboa and brings Saul's crown and armlet (1:1–10).
- David has the Amalekite executed for killing "the LORD's anointed" (1:13–16).
- David composes a lament for Saul and Jonathan ("How the mighty have fallen"), which is said to be written in the **Book of Jashar** (1:17–27).

## External Evidence

### No external record of Saul or his death

No inscription names Saul, Jonathan, or a battle at Gilboa. Saul's kingdom is attested only in the Bible. Israel Finkelstein (*The Forgotten Kingdom*, 2013) argues that the territories later attributed to Ishbaal (2 Sam 2:9) reflect a real 10th-century polity on the Benjamin plateau and in Gilead. His argument is an inference from settlement patterns and from the biblical text, not from a named source.

### Amalekites

The Amalekites are **not attested in any extra-biblical source**. They appear in the Bible as southern nomads and the archetypal enemy (Exod 17; 1 Sam 15). Since they left no material culture that can be identified as theirs, their history cannot be checked.

### Ziklag

Ziklag's location is uncertain. Candidates include Tel Sera', Tel Halif, and Khirbet a-Ra'i. Yosef Garfinkel and colleagues proposed Khirbet a-Ra'i in 2019, citing an early Iron Age IIA settlement over a Philistine-period phase. That identification is debated.

### The Book of Jashar

The "Book of Jashar" is also cited in Joshua 10:13. It seems to have been an anthology of old poetry, and it is lost. The citation shows that the editor used written sources, but those sources cannot be dated.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistine military pressure on the central highlands and the Jezreel Valley in Iron I–early Iron IIA fits the archaeological picture of Philistine expansion and highland–lowland conflict.
- The lament's archaic vocabulary and lack of religious content have led many scholars, including McCarter, to regard it as one of the oldest compositions in the book. It may even go back to David.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Samuel 1 | Other evidence |
|------------|----------------|
| An Amalekite kills Saul at his request (1:10) | 1 Sam 31:4–5: Saul falls on his own sword; his armor-bearer dies too |
| The Amalekite has Saul's crown | 1 Sam 31:9: the Philistines strip Saul's body the next day |

Harmonizers suggest the Amalekite lied to win David's favor, and this is a reasonable reading of the story as it stands. Source critics usually see two independent traditions joined together.

## Assessment

The chapter is shaped by royal apologetic, a genre P. Kyle McCarter compared to Hittite and Assyrian usurper texts. David profits from Saul's death and receives the regalia, but the text insists that he grieved and punished the killer. The repetition of this pattern (compare chapters 3 and 4) suggests that David's enemies held him responsible. The narrative events cannot be checked. The lament is the chapter's most likely authentic early material.
