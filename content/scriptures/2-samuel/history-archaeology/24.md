## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter is an etiology, a story explaining why an altar stood at the threshing floor of Araunah in Jerusalem. It is framed by a theological problem (why a census is sinful). Its population figures are impossible for the 10th century. Its parallel in 1 Chronicles 21 changes the theology, the numbers, and the price, which shows how freely the tradition was reworked.

## What the Text Claims

- "The anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and **he incited David**" to count Israel and Judah (24:1).
- Joab's census circuit runs from Aroer and Gad through Gilead, Dan, Sidon, and Tyre to Beersheba. It counts **800,000** fighting men in Israel and **500,000** in Judah (24:2–9).
- David chooses three days of plague, and **70,000** die (24:10–17).
- David buys the **threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite** for **50 shekels of silver**, builds an altar, and the plague stops (24:18–25).

## External Evidence

### Census and ritual danger

E. A. Speiser (1958) pointed to texts from **Mari** (18th c. BCE) in which military registration was accompanied by purification rites. That parallels Exod 30:11–16, where each person counted pays a ransom "so that no plague may come upon them." The idea that a census is ritually dangerous is therefore genuinely ancient. The parallel does not date the story.

### Population figures

1.3 million fighting men implies a total population of perhaps 4–5 million. Survey-based estimates (Broshi and Finkelstein, 1992) put the population of the whole land west of the Jordan at about 400,000 even at its 8th-century peak. The 10th-century figure was much lower. The numbers are impossible as history.

### The name Araunah

"Araunah" is spelled several ways in the Hebrew text (*'wrnh*, *'rwnh*, *h'wrnh*), and Chronicles has **Ornan**. Many scholars connect it with the **Hurrian** word *ewri* / *ewir-ne*, "lord," so it may be a title rather than a name. A Hurrian element in pre-Israelite Jerusalem is plausible, since the Amarna-era ruler **Abdi-Heba** bears a name containing the Hurrian goddess Hebat.

### The Temple site

2 Samuel does not say the threshing floor became the Temple site. **2 Chr 3:1** makes the identification and also equates the place with "Mount Moriah" (Gen 22). No archaeological remains of the First Temple have been identified on the Temple Mount, where excavation is not possible.

## Points of Agreement

- The ritual danger of census-taking is attested in the ancient Near East (Mari) and in Israelite law (Exod 30).
- A Jebusite or Hurrian landowner in early Jerusalem fits what the Amarna letters reveal about the city's pre-Israelite population.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Samuel 24 | 1 Chronicles 21 |
|-------------|-----------------|
| **YHWH** incites David (24:1) | **Satan** (or "an adversary") incites David (21:1) |
| Israel 800,000; Judah 500,000 | Israel 1,100,000; Judah 470,000 (Levi and Benjamin not counted) |
| **Seven** years of famine offered (24:13, MT) | **Three** years (21:12; also LXX of 2 Samuel) |
| 50 shekels of **silver** for floor and oxen | 600 shekels of **gold** for the site |
| Araunah | Ornan |
| David sees the angel (24:17) | David and the elders see the angel standing between earth and heaven (21:16), a reading partly shared by 4QSam<sup>a</sup> |

The shift from YHWH to Satan is one of the clearest examples of theological development within the Hebrew Bible. A post-exilic author (4th c. BCE) was uncomfortable attributing an incitement to sin directly to God. The figure of a heavenly adversary, also found in Job 1–2 and Zech 3, became available to him. The increased price reflects the later importance of the Temple site. The Qumran manuscript 4QSam<sup>a</sup> sometimes agrees with Chronicles against the Masoretic text, which shows that the Chronicler used a different Samuel text form, not only that he invented changes.

## Assessment

2 Samuel 24 is a cult-foundation legend. Its purpose is to explain the sanctity of a Jerusalem altar site, and it links that site to David. The census itself may echo a real royal registration for military or tax purposes, which states routinely carried out. But the numbers, the plague, and the angel are theological narrative. The comparison with Chronicles is historically the most instructive feature of the chapter: it shows ancient authors revising theology and figures within a few centuries.
