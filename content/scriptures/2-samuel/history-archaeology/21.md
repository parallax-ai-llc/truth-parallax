## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Chapters 21–24 form an appendix of older miscellaneous traditions. The execution of seven Saulides may preserve a real and politically convenient purge. The notice in 21:19 that **Elhanan killed Goliath** is widely regarded as the older tradition, later transferred to David in 1 Samuel 17. Chronicles' harmonization ("Lahmi the brother of Goliath") is a secondary correction.

## What the Text Claims

- A three-year famine is blamed on Saul's massacre of the **Gibeonites**, which violated an oath (Josh 9). David hands over **seven of Saul's descendants**, and the Gibeonites impale or expose them at Gibeah (21:1–9).
- **Rizpah** guards the bodies until the rains. David reburies the bones of Saul and Jonathan at Zela (21:10–14).
- Four Philistine "descendants of the giant" (*Rapha*) from Gath are killed by David's men. In 21:19: "**Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite killed Goliath the Gittite**, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam" (21:15–22).

## External Evidence

### Gath and the name "Goliath"

Aren Maeir's excavations at **Tell es-Safi/Gath** show that it was a major Philistine city in Iron I–IIA, until Hazael destroyed it c. 830 BCE (compare 2 Kgs 12:17). In 2005 an ostracon dated to the 10th–early 9th centuries was found there. It bears two names, **'LWT** and **WLT**, which are non-Semitic and etymologically comparable to "Goliath." It does **not** mention Goliath, but it shows that names of this type were used at Gath in roughly the right period.

### Gibeon

Gibeon (el-Jib) is securely identified from inscribed jar handles (Pritchard). Saul's massacre of the Gibeonites is mentioned nowhere else, even in 1 Samuel, and has no external evidence.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistine Gath was powerful in the 10th century, and the tradition of warriors from Gath fits the archaeology.
- Blaming a national disaster on a broken oath has a well-known ancient Near Eastern parallel: the Hittite **plague prayers of Mursili II** (14th c. BCE) trace a long plague to his father's violation of a treaty sworn before the gods.
- The removal of potential Saulide claimants is politically logical, as Shimei's charge (16:7–8) suggests.

## Points of Conflict

| Text | Who killed Goliath? |
|------|---------------------|
| 2 Sam 21:19 | **Elhanan** of Bethlehem killed Goliath the Gittite |
| 1 Sam 17 | **David** killed the Philistine (named Goliath only in 17:4, 23) |
| 1 Chr 20:5 | Elhanan son of Jair killed **Lahmi the brother of Goliath** |

Most critical scholars (e.g., McCarter, Halpern) conclude:
- The Chronicler's "Lahmi" was created from "Beth-lehemite" (*bet hallahmi* → *'et-lahmi*) to remove the contradiction.
- "Jaare-**oregim**" is a scribal error copied from "weaver's beam" (*menor* **'oregim**) later in the verse.
- The heroic deed belonged originally to Elhanan, and the name Goliath was later attached to David's anonymous Philistine in 1 Sam 17, which mostly calls him simply "the Philistine."

**Harmonizing proposals**, favored in conservative scholarship, hold that Elhanan was another name for David (a regnal-name theory associated with A. M. Honeyman, 1948), or that 1 Chr 20:5 preserves the original text. Neither proposal is widely accepted.

Other issues:
- **Placement:** The Gibeonite episode probably belongs before chapter 9 (see 9:1; 21:7) and has been moved to the appendix.
- **Merab or Michal?** 21:8 (MT) says the five sons were born to **Michal**, who was childless (6:23). Some manuscripts and most translations read **Merab** (compare 1 Sam 18:19).

## Assessment

The appendix preserves rough, older traditions that the main narrative smoothed over. The Saulide purge is plausible and politically suspicious. The Elhanan notice is a rare example where Samuel itself preserves evidence that one of David's most famous deeds was attributed to him after the fact. Chronicles' version shows that ancient readers already saw the problem and corrected it.
