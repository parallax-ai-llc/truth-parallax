## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** It is plausible that a ruler of Jerusalem would install a revered cult object to legitimize a new capital. The ark itself cannot be verified, however, and the story's religious content belongs to the cultic legend of the Jerusalem sanctuary. Recent excavations at Kiriath-jearim suggest that the ark tradition may have been shaped in the 8th century BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- David brings the **ark of God** from Baale-judah (Kiriath-jearim) on a new cart (6:1–5).
- **Uzzah** touches the ark to steady it and is struck dead (6:6–11). The ark stays three months with Obed-edom the Gittite.
- David brings it to the city of David with sacrifices and dances "before the LORD" in a linen ephod (6:12–19).
- **Michal** despises him. She remains childless (6:20–23).

## External Evidence

### The ark

No physical or textual trace of the ark exists outside the Bible. Portable divine thrones and shrines carried in procession are well attested in Egypt (barque shrines) and in Mesopotamian festivals, so the concept is at home in the ancient Near East. These parallels show what kind of object the ark was imagined to be. They do not show that this particular one existed.

### Kiriath-jearim

From 2017, Israel Finkelstein, Thomas Römer, and Christophe Nicolle excavated at **Kiriath-jearim** (Deir el-Azar). They found a large artificial platform on the summit, which they date to the first half of the 8th century BCE and associate with Jeroboam II of Israel. Römer and Finkelstein argue that the ark tradition centered on Kiriath-jearim (1 Sam 6–7; 2 Sam 6) was developed or adopted in that period. If so, the transfer story would be a Judahite claim to an Israelite cult tradition. This is an interpretation of the site, and it remains under discussion.

### The "Ark Narrative" source

Leonhard Rost (1926) proposed an independent **Ark Narrative** (1 Sam 4:1b–7:1 + 2 Sam 6). Many scholars accept a separate source but disagree on its date. Proposals run from the 10th century (Patrick Miller and J. J. M. Roberts, *The Hand of the Lord*, 1977) to the late monarchy or exile.

## Points of Agreement

- Kings across the Near East founded or renewed capitals by installing a national god's emblem with royal festival. Examples are Akhenaten at Amarna and Assyrian kings at new capitals such as Kalhu. A David bringing an older tribal palladium into a conquered town would fit this pattern.
- Obed-edom "the Gittite" (from Gath) is one of several Philistine-connected figures around David (compare Ittai, 2 Sam 15). That fits the later evidence for foreign mercenaries in his service.

## Points of Conflict

- **Uzzah's death and the miraculous blessing** of Obed-edom's household belong to sacred legend and cannot be evaluated historically.
- **The parallel in 1 Chr 13 and 15** expands the event into a Levitical procession with organized singers and musicians. This reflects Second Temple cultic arrangements and is plainly anachronistic for c. 1000 BCE.
- **The date of the tradition:** If the Kiriath-jearim results are interpreted correctly, the ark story as we have it may be 8th century or later in its shaping. It would then not be a 10th-century record.

## Assessment

The chapter is a foundation legend for Jerusalem's royal sanctuary. Its political logic is credible: a new dynasty legitimizes a new capital with an ancestral cult object. Its details cannot be checked, however, and parts are clearly theological. The Michal scene ends the hope of a Saulide heir through David, and it serves the book's dynastic apologetic more than the record.
