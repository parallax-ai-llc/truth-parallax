## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter opens the **Court History** or **Succession Narrative** (2 Sam 9–20; 1 Kgs 1–2). It has no external corroboration. Its realism is literary rather than documentary. Keeping a surviving heir of the previous dynasty at court, where he can be watched, is politically credible.

## What the Text Claims

- David asks whether anyone of Saul's house remains, "that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake" (9:1).
- Saul's servant **Ziba** reports that Jonathan's lame son **Mephibosheth** lives at **Lo-debar** in the house of Machir son of Ammiel (9:2–4).
- David restores Saul's land to Mephibosheth, assigns Ziba and his household to farm it, and has Mephibosheth eat at the king's table (9:5–13).

## External Evidence

There is no extra-biblical mention of any person here. **Lo-debar** in Gilead (compare Amos 6:13) has not been securely located.

### The Succession Narrative debate

- **Leonhard Rost** (1926) defined 2 Sam 9–20 + 1 Kgs 1–2 as a unified source whose theme is "who will sit on David's throne."
- **Gerhard von Rad** (1944) called it the beginning of true history-writing. He saw a secular, psychologically realistic account by an eyewitness at Solomon's court, and this view dominated mid-20th-century scholarship.
- Later scholars challenged it. **R. N. Whybray** (1968) read it as a political novel with wisdom-school features. **David Gunn** (1978) emphasized traditional storytelling patterns. **John Van Seters** (1983, 2009) dated it after the exile as an anti-monarchic addition to the Deuteronomistic History. Others, such as P. Kyle McCarter, Baruch Halpern, and Steven McKenzie, still date its core to the early monarchy but treat it as partisan.
- The current consensus is that the narrative is a sophisticated literary work. Its vividness is not proof of an eyewitness author, and its date is contested.

## Points of Agreement

- Usurping dynasties in the ancient Near East commonly either eliminated or closely controlled the heirs of their predecessors. Keeping Meribbaal "at the king's table" works as both generosity and surveillance. Compare Jehoiachin eating at the Babylonian king's table (2 Kgs 25:29), which is confirmed by Babylonian ration tablets listing "Yaukin king of Judah."
- The link to Machir of Lo-debar, who later supports David during Absalom's revolt (17:27), shows a coherent network of Transjordanian loyalties.

## Points of Conflict

- **Motive:** The text presents David's act as covenant loyalty to Jonathan (1 Sam 20). Many historians read it as political management of a Saulide claimant. This is a difference of interpretation, not of fact.
- **Placement:** Chapter 21, the execution of seven Saulides, may originally have preceded chapter 9. David's question "Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul?" (9:1) makes most sense *after* that purge, and 21:7 explicitly mentions sparing Mephibosheth. Many scholars think the editor displaced the episode.

## Assessment

Chapter 9 is not independently verifiable. It is plausible as political history and skillful as literature. Its value lies in showing how the Court History treats David: with sympathy, but alert to the politics behind his kindness.
