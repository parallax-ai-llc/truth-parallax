## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not verifiable.** The chapter is a literary scene of persuasion and reconciliation with no historical claims that can be tested. Its picture of the king as supreme judge in cases of blood vengeance accurately reflects ancient Near Eastern royal ideology.

## What the Text Claims

- **Joab** brings a "wise woman" from **Tekoa** who presents a fictitious case: one of her sons killed the other, and the clan demands the survivor's death. David grants protection (14:1–11).
- She then applies the case to Absalom's banishment, and David recognizes Joab's hand (14:12–20).
- Absalom returns to Jerusalem but is not admitted to the king's presence for two years. He sets Joab's barley field on fire to force a meeting, and David kisses him (14:21–33).
- Absalom's beauty and hair are described: his yearly cut hair weighs **200 shekels** "by the king's weight" (14:25–26).

## External Evidence

- **Tekoa** (Khirbet Tequ', south of Bethlehem) is a known Judahite town, later the home of Amos (Amos 1:1). Iron Age remains there are limited.
- **Royal justice:** Kings as final judges who could override clan blood-vengeance are well attested. Hammurabi's prologue and epilogue present the king as protector of the weak, and Ugaritic texts depict kings judging the cases of widows and orphans (e.g., Danel in the *Aqhat* epic). The petition of a widow to the king is a recognized ancient genre.
- **"The king's weight"** (14:26) refers to a royal standard of weights. Inscribed Judahite shekel weights are attested from the 8th–7th centuries BCE. Whether there was a royal standard in the 10th century is unknown, so the phrase may reflect the writer's own time.

## Points of Agreement

- The legal problem is coherent: a clan's right of blood redemption against a family's survival. It matches biblical law about the avenger of blood (Num 35; Deut 19) and general Near Eastern practice.
- "Wise women" as clan mediators reappear in 20:16–22 (Abel-beth-maacah), suggesting a recognized social role.

## Points of Conflict

- **Absalom's hair:** 200 shekels is about 2.3 kg. This is hyperbole, and it prepares the reader for his death caught by the head in 18:9.
- **Absalom's children:** 14:27 gives him three sons, while 18:18 says he had no son. Commentators suggest the sons died young or that the notes come from different traditions.
- **"Weight of the king"** may be anachronistic for the 10th century (see above).

## Assessment

Chapter 14 shows the Court History at its most literary. It contains a fictional case within the story, a carefully staged dialogue, and motifs that point forward (hair, the field fire, Joab's maneuvering). It contains nothing an external source could confirm. Its social and legal background is authentic to the ancient Near East, but that background would be equally authentic in the 8th or 7th century, so it does not date the narrative.
