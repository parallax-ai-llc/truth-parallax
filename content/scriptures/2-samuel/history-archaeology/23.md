## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed to high (for the list).** The poem in 23:1–7 is an oracle with no historical claims and an uncertain date. The roster of David's heroes (23:8–39) is widely regarded as one of the oldest and most authentic documents in Samuel. It contains obscure names, local place-names, and foreign warriors. No external source confirms any individual on it, and the heroic anecdotes attached to it are saga.

## What the Text Claims

- **"The last words of David"** (23:1–7) describe the ideal ruler as like morning light, grounded in an "everlasting covenant."
- **The Three:** Josheb-basshebeth, who killed 800 at once; Eleazar son of Dodo; and Shammah son of Agee (23:8–12).
- Three heroes break through the Philistine garrison at Bethlehem to bring David water. He pours it out as an offering (23:13–17).
- Abishai and **Benaiah** (who killed two "ariels" of Moab, a lion in a pit, and an Egyptian giant) (23:18–23).
- **"The Thirty":** a list of names, ending with **Uriah the Hittite**, "thirty-seven in all" (23:24–39).

## External Evidence

- No individual on the list is attested outside the Bible.
- The **place-names** of origin are almost all real highland and Shephelah towns: Bethlehem, Tekoa, Anathoth, Netophah, Gibeah, Pirathon, Gilo, Maacah, Zelah, Beeroth, among others. Many cluster in Judah and Benjamin, consistent with a small southern power base.
- **Foreign warriors** include Uriah the Hittite, Zelek the Ammonite, Igal of Zobah, and Eliahba the Shaalbonite. They fit the pattern of mercenaries around David (Cherethites, Pelethites, Ittai's Gittites).
- **Benjamin Mazar** (1963) analyzed the list as an authentic register of a royal military elite, comparable to elite corps in other ancient Near Eastern courts. Nadav Na'aman and others likewise regard it as early archival material, though possibly with secondary additions.

## Points of Agreement

- The list's dry form and its many otherwise unknown names argue against late invention. A later author creating an ideal roster would be unlikely to include so many obscure individuals and a foreigner whose death the book itself blames on David.
- **Eliam son of Ahithophel** (23:34) and **Eliam** father of Bathsheba (11:3) may be the same person. That would make Bathsheba Ahithophel's granddaughter and might explain Ahithophel's defection to Absalom. This is an inference, but a widely noted one.
- **Asahel** is listed although he died early in the Hebron period (2:23). That suggests the roster dates from that early stage, a point in favor of its antiquity.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Samuel 23 | 1 Chronicles 11 and other notes |
|-------------|---------------------------------|
| Josheb-basshebeth killed **800** (23:8) | Jashobeam killed **300** (1 Chr 11:11) |
| "Josheb-basshebeth" | A corrupted name; Chronicles' "Jashobeam" or LXX "Ishbaal" may be original (another *baal* → *bosheth* alteration) |
| "The Thirty" | 37 names counted (23:39); "Thirty" is an institutional title, not a number |
| Ends with Uriah | Chronicles adds sixteen more names (1 Chr 11:41–47) |

The **last words of David** (23:1–7) use archaic poetic language. Their theme of "everlasting covenant" with the house of David links them to 2 Sam 7 and to dynastic theology, which is generally considered later.

## Assessment

This chapter offers some of the most credible historical data in 2 Samuel. The roster probably preserves a real memory of David's warrior band and its regional and foreign makeup. The heroic anecdotes (killing 800, a lion in a pit, an Egyptian giant) are typical warrior saga. The poem is dynastic theology whose date is uncertain.
