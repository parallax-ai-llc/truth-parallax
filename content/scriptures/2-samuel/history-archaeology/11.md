## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not verifiable.** The story is private and has no external trace. It is told with novelistic intimacy, reporting thoughts, messages, and secret instructions that no one outside the circle could have known. Its historical value is debated. The fact that a court narrative includes such damaging material has persuaded some scholars that it rests on a real scandal.

## What the Text Claims

- In spring, "when kings go out to battle," Joab besieges **Rabbah** of the Ammonites, while David stays in Jerusalem (11:1).
- David sees **Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam, wife of Uriah the Hittite**, sleeps with her, and she becomes pregnant (11:2–5).
- When Uriah refuses to go home, David sends him back with a letter instructing Joab to place him where he will die. Uriah is killed at the wall of Rabbah (11:6–25).
- David marries Bathsheba. "The thing David had done displeased the LORD" (11:26–27).

## External Evidence

- No external mention exists of Bathsheba, Uriah, or a siege of Rabbah in the 10th century.
- **Rabbah** (Rabbath-Ammon) lies beneath the **Amman Citadel**. Excavations there have exposed substantial Iron II remains, mostly 9th–7th centuries. Earlier Iron Age levels are less well known.
- **"Uriah the Hittite":** After the Hittite empire collapsed (c. 1180 BCE), "Hittite" (*Ḫatti*) was used in Assyrian texts for the Neo-Hittite and Luwian states of Syria and, more loosely, for western peoples generally. A foreign soldier in David's service fits the evidence of mercenaries (Cherethites, Pelethites, Gittites). Uriah's own name is Yahwistic ("YHWH is my light"), so he was a foreigner integrated into Israelite culture, or the name was Hebraized.
- Joab's reference to **Abimelech killed by a millstone at Thebez** (11:21; Judg 9:53) shows that the author knew the Judges tradition. That suggests either an older shared tradition or later literary composition.

## Points of Agreement

- Spring campaigning (11:1) matches a common pattern of Near Eastern warfare: Assyrian annals routinely date campaigns by regnal year and season.
- Siege tactics in which defenders shoot from the walls and attackers approach the gate are realistic.
- A king staying home while a general campaigns fits the professional army implied by Joab's role.

## Points of Conflict

- **Point of view:** The narrator reports what David privately intended and what he wrote secretly to Joab. This omniscience marks it as literature, not a court record. Von Rad's view of the Court History as eyewitness history depended on such vividness, and it has been widely abandoned.
- **Chronicles omits it entirely** (1 Chr 20:1–3 jumps from the siege to its conclusion). This shows how a later historian could edit out unflattering material. It also shows that the Samuel version was not universally treated as indispensable history.

## Assessment

Historians are divided. One view (Halpern, McKenzie) holds that a pro-Davidic court would not invent a story so damaging, so it reflects a real scandal around Solomon's mother. Another (Van Seters and others) sees an anti-monarchic writer, perhaps exilic, criticizing royal abuse of power. A third treats it as a moral novella whose truth is not historical. None of these can be tested. What can be said is that the setting is plausible and the story itself is unverifiable.
