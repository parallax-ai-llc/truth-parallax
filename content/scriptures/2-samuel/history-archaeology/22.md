## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry).** This is a royal victory psalm that appears again, with minor variants, as Psalm 18. It makes no specific historical claims. The historical questions are its date and whether it can plausibly go back to David. Scholars disagree. Some see an archaic core of the 10th–9th centuries; others see a monarchic composition later attributed to David.

## What the Text Claims

- The superscription attributes the song to David "on the day when the LORD delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul" (22:1).
- YHWH appears in a **storm theophany**: earth quaking, smoke and fire, riding on a cherub, thundering from heaven, arrows of lightning, the channels of the sea laid bare (22:8–16).
- The king is delivered, trained for war, and made "head of the nations"; foreign peoples submit to him (22:17–51).

## Dating and Authorship

### Arguments for an early date

Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman (1953; *Studies in Ancient Yahwistic Poetry*, 1975) analyzed the song's orthography, grammar, and poetic style. They argued for archaic features and dated the original composition to the 10th–9th centuries. Their arguments include sparse use of prose particles, old verb forms, and parallels to Ugaritic poetry. On that view a Davidic origin is possible, though unprovable.

### Arguments for a later date or editing

- The poem contains Deuteronomistic-sounding passages on the king's blamelessness and keeping of statutes (22:21–25). Many scholars regard these as later additions.
- The attribution to David in the superscription is editorial, as in many Psalm headings. Superscriptions are generally regarded as later than the poems.
- The phrase "his anointed, David and his descendants forever" (22:51) presupposes the dynastic ideology of 2 Sam 7.
- Archaic style can be deliberately imitated, so linguistic dating of poetry is contested (see the debates around Ian Young, Robert Rezetko, and Martin Ehrensvärd).

## The Background the Poem Presupposes

- **Storm-god imagery:** The theophany draws on imagery associated in Ugaritic texts with **Baal**, the storm god who rides the clouds, thunders, and defeats Sea (*Yam*). Its closest parallels are in the Baal Cycle from Ugarit (14th–13th c. BCE). This shows that Israelite poetry inherited Canaanite mythic language. It does not fix the poem's date.
- **Royal ideology:** A king who credits a deity with victory and the submission of foreigners is a standard theme of ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions. The Mesha Stele credits Kemosh, and Assyrian annals credit Ashur.

## Points of Agreement

- The double transmission (2 Sam 22 / Ps 18) with textual variants shows the poem existed independently before being placed in Samuel.
- The general setting, a warrior-king's thanksgiving for victory, fits the monarchic period of Judah.

## Points of Conflict

- The superscription's historical setting (deliverance from Saul) does not match the poem's content, which speaks of foreign nations and international dominion.
- "Head of the nations" (22:44) reflects the same imperial idealization as 2 Sam 8.

## Assessment

The song is genuine ancient Hebrew royal poetry. It may contain some of the oldest material in Samuel, but attribution to David cannot be verified. It is valuable as evidence of early Israelite religious language and its Canaanite inheritance. It cannot serve as evidence for events in David's life.
