## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter strings together escape episodes, at least one of which duplicates an earlier story. The household teraphim are a genuine Iron Age religious detail, but none of the events can be verified.

## What the Text Claims

- Jonathan briefly reconciles Saul and David (19:1–7).
- Saul throws his spear at David a second time (19:9–10).
- Michal helps David escape through a window and places the **teraphim** in his bed, covered with goat's hair (19:11–17).
- David flees to Samuel at **Naioth in Ramah**; Saul's messengers and Saul himself fall into prophetic frenzy, and the proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" is explained again (19:18–24).

## External Evidence

### Teraphim

*Těrāpîm* were household cult images, probably related to ancestors or family gods (cf. Gen 31:19, 34; Judg 17:5; 2 Kgs 23:24). Small domestic figurines are common in Iron Age houses, and the Judean pillar figurines of the 8th–7th centuries show the vitality of household religion. The teraphim in Michal's bed appear to be roughly human-sized, which does not match the small figurines usually found; their size and form remain uncertain.

### Ecstatic prophecy

Group frenzy and prophetic trance are attested at **Mari** and in the **Report of Wenamun**. Saul stripping and lying naked "all that day and all that night" (19:24) fits the picture of uncontrolled possession.

## Points of Agreement

- Household images in a royal family's house are plausible for the period; the narrator shows no disapproval, an early feature.
- Ramah as Samuel's home is consistent throughout the book.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 19 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| Saul's spear attack (19:9–10) | Repeats 18:10–11 |
| "Is Saul also among the prophets?" (19:24) | A different origin story for the same proverb as 10:11–12 |
| Saul "never saw Samuel again until the day of his death" (15:35) | Contradicted by his meeting Samuel here (19:24) |

## Assessment

The chapter shows clear signs of compilation from parallel traditions. Its two most interesting historical details, the teraphim and the prophetic frenzy, illuminate Iron Age religion rather than political events. The repeated theme of David escaping while Saul is helpless before God's spirit continues the book's apologetic argument.
