## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The three Philistine cities named are real, were major urban centres in the 11th century, and lie in a geographically sensible sequence. The miraculous events, the toppled idol and the plague of "tumours," are theological storytelling. The identification of Dagon as Ashdod's chief god has some support but is not confirmed for this period.

## What the Text Claims

- The Philistines take the Ark to **Ashdod** and place it in the temple of **Dagon**. The statue falls before the Ark twice, the second time breaking off its head and hands (5:1–5).
- The people of Ashdod are struck with tumours (*'ŏpālîm*, read in the margin as *ṭeḥōrîm*), and the Ark is moved to **Gath** and then **Ekron**, where the plague follows it (5:6–12).

## External Evidence

### The three cities

- **Ashdod** (Moshe Dothan, 1962–1972): Iron I strata contain abundant Philistine pottery and the "**Ashdoda**," a female figurine fused with a chair or throne, in Aegean style. No temple of Dagon has been found. A temple of Dagon at Ashdod is mentioned in 1 Maccabees 10:83–84 (2nd century BCE).
- **Gath / Tell es-Safi** (Aren Maeir, 1996 onwards): a large Philistine city in Iron I, growing into one of the largest in the region by the 10th–9th centuries.
- **Ekron / Tel Miqne** (Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin, 1981–1996): a fortified Philistine city founded in the 12th century, with Aegean-style hearths and cultic rooms. In the 7th century it became a major olive-oil production centre.

### Dagon

Dagan is a well-attested **Semitic** grain or storm deity at Ebla, Mari, and Ugarit, long before the Philistines arrived. The Philistines, who came from the Aegean world, apparently adopted local gods. The Ekron royal inscription (7th century) names the goddess *Ptgyh*, whose name may be non-Semitic. The medieval explanation of Dagon as a "fish-god" (from Hebrew *dāg*, "fish") is a folk etymology that modern scholars reject.

### The plague

The "tumours" together with the mice in chapter 6 have led some interpreters to suggest bubonic plague. This is a modern inference, not an established diagnosis.

## Points of Agreement

- Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron were three of the five Philistine centres (6:17), all flourishing in the 11th century.
- The route from Ashdod inland to Gath and then north to Ekron is coherent.
- The Philistines did adopt Semitic deities, and Dagon's presence among them is plausible.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 5 | Historical analysis |
|------------|---------------------|
| Dagon's temple at Ashdod in the 11th century | Not found archaeologically; first external reference is 2nd century BCE |
| Statue falls and breaks before the Ark | A polemical miracle story mocking a rival god |
| A city-wide plague that follows the Ark | No external evidence; serves the story's theological point |

## Assessment

The chapter uses real places and a plausible cultural setting to tell a story of divine humiliation: the God of Israel defeats Dagon in his own house. Ancient stories of captured divine images often stressed that the god chose to leave and punished the captors, and this is the same genre. Historians can confirm the Philistine cities but not the events.
