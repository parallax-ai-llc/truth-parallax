## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The episode at Nob introduces the massacre of chapter 22 and has no external confirmation. David's flight to **Achish of Gath** is more interesting historically: Gath was a powerful city at the time, and "Achish" is a genuine Philistine royal name, though attested only in the 7th century BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- David comes to the priest **Ahimelech** at **Nob**, receives the holy bread and Goliath's sword (21:1–9).
- Doeg the Edomite, Saul's chief herdsman, is present (21:7).
- David flees to **Achish king of Gath**, where he feigns madness to escape (21:10–15).

## External Evidence

### Achish and the Ekron inscription

The **Ekron royal dedicatory inscription**, found at Tel Miqne in 1996, names "**Achish** (*'kyš*) son of Padi, ruler of Ekron," who built a temple for the goddess Ptgyh. This ruler appears in Assyrian records of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal as **Ikausu** of Ekron, and his father Padi appears in Sennacherib's annals (701 BCE). The inscription dates to the **early 7th century BCE**.

This shows that Achish was an authentic Philistine royal name. It does not attest the biblical Achish of Gath, who would have lived some 300 years earlier. Two interpretations are possible:

- The David traditions preserved a genuine early name of a type that continued in Philistine royal families.
- Late-monarchic writers gave David's Philistine overlord a name familiar from their own time. Finkelstein has used this as one argument for a 7th-century shaping of the Philistine material.

Some scholars link the name to Greek *Achaios* ("Achaean"), which would suit the Philistines' Aegean origins, but this etymology is uncertain.

### Gath

Tell es-Safi/Gath was a very large Philistine city in the 10th–9th centuries until its destruction around 830 BCE; by the 7th century it was minor. Gath's prominence as a royal seat in the David stories therefore fits an early period better than a late one.

### Nob

Nob was near Jerusalem (Isa 10:32), probably on Mount Scopus or nearby; it has not been securely identified or excavated.

### Psalm 34

The superscription of Psalm 34 refers to this episode but names the king "Abimelech." The superscriptions were added later and the discrepancy shows the loose way the tradition was transmitted.

## Points of Agreement

- Gath as a powerful Philistine kingdom fits the 11th–9th centuries.
- Achish is a genuine Philistine name.
- The holy bread (cf. Lev 24:5–9) and ritual purity of warriors on campaign (21:5) reflect real ideas about holy war.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 21 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| David flees to Achish and must feign madness | In ch. 27 he becomes Achish's trusted vassal; two different traditions of David at Gath |
| Goliath's sword kept at Nob | Depends on the Goliath tradition, which 2 Sam 21:19 credits to Elhanan |
| Achish called "king" | The Ekron inscription calls its Achish *śr* ("ruler"); the Bible usually uses *seren* for Philistine lords |

## Assessment

The chapter's links with real Philistine geography and naming conventions give it a historical feel, while the episodes themselves are literary. The double tradition of David's relationship with Achish, a hostile court in chapter 21 versus loyal vassalage in chapter 27, suggests that an embarrassing memory, David's service under a Philistine king, has been softened here.
