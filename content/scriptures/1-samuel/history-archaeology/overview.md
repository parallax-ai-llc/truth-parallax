## Overview

This page does not read 1 Samuel as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the events and setting 1 Samuel describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

1 Samuel is set in the late 11th century BCE, the transition from the tribal "judges" to the first Israelite kings, Saul and David. Mainstream critical scholarship holds that the book in its present form is part of the **Deuteronomistic History** (Deuteronomy through 2 Kings), edited in the late monarchic period (7th century BCE, under Josiah) and revised during the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE). The editors worked with older written sources. The most important are an **Ark Narrative** (roughly 1 Sam 4:1b–7:1, with 2 Sam 6) and a **History of David's Rise** (roughly 1 Sam 16 – 2 Sam 5), which many scholars date to the 10th–8th centuries BCE.

The result is a book that contains real Iron Age memories embedded in later literary and theological shaping. It is far more historically grounded than Daniel or the patriarchal stories, but it is not a court chronicle. No person named in 1 Samuel is attested outside the Bible by a contemporary source.

## Composition and Sources

### The Deuteronomistic framework

Martin Noth (1943) argued that a single exilic author compiled Deuteronomy–Kings, marking key moments with programmatic speeches. In 1 Samuel the clearest examples are the anti-monarchic warning of chapter 8 and Samuel's farewell speech in chapter 12. Frank Moore Cross later proposed a first edition under Josiah and an exilic revision. Either way, the theological framework (kingship as both a gift and a rejection of God, obedience rewarded and disobedience punished) belongs to the editors, not to the events.

### Older sources

- **Ark Narrative** (Leonhard Rost, 1926): the loss of the Ark to the Philistines and its return (chapters 4–6). P. D. Miller and J. J. M. Roberts argued that it was composed before David's capture of Jerusalem. More recently Israel Finkelstein and Thomas Römer have linked it to the northern kingdom in the 8th century, partly on the basis of their 2017–2019 excavations at Kiriath-jearim.
- **History of David's Rise:** a long narrative explaining how David, a Judahite outsider, legitimately replaced Saul's house. P. Kyle McCarter Jr. and others read it as a royal **apology**, written to answer accusations that David was a usurper, a Philistine collaborator, and complicit in the deaths of Saul, Jonathan, Abner, and Ish-bosheth. Its defensive tone is itself a sign that it was written close enough to David's time for those accusations to matter.
- **Saul traditions** (chapters 9–11, 13–14) appear to be older stories that were reworked to present Saul as rejected.

### Doublets and competing accounts

The editors kept parallel versions side by side rather than harmonising them:

- **Three accounts of Saul becoming king:** private anointing (9:1–10:16), selection by lot at Mizpah (10:17–27), and acclamation after victory at Jabesh-gilead (11:1–15).
- **Two rejections of Saul** (13:7–15 and 15:10–35).
- **Two introductions of David:** as a skilled musician and armour-bearer already known to Saul (16:14–23), and as an unknown shepherd boy whom Saul does not recognise (17:55–58).
- **Two offers of Saul's daughters** (Merab, 18:17–19; Michal, 18:20–27).
- **Two "Is Saul also among the prophets?" etiologies** (10:10–12 and 19:23–24).
- **Two betrayals by the Ziphites and two sparings of Saul** (chapters 23–24 and 26).
- **Two accounts of Saul's death:** suicide (31:4) and killing by an Amalekite (2 Sam 1:6–10).

The Greek Septuagint (Codex Vaticanus) has a much shorter text of chapters 17–18, lacking roughly 45 per cent of the Hebrew, including David's arrival from Bethlehem and Saul's failure to recognise him. This is strong evidence that two Goliath stories were combined.

## The Philistines: Where the Book Is Strongest

Archaeology has given the Philistines a vivid material profile, and here 1 Samuel matches the record well:

- **Arrival:** The Medinet Habu reliefs of Ramesses III (c. 1177 BCE) depict the *Peleset* among the "Sea Peoples." Their settlement on the southern coastal plain in the 12th century is marked by locally made Aegean-style pottery (Mycenaean IIIC), followed in the 11th century by distinctive **Philistine bichrome ware**, as well as hearths, loom weights, and a high proportion of pig bones.
- **The Pentapolis:** Four of the five cities named in 6:17 have been excavated: **Ashdod** (Moshe Dothan, 1962–1972), **Ekron/Tel Miqne** (Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin, 1981–1996), **Gath/Tell es-Safi** (Aren Maeir, 1996 onwards), and **Ashkelon** (Lawrence Stager and Daniel Master, 1985–2016). All were substantial urban centres in Iron I. Gaza remains largely unexcavated.
- **Gath** was one of the largest cities in the Levant in the 10th–9th centuries and was destroyed around 830 BCE, probably by Hazael of Damascus (cf. 2 Kgs 12:17). Its prominence in 1 Samuel fits the period before that destruction, not the 7th century, when Gath was a minor site. This is one of the strongest arguments that the David stories preserve **early** memories.
- **The Ekron inscription** (found 1996) records a temple built by "Achish (*'kyš*) son of Padi, ruler of Ekron." It dates to the **7th century BCE**, and this Achish (Ikausu) also appears in Assyrian records. It shows that "Achish," the name of David's Philistine overlord at Gath (21:10; 27:2), was a genuine Philistine royal name. It does not attest the biblical Achish, and some scholars think the biblical writers borrowed a name current in their own time.

## Saul

Saul is portrayed as a Benjaminite war-leader whose power base was the Benjamin plateau north of Jerusalem (Gibeah, Geba, Michmash, Gibeon). That geography is precise and realistic. W. F. Albright's identification of a "fortress of Saul" at Tell el-Ful (Gibeah) has not held up; later reassessment found little Iron I architecture there. No inscription names Saul. Finkelstein has argued for a real "Saulide polity" in the Benjamin and Gibeon area in the late 11th–10th centuries, possibly the target of Pharaoh Sheshonq I's campaign (c. 925 BCE), whose list includes several towns in that region. Most historians accept Saul as a historical chieftain whose memory was later subordinated to David's.

## David as a Historical Person

1 Samuel introduces David, so it is important to state plainly what is and is not known about him:

- **Tel Dan Stele** (fragments found 1993–1994): an Aramaic victory inscription, probably of Hazael, from the mid-to-late **9th century BCE**. It mentions a king of Israel and a king of the "**House of David**" (*bytdwd*). This is the earliest extra-biblical reference to David's dynasty, about 150 years after his presumed lifetime. Early suggestions that *bytdwd* was a place name or a deity epithet have been largely abandoned.
- **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE): André Lemaire (1994) proposed reading line 31 as "House of [D]avid." The reading is **debated**, since the stone is damaged at that point. In 2019 Finkelstein, Na'aman, and Römer proposed reading a single-name king instead (possibly "Balak").
- **Sheshonq I's list** at Karnak (c. 925 BCE): K. A. Kitchen proposed that the phrase read as *ḥdbt dwt* means "heights of David." This is a **minority view**; the reading and interpretation are contested.
- **Jerusalem:** The **Stepped Stone Structure** and the adjoining **Large Stone Structure** on the City of David ridge were claimed by Eilat Mazar (2005 onwards) as David's palace. Critics such as Finkelstein, Herzog, Singer-Avitz, and Ussishkin argued that the remains belong to different periods. Recent radiocarbon work indicates building activity in Jerusalem in the 12th–10th centuries BCE, but the city's size and status in the 10th century remain disputed.

**Conclusion:** The Tel Dan Stele makes it very likely that David was a historical founder of the Judahite royal house. Whether he ruled a substantial kingdom, as the Bible says, or a modest highland chiefdom centred on a small Jerusalem is the central debate.

## The Chronology Debate and Khirbet Qeiyafa

The traditional chronology, defended in modified form by **Amihai Mazar** (the "modified conventional chronology"), dates the start of the Iron IIA period around 980 BCE. On that basis, monumental gates and palaces at sites such as Megiddo, Hazor, and Gezer can belong to the 10th century, the era of David and Solomon. **Finkelstein's "low chronology"** (1996 onwards) lowers those strata by about 75–100 years to the 9th century and the Omride kings of Israel. That leaves 10th-century Judah as a sparsely settled highland society. **Avraham Faust** and others argue for a real, if modest, early state.

**Khirbet Qeiyafa**, a fortified site overlooking the Elah Valley (the setting of chapter 17), was excavated by **Yosef Garfinkel** and Saar Ganor in 2007–2013. It has a casemate wall, two gates, radiocarbon dates of around 1020–980 BCE, no pig bones, and an ink ostracon in early alphabetic script. Garfinkel identifies it as a **Judahite fortress of David's time** and as evidence for an early Judahite state. Others attribute it to a Saulide or north-Israelite polity (Finkelstein and Fantalkin), to a local Canaanite population (Na'aman), or leave its ethnic identity open. The ostracon's language and content are disputed. The site shows organised building in the Elah Valley at about the right time. It does not show who built it.

## Anachronisms and Signs of Date

| Feature | Assessment |
|---------|------------|
| Philistine iron monopoly (13:19–22) | Not supported archaeologically; iron was rare everywhere in Iron I |
| *Pim* weights (13:21) | A genuine Iron Age term, attested on late-monarchic weights and lost by Hellenistic times. Evidence of pre-exilic tradition, not necessarily 11th-century |
| Camels of the Amalekites (30:17) | Domesticated dromedaries are attested in the southern Levant from about the 10th century; plausible for desert raiders but possibly a later detail |
| Goliath's armour (17:5–7) | Some scholars see features of 7th–6th-century Greek hoplite equipment; others defend an Iron I warrior profile |
| Goliath killed by Elhanan (2 Sam 21:19) | A competing tradition; the feat was probably later transferred to David |
| "Kings of Judah to this day" (27:6) | Written after the divided monarchy |
| "Seer" gloss (9:9) | An editor explaining an obsolete term to later readers |

## Reliability Assessment

1 Samuel is **mixed** as history. Its geography, its picture of a highland society under Philistine pressure, its Philistine Pentapolis with a dominant Gath, and its portrait of Saul's and David's power bases fit the Iron I–IIA world reasonably well. Some details, such as the *pim*, the prominence of Gath, and the apologetic need to defend David, point to traditions older than the Deuteronomistic editors.

At the same time, the book's individual episodes cannot be verified, and many show clear signs of legend (the Ark's victory over Dagon, Goliath), theological reshaping (Samuel's speeches), propaganda (David's innocence in every death that benefits him), and doublets. Numbers are frequently inflated or corrupt. The best historical reading is that 1 Samuel preserves the memory of a real transition from tribal leadership to monarchy, centred on Saul and David, told by later Judahite writers who wanted to explain why David's house, not Saul's, held the throne.
