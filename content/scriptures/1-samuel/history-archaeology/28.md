## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (as report) / valuable for religious history.** A private séance cannot be verified, and the scene is shaped to announce Saul's doom. But the practices it describes, consulting the dead through a ghost-pit or spirit (*'ôb*), are well-attested features of ancient Near Eastern religion.

## What the Text Claims

- The Philistines camp at **Shunem**; Saul camps at **Gilboa** (28:4).
- God does not answer Saul by dreams, Urim, or prophets (28:6).
- Saul, who had expelled mediums, consults a woman at **Endor** who has an *'ôb*. She sees "a god (*'ĕlōhîm*) coming up out of the earth," and Samuel speaks (28:7–14).
- Samuel announces that Saul and his sons will die the next day (28:15–19).

## External Evidence

### Necromancy in the ancient Near East

- Harry Hoffner (1967) compared Hebrew *'ôb* with the Hittite *a-a-bi*, a ritual pit through which the dead were summoned, and with similar terms in Akkadian and Hurrian.
- Mesopotamian texts describe necromancers (*ša eṭemmi*) and rituals for calling up ghosts.
- Ugaritic texts refer to the deified dead (*rpum*) and ancestor rituals.
- Biblical prohibitions (Lev 19:31; Deut 18:10–11; 2 Kgs 21:6; 23:24) show that consulting the dead was a real practice in Israel and Judah that later reformers opposed.

Calling the dead Samuel *'ĕlōhîm* ("a divine being") reflects early Israelite ideas about the dead.

### Geography

Shunem (Solem) lies on the southern slope of the Hill of Moreh in the Jezreel Valley; Gilboa faces it to the south; Endor lies north of Moreh. Saul's night journey to Endor would have meant passing near the Philistine camp. The geography is accurate.

### The Philistines in the Jezreel Valley

A Philistine army in the Jezreel Valley, far north of their heartland, is questioned by some historians. Philistine pottery is limited in the north, and some scholars suspect later writers projected Philistine power too broadly. Others note that an army could campaign far from its settlement zone.

## Points of Agreement

- Accurate local geography.
- Authentic religious practices, opposed by later legislation.
- Divination by dreams, lots, and prophets (28:6) matches ancient Near Eastern methods.

## Points of Conflict

- **Editorial expansion.** Samuel's words in 28:17–18 repeat 15:28 and the Amalek theme, and are usually ascribed to Deuteronomistic editors.
- **Samuel's death notice** (28:3) repeats 25:1, showing compilation.

## Assessment

The Endor story is a powerful literary scene with an authentic religious background. It uses real geography and practices to dramatise Saul's abandonment by God. Its value for historians lies chiefly in what it reveals about Israelite beliefs about the dead, which were far more varied than later orthodox texts suggest.
