## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The geography of the southern Judean hills is accurate and detailed, and Keilah is attested in the Late Bronze Age Amarna letters. The pursuit narrative, with its oracle consultations and near escapes, is literary, and the Ziphite betrayal is told twice (here and in chapter 26).

## What the Text Claims

- David rescues **Keilah** from Philistine raiders but must leave when an oracle (through Abiathar's ephod) warns that its citizens will hand him over (23:1–13).
- He hides in the wilderness of **Ziph**; Jonathan visits him and declares, "You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be second to you" (23:14–18).
- The Ziphites betray him; Saul nearly catches him in the wilderness of **Maon**, but a Philistine raid forces Saul to withdraw (23:19–29).

## External Evidence

### Keilah in the Amarna letters

Keilah appears as **Qiltu** in the Amarna correspondence (14th century BCE). Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem and Shuwardata (probably of Gath or Hebron) accuse each other of seizing it (EA 279, 280, 289, 290). Keilah was thus a contested town between highland and lowland powers centuries before David, which makes the situation in 23:1–13 realistic. It is identified with Khirbet Qila in the Shephelah.

### Ziph and Maon

Tel Ziph and Khirbet Ma'in, south-east of Hebron, preserve the ancient names. **Ziph** later appears on royal Judahite *lmlk* jar stamps (late 8th century BCE) as one of four administrative centres. The Judean wilderness to the east, with its caves and ravines, suits a fugitive band.

### The ephod oracle

Consulting God through a priest with an **ephod** to receive yes/no answers (23:9–12) is an archaic practice later abandoned. Its approving depiction is an early feature.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistine raids on Shephelah towns at harvest time are plausible.
- Accurate geography of the Hebron hills and Judean wilderness.
- Keilah's history as a border town contested by rival powers.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 23 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| Ziphites betray David at the hill of Hachilah (23:19) | Repeated almost word for word in 26:1 |
| Jonathan's declaration (23:17) | Apologetic: Saul's heir and even Saul (24:20) recognise David's kingship |
| Saul saved by a timely Philistine raid | A narrative device; unverifiable |

## Assessment

The chapter is grounded in the real landscape and politics of southern Judah, and Keilah's long record as a contested town lends credibility to the setting. As with most of the David stories, the specific events are told to show divine guidance and the inevitability of David's kingship. The Ziphite betrayal is one of several doublets in chapters 23–26.
