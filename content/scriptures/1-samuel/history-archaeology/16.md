## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (as report).** Chapter 16 opens the **History of David's Rise**, which most scholars read as a royal apology defending David's claim to the throne. The secret anointing is a legitimation story, and David's introduction as Saul's musician conflicts with his introduction in chapter 17.

## What the Text Claims

- Samuel goes to **Bethlehem** and anoints Jesse's youngest son, David, a shepherd (16:1–13).
- An evil spirit from God troubles Saul; David, described as a skilled lyre player, "a man of valour, a man of war," is brought to court and becomes Saul's armour-bearer (16:14–23).

## External Evidence

### David as a historical figure

The **Tel Dan Stele** (9th century BCE) refers to the "House of David" (*bytdwd*), making it very likely that David was the founder of Judah's ruling dynasty. No source outside the Bible mentions his early career, his family, or his anointing. (See the book overview for a full discussion.)

### Bethlehem

Bethlehem is mentioned in the **Amarna letters** (14th century BCE) as *Bīt-Laḫmi*, if the usual reading is accepted (EA 290). In 2012 a bulla reading "Bethlehem" was found in Jerusalem, dated to the 8th–7th century BCE. Iron I remains at Bethlehem itself are sparse, as the town lies beneath the modern city.

### The lyre

The *kinnôr* (lyre) is well attested in the Levant. It appears in Ugaritic texts and in Egyptian depictions of Semitic musicians, such as the Beni Hasan tomb painting (c. 1900 BCE). Music used to soothe a troubled mind is a common ancient motif.

### The royal apology

P. Kyle McCarter Jr. compared the History of David's Rise with the **Apology of Hattusili III** (13th century BCE), in which a Hittite king who seized the throne explains that the gods chose him and that he acted only under provocation. Like that text, the David story insists that David was divinely chosen while Saul still reigned and that he never sought Saul's harm.

## Points of Agreement

- Bethlehem is a real Judahite town.
- Music at court is plausible.
- The youngest-son motif matches a common pattern in ancient Near Eastern legitimation stories.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 16 | Internal and historical analysis |
|-------------|----------------------------------|
| David is already a seasoned warrior and Saul's armour-bearer (16:18, 21) | In 17:33 he is a youth unfit for battle, and in 17:55–58 Saul does not know who he is |
| Secret anointing by Samuel | No witness outside the family; functions to legitimate David before Saul's death |
| "The spirit of the LORD departed from Saul" (16:14) | Theological interpretation of Saul's mental state |

## Assessment

The chapter is a theological and political preface. By having God choose David while Saul still reigned, it answers the charge that David usurped the throne. That such a defence was thought necessary suggests the accusation was real and early, which supports the view that David was a historical figure whose rise was contested. The specific events of the chapter, however, cannot be regarded as historical reports.
