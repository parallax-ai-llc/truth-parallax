## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** David's marriage into Saul's family is plausible and politically significant, since it gave him a claim on Saul's house. The chapter, however, contains doublets (two daughters offered, two spear attacks), and the Septuagint again lacks large parts of it.

## What the Text Claims

- Jonathan makes a covenant with David (18:1–4).
- Women sing, "Saul has killed his thousands, and David his ten thousands," and Saul becomes jealous (18:6–9).
- Saul twice throws his spear at David (18:10–11).
- Saul offers his elder daughter **Merab**, then gives her to another man (18:17–19). He offers **Michal** for a bride price of 100 Philistine foreskins; David brings 200 (18:20–27).

## External Evidence

### The Septuagint's shorter text

Codex Vaticanus lacks 18:1–5, 18:10–11, 18:17–19, and 18:29b–30. In the shorter version there is no covenant with Jonathan at this point, no spear attack, and no offer of Merab. This supports the view that the Hebrew text combines two versions of the David–Saul story (see chapter 17).

### Counting the enemy dead

Taking body parts as proof of kills is attested in Egyptian sources. At **Medinet Habu**, reliefs of Ramesses III (12th century BCE) show scribes counting severed hands and phalli of slain Libyans, and a **Merneptah** inscription at Karnak (late 13th century BCE) records phalli taken from uncircumcised Libyans. The Philistines, like the Libyans, did not practise circumcision, so the demand is culturally coherent.

### The victory song

The parallelism "thousands … ten thousands" is a standard poetic pair also found in Ugaritic poetry. It is a formula, not a count.

## Points of Agreement

- David's marriage to Michal is attested in several independent passages (19:11–17; 25:44; 2 Sam 3:13–16; 6:20–23), suggesting a real tradition.
- Bride-price customs and the political use of royal marriages fit ancient Near Eastern practice.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 18 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| Two daughters offered (Merab and Michal) | Doublet; the Merab episode is absent from the Septuagint |
| Saul's spear attacks (18:10–11 and 19:9–10) | Doublet; the first is absent from the Septuagint |
| 200 foreskins | Folk-heroic exaggeration |
| "All Israel and Judah loved David" (18:16) | Presupposes the later two-kingdom division |

## Assessment

The chapter's theme, that the people and Saul's own children turned to David, serves the apologetic goal of the History of David's Rise: David did not seize power; it came to him. Behind it lies a historically plausible fact. A successful Judahite warrior joined Saul's household and married his daughter, giving him a later claim to rule Saul's people. Halpern and others suggest that this marriage was a key element of David's political strategy.
