## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (as report).** The chapter is a private conversation between David and Jonathan that no outside witness could record. It plays a central role in the apology for David: Saul's own heir recognises David's right to the throne. The new-moon feast is an authentic custom.

## What the Text Claims

- David and Jonathan arrange a test of Saul's intentions at the **new moon** feast (20:1–23).
- Saul rages at Jonathan: "As long as the son of Jesse lives on the earth, neither you nor your kingdom will be established" (20:30–31).
- Jonathan signals with arrows, and the friends part after renewing their covenant, which includes Jonathan's descendants (20:35–42).

## External Evidence

### The new moon festival

The new moon (*ḥōdeš*) was an important festival in monarchic Israel, often paired with the Sabbath (Amos 8:5; Hos 2:11; Isa 1:13; 2 Kgs 4:23). A royal meal at the new moon with a fixed seating order fits the setting of a ruler's household. Saul's annual clan sacrifice in Bethlehem (20:6, 29) also reflects the importance of clan-based religion.

### Covenant language

The phrases *ḥesed* ("loyalty") and "covenant of the LORD" between Jonathan and David (20:8, 14–17) use the vocabulary of ancient Near Eastern treaties between rulers and vassals. Jonathan asks David to protect his descendants, setting up the later story of Mephibosheth (2 Sam 9).

## Points of Agreement

- Festival customs and clan sacrifice are realistic for Iron Age Israel.
- The political logic, that Saul saw David as a threat to his dynasty, is credible.

## Points of Conflict

- **Who could know?** The conversation is private, so its content is the narrator's construction.
- **The apologetic purpose.** Jonathan, the legitimate heir, effectively hands David the succession (20:13–16; cf. 23:17). McCarter, McKenzie, and Halpern see this as answering the charge that David displaced Saul's house by force. Later, all of Saul's male heirs except the disabled Mephibosheth die violently (2 Sam 2–4; 21), which explains why such a defence was needed.

## Assessment

Chapter 20 is literature of great emotional power whose historical function is to legitimate David. It may preserve the memory that David had a close bond with Saul's son, but the details of covenant and succession are presented to answer questions that arose after David took power.
