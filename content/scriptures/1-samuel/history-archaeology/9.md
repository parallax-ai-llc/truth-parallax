## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The story is a folktale ("the young man who went looking for donkeys and found a kingdom"), but it rests on a credible social world: a well-off Benjaminite family, a local seer paid for oracles, and a sacrificial meal at a village high place. An editorial gloss (9:9) shows that the story was written down long after the period it describes.

## What the Text Claims

- Saul son of Kish, a Benjaminite of wealth, is sent to find his father's lost donkeys (9:1–4).
- In the land of Zuph his servant suggests consulting a "man of God," a seer, offering a quarter shekel of silver (9:5–10).
- Samuel, forewarned by God, hosts Saul at the high place and tells him he is to be leader (*nāgîd*) to save Israel from the Philistines (9:15–24).

## External Evidence

### The editorial gloss in 9:9

"Formerly in Israel… the one who is now called a prophet (*nābî'*) was formerly called a seer (*rō'eh*)." This explanation was added for readers who no longer knew the old term. It is clear internal evidence of a later editor working on an older story. The gloss also sits awkwardly, since the term "seer" is used only in the following verses, suggesting that it was inserted in the wrong place.

### Payment for oracles

Paying a diviner or seer for consultation is well attested in the ancient Near East and elsewhere in the Bible (1 Kgs 14:3; 2 Kgs 8:8). A quarter shekel of silver (9:8) is a realistic small payment. Silver was used by weight; coinage was not introduced until the 6th century BCE.

### High places

Open-air or small shrines (*bāmôt*) for communal sacrifice and feasting were common in Iron Age Israel. Archaeological examples include the Iron I "Bull Site" in northern Samaria, published by Amihai Mazar. Later Deuteronomistic writers condemned such shrines, but this story presents a high place approvingly, a sign that it predates that ideology.

## Points of Agreement

- Saul's tribe (Benjamin) and region match the rest of the Saul tradition.
- The approving depiction of a local high place fits pre-Deuteronomic religious practice.
- The idea of a local seer anointing a leader reflects a real link between prophetic figures and early leadership seen elsewhere in the ancient Near East.

## Points of Conflict

- **Samuel's role.** Samuel is a minor local seer whom Saul does not know, which sits uneasily with chapters 3–7, where he is the famous national judge. Many scholars think the original story concerned an anonymous seer and was later attached to Samuel.
- **Saul's "nāgîd" commission** to save Israel from the Philistines (9:16) contradicts 7:13, where the Philistines are already subdued.

## Assessment

Chapter 9 is one of the oldest-feeling stories in the book, with an unpretentious folk tone and pre-Deuteronomic religious assumptions. It does not report a verifiable event, but it gives a credible picture of village life in Iron Age Benjamin. Its integration into the larger Samuel story, and the 9:9 gloss, show the editorial work of much later writers.
