## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Saul's death in battle against the Philistines on Mount Gilboa is accepted by most historians as a historical core. The details are contradicted by 2 Samuel 1, and archaeology raises questions about Philistine control of Beth-shan.

## What the Text Claims

- The Philistines defeat Israel on **Mount Gilboa**; Saul's sons Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua are killed (31:1–2).
- Wounded by archers, Saul asks his armour-bearer to kill him; when he refuses, Saul falls on his own sword (31:3–6).
- The Philistines put his armour in the temple of **Ashtaroth** and fasten his body to the wall of **Beth-shan** (31:8–10).
- The men of **Jabesh-gilead** retrieve the bodies, burn them, and bury the bones under a tamarisk (31:11–13).

## External Evidence

### Beth-shan

Beth-shan (Tel Beth-Shean) was excavated by the University of Pennsylvania (1921–1933) and by Amihai Mazar (1989–1996). It was an Egyptian garrison town until the mid-12th century BCE. In Iron I it was a Canaanite town. The Penn excavators (Alan Rowe) identified two temples of their "Level V" with the "temple of Ashtaroth" (31:10) and the "house of Dagon" (1 Chr 10:10). Modern scholars regard that identification as uncertain.

Very little Philistine pottery has been found at Beth-shan. This makes Philistine **occupation** of the town unlikely, though cooperation with or temporary control over a Canaanite city cannot be excluded.

### Gilboa

Mount Gilboa overlooks the eastern Jezreel Valley and the route to Beth-shan. A battle there fits the geography of a struggle for control of the valley. Finkelstein has argued that the Gilboa story reflects Saul's attempt to expand his highland polity northward.

### Two accounts of Saul's death

| 1 Samuel 31 | 2 Samuel 1 |
|-------------|-----------|
| Saul falls on his own sword after his armour-bearer refuses (31:4) | An Amalekite says he killed Saul at Saul's request, leaning on his spear (2 Sam 1:6–10) |
| Saul is pressed by archers | Saul is pursued by chariots and horsemen |
| No witness reports to David | The Amalekite brings Saul's crown and armlet to David |

Some harmonise by assuming the Amalekite lied to gain a reward. Critical scholars generally see two traditions. In either version David is absent and innocent, and in 2 Samuel 1 he executes the man who claims to have killed Saul.

1 Chronicles 10 retells this chapter and adds that "all his house died together" (10:6), ignoring Ish-bosheth, who ruled after Saul (2 Sam 2:8–10).

### Burning the bodies

Cremation was not a normal Israelite practice. The burning in 31:12 is unusual; 1 Chronicles 10:12 omits it. It may reflect the need to deal with mutilated, decomposing remains.

## Points of Agreement

- Saul's death fighting the Philistines is the consistent core of all biblical traditions.
- The Gilboa–Jezreel–Beth-shan geography is accurate.
- The bond between Saul's house and Jabesh-gilead is consistent with chapter 11.

## Points of Conflict

- **Two accounts of Saul's death** (31 and 2 Sam 1).
- **Philistine control of Beth-shan** is not supported by the archaeological record.
- **The temple identification** at Beth-shan is uncertain.

## Assessment

The end of 1 Samuel probably preserves the historical memory that Saul, the first king of a highland Israelite polity, died fighting the Philistines in the north, with several of his sons. The story is told to emphasise his tragic end and, alongside chapters 29–30, to separate David from it. That a second account of Saul's death exists in 2 Samuel 1 shows that the circumstances were remembered differently and that the question of who benefited from Saul's death mattered to the writers.
