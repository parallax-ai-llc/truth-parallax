## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The setting, a pilgrimage shrine at Shiloh in the central highlands, fits what archaeology shows about Iron I Shiloh. The birth story itself is a conventional "barren mother" tale that cannot be tested, and there are signs it was first told about Saul rather than Samuel.

## What the Text Claims

- Elkanah of Ramathaim-zophim in the hill country of Ephraim goes up yearly to sacrifice at **Shiloh**, where Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas are priests (1:1–3).
- Childless Hannah prays at the "temple" (*hêkal*) of the LORD, sitting by its doorpost, and vows her son to God (1:9–11).
- She bears Samuel and, after weaning him, brings him to Shiloh with a sacrifice (1:20–28).

## External Evidence

### Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)

- **Danish excavations** (Hans Kjaer, 1926–1932) and **Israel Finkelstein's excavations** (1981–1984) found an Iron I settlement of pillared buildings with many storage jars (collared-rim jars). It was destroyed by fire, probably in the **mid-11th century BCE**.
- Shiloh was a prominent regional centre in Iron I, with older Middle and Late Bronze Age cultic remains (Late Bronze Age votive deposits were found). This supports its role as a traditional sanctuary.
- **No temple building** from Iron I has been identified. Its presumed location on the summit was eroded or built over. Since 2017, a team led by Scott Stripling (Associates for Biblical Research, a conservative organisation) has excavated at the site; claims about cultic finds from that project are not yet fully published and should be treated cautiously.

### The text of the chapter

The Qumran scroll **4QSam<sup>a</sup>** and the Septuagint preserve a fuller text in places. In 1:22, 4QSam<sup>a</sup> adds that Samuel will be "a Nazirite forever," making explicit what the Hebrew only implies in 1:11.

## Points of Agreement

- A sanctuary at Shiloh in the pre-monarchic highlands matches the archaeology and later memory (Jer 7:12–14; Ps 78:60).
- The annual family pilgrimage and sacrificial meal fit what is known of Iron Age local religion, which was centred on regional shrines rather than a single national temple.
- The hill country of Ephraim was densely settled with small villages in Iron I, the world Elkanah belongs to.

## Points of Conflict

- **A "temple" with doorposts** (1:9; 3:3) clashes with the Pentateuch's portable tent (Josh 18:1; 2 Sam 7:6). This is an internal tension, not proof of error, but it shows that different traditions about Shiloh's sanctuary were combined.
- **The name explanation.** Hannah names him Samuel "because I asked him (*šĕ'iltîw*) of the LORD" (1:20), and in 1:28 she says he is "lent" (*šā'ûl*) to the LORD. These wordplays fit the name **Saul** (*šā'ûl*, "asked") far better than Samuel. Many scholars, including McCarter, think the birth story was originally Saul's and was transferred to Samuel when Samuel became the hero of the opening chapters.

## Assessment

The chapter preserves a credible backdrop: a central highland shrine at Shiloh in the 11th century BCE, staffed by a priestly family and visited by local households. The barren-woman motif (compare Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother) is a literary pattern that marks the child as divinely chosen, and it cannot be treated as historical reporting. Samuel himself is not attested outside the Bible, and the possible reassignment of a Saul birth story shows how the tradition was reworked to give the prophet priority over the first king.
