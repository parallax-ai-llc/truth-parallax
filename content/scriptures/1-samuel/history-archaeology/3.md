## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (call narrative).** The chapter is a prophetic call story with no external claims to test. Its framing reflects the later literary aim of establishing Samuel as Israel's prophet.

## What the Text Claims

- "The word of the LORD was rare in those days" (3:1).
- The boy Samuel sleeps in the "temple of the LORD, where the ark of God was," before "the lamp of God had gone out" (3:3).
- God calls Samuel three times and announces judgment on Eli's house (3:4–14).
- "All Israel from Dan to Beersheba" recognised Samuel as a prophet (3:20).

## External Evidence

### Prophecy in the ancient Near East

There is no direct external evidence for this episode. Comparative texts show that receiving divine messages through dreams or night visions at sanctuaries (incubation) was widespread. Letters from **Mari** (18th century BCE) report prophetic dreams sent to the king, and the Egyptian **Report of Wenamun** (c. 11th century BCE) describes an ecstatic prophet at Byblos. Samuel's experience fits this broad pattern.

### "From Dan to Beersheba"

This formula describes the boundaries of the later monarchy. Excavations at **Tel Dan** (Avraham Biran, 1966–1999) show Iron I settlement there, and the name Dan appears in the tribal stories (Judg 18). As a phrase for "all Israel," however, it presupposes a unified territory that did not exist in Samuel's day.

## Points of Agreement

- Sleeping at a sanctuary near the sacred object, and receiving a message at night, matches known ancient practice.
- The repetition of the oracle against Eli continues chapter 2.

## Points of Conflict

- **National recognition** of Samuel "from Dan to Beersheba" is an anachronistic framing; Samuel's activity elsewhere in the book is limited to a small circuit in Benjamin and Ephraim (7:16–17).
- **The Ark in a "temple"** with a lamp (Exod 27:20–21) reflects priestly ideals of a later sanctuary.

## Assessment

Chapter 3 is a theological transition: the word of God passes from a failing priestly house to a new prophet. Historians can say only that the story fits ancient ideas about how gods spoke, and that its language places Samuel within a later "all Israel" perspective. It should not be read as a datable event.
