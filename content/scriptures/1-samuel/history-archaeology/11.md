## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed to high (core event).** Many historians regard Saul's rescue of Jabesh-gilead as the most plausible account of how he actually came to power: a charismatic war leader whose victory earned him acclamation. The Qumran text fills in a credible political background. The army figures are greatly inflated.

## What the Text Claims

- **Nahash the Ammonite** besieges Jabesh-gilead and demands to gouge out every man's right eye (11:1–2).
- Saul, ploughing in the field, cuts up oxen and sends pieces throughout Israel to summon the tribes (11:5–7).
- He musters 330,000 men, defeats the Ammonites, and is made king at Gilgal (11:8–15).

## External Evidence

### The 4QSam<sup>a</sup> paragraph

The Qumran scroll **4QSam<sup>a</sup>** has a passage, missing from the Masoretic text, before 11:1. It says Nahash had already been oppressing the Gadites and Reubenites east of the Jordan, gouging out their right eyes, and that 7,000 men escaped to Jabesh-gilead. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.68–71) paraphrases the same information. Frank Moore Cross argued that the paragraph is original and was lost by scribal error. Others (e.g. Alexander Rofé) see it as an early expansion. If original, it explains why Nahash targeted Jabesh.

### Ammon

Ammon was an Iron Age kingdom centred on Rabbah (modern Amman). Its earliest substantial inscription, the **Amman Citadel Inscription**, dates to the 9th century BCE. There is no extra-biblical evidence for Nahash, but the same name appears for an Ammonite king in 2 Samuel 10:2, whose son Hanun succeeded him, and the name is not otherwise suspicious.

### Jabesh-gilead and Benjamin

The link between Saul and Jabesh recurs (11; 31:11–13; 2 Sam 2:4–7; 21:12). Judges 21 even makes Jabesh the source of Benjamin's wives. This cluster of traditions suggests a real historical connection between Saul's house and Jabesh across the Jordan.

### Mustering by dismembered animal

Sending pieces of an animal as a summons with a curse ("so shall it be done to his oxen") has a parallel in a **Mari** letter (18th century BCE), in which an official proposes sending a criminal's head around villages to force them to muster. The closest biblical parallel is Judges 19:29.

## Points of Agreement

- Ammonite aggression in Gilead is consistent with later conflicts (2 Sam 10; Amos 1:13).
- The Saul–Jabesh bond is attested independently across several traditions.
- A charismatic war leader gaining kingship through victory matches anthropological models of chiefdom formation.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 11 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| 300,000 Israelites and 30,000 Judahites (11:8) | Impossible for the Iron I population; the separate count for Judah reflects the later two-kingdom division |
| Kingship "renewed" at Gilgal (11:14) | An editorial harmonisation with 10:17–24; originally a separate account of Saul's accession |

## Assessment

Chapter 11 is probably the oldest and most historically credible tradition about the beginning of Saul's rule. It portrays a local military leader responding to an external threat in a way that matches what is known about early leadership in the region. The figures and the Gilgal "renewal" are later features; the core memory of Saul's relief of Jabesh-gilead is plausible.
