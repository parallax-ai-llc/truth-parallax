## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Two separate accounts of Saul's elevation are combined here: a secret anointing and a public selection by lot. Neither can be verified, and the doubling shows that the book draws on competing traditions. Background details, such as Philistine outposts in the highlands and bands of ecstatic prophets, are plausible.

## What the Text Claims

- Samuel anoints Saul privately and gives him three signs (10:1–8).
- Saul meets a band of prophets, prophesies with them, and the proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" arises (10:9–13).
- At **Mizpah**, Saul is chosen by lot, hiding among the baggage, and is acclaimed king (10:17–24).
- Some "worthless men" despise him (10:27).

## External Evidence

### Ecstatic prophecy

Groups of ecstatics using music are well attested. The Egyptian **Report of Wenamun** (c. 11th century BCE) describes a young man at Byblos seized by a god in a frenzy. **Mari** texts (18th century BCE) mention ecstatics (*muḫḫûm*). Saul's experience fits that Levantine background.

### The Philistine "garrison" at Gibeah

A Philistine *nĕṣîb* (garrison, prefect, or perhaps a stele) at "Gibeath-elohim" (10:5) implies Philistine control in the Benjamin highlands. Philistine pottery is rare in the highlands, so Philistine domination there, if real, was probably exercised through raids and tribute rather than settlement.

### The text of 10:27 and 11:1

**4QSam<sup>a</sup>** contains a paragraph absent from the Masoretic text, describing how Nahash king of Ammon oppressed the Gadites and Reubenites by gouging out their right eyes. Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.68–71) knew this material. Many modern translations (e.g. the NRSV) now include it at the end of chapter 10. See the commentary on chapter 11.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistine pressure on the highlands is the consistent background of Saul's career.
- Ecstatic prophetic groups are attested in the region.
- Selection by lot was a known method of divine decision (cf. Josh 7:14–18; 1 Sam 14:41–42).

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 10 | Analysis |
|-------------|----------|
| Private anointing (10:1) and public lot (10:17–24) | Two different accounts of how Saul became king; a third follows in ch. 11 |
| "Is Saul also among the prophets?" (10:11–12) | A second, different explanation of the same proverb appears in 19:24 |
| Mizpah assembly speech (10:18–19) | Deuteronomistic language repeating ch. 8's theme of rejection |

## Assessment

The chapter gathers older stories that legitimated Saul and frames them with the Deuteronomistic view that kingship was a concession. That the editors kept multiple accounts suggests the traditions were well established and respected. Historians can say that a Benjaminite named Saul probably emerged as a leader in a time of Philistine pressure. The mechanism, whether anointing, lot, or military acclamation, cannot be determined from these stories.
