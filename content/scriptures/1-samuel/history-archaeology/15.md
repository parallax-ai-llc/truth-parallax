## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The Amalekites are not attested outside the Bible, the campaign's scale is impossible, and the story's purpose is to explain why God rejected Saul. The practice of *ḥerem*, the total destruction of enemies as an offering to a god, is genuinely ancient, as the Mesha Stele shows.

## What the Text Claims

- God commands Saul to destroy **Amalek** utterly: men, women, children, infants, cattle, sheep, camels, and donkeys (15:2–3).
- Saul musters 210,000 men at Telaim and defeats Amalek "from Havilah to Shur, east of Egypt" (15:4–7).
- He spares King **Agag** and the best livestock; Samuel declares Saul rejected and kills Agag himself (15:8–33).

## External Evidence

### *Ḥerem* and the Mesha Stele

The **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE), set up by the king of Moab, describes how Mesha captured the Israelite town of Nebo and killed its 7,000 inhabitants, "for I had devoted it (*ḥrm*) to Ashtar-Kemosh." This uses the same word and concept as 1 Samuel 15. It shows that "devoting" a conquered population to a national god was a real Iron Age practice in the region, not just a late literary idea.

### Amalek

The Amalekites appear only in biblical texts, as raiding nomads of the Negev and Sinai. No inscription or archaeological culture has been identified with them. Some archaeologists connect the Negev highland settlements of Iron I–IIA with semi-nomadic groups, but any link to "Amalek" is speculative.

### Camels

Domesticated dromedary camels appear in the archaeological record of the southern Levant around the late 10th century BCE, notably at the Aravah copper mines (Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef, 2013). Dromedaries were domesticated earlier in Arabia. Camels owned by desert raiders are therefore plausible in the 11th–10th centuries, though the detail may reflect later familiarity.

## Points of Agreement

- *Ḥerem* warfare is an authentic Iron Age concept.
- Desert raiders on Judah's southern frontier fit the geography; Telaim may be the Telem of Joshua 15:24 in the Negev.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Samuel 15 | Historical analysis |
|-------------|---------------------|
| 210,000 soldiers (15:4) | Impossible for the period |
| Amalek destroyed "from Havilah to Shur" | Amalekites reappear in strength in ch. 27 and 30, raiding Ziklag with 400 escaping on camels |
| Second rejection of Saul | Duplicates 13:13–14 |
| Agag as a significant king | No evidence of an Amalekite monarchy |

## Assessment

Chapter 15 is a theological story about obedience and rejection, set against an enemy that later Israelite tradition remembered as its archetypal foe (Exod 17:8–16; Deut 25:17–19). The *ḥerem* motif is authentic to the period. The war itself cannot be verified, its scale is impossible, and the Amalekites' survival in later chapters contradicts its outcome. The text's moral problem, a divine command to kill infants, belongs to theology and ethics rather than to history.
