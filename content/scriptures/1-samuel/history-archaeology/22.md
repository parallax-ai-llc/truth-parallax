## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The picture of David as the leader of a band of outlaws and debtors on Judah's margins is widely regarded by historians as realistic, even unflattering enough to be authentic. The massacre at Nob cannot be verified, but it explains the link between David and the priest Abiathar, which is well attested.

## What the Text Claims

- David hides in the cave of **Adullam**; his family and "everyone in distress, everyone in debt, and everyone discontented" gather to him, about 400 men (22:1–2).
- He leaves his parents with the king of **Moab** (22:3–4).
- Saul learns from **Doeg the Edomite** that Ahimelech helped David; Doeg kills 85 priests and the town of Nob (22:9–19).
- **Abiathar**, Ahimelech's son, escapes to David (22:20–23).

## External Evidence

### Habiru-like bands

The Late Bronze Age **Amarna letters** (14th century BCE) frequently mention *ḫabiru/'apiru*: displaced people, debtors, and runaways who formed armed bands and hired themselves out to local rulers. Nadav Na'aman and others have noted that David's band in 22:2 matches this social type closely. The comparison suggests that the tradition preserves a realistic picture of how an ambitious outsider could build power in the hill country.

### Adullam

Adullam is identified with Tel Adullam (Khirbet esh-Sheikh Madhkur) in the Shephelah, near the Elah Valley. The area is full of caves.

### Moab

The Moabite kingdom is attested from the 9th century BCE by the **Mesha Stele**. The book of Ruth makes David's great-grandmother a Moabite, but Ruth is generally dated later. David's later harsh treatment of Moab (2 Sam 8:2) sits oddly with this friendly refuge.

### Edom and Doeg

Doeg is called an Edomite. Excavations by Thomas Levy and Erez Ben-Yosef at **Khirbat en-Nahas** in the Faynan copper district have shown large-scale copper production and social complexity in the lowlands of Edom in the 11th–10th centuries BCE. An Edomite serving at an Israelite court is plausible, though Doeg himself is not attested.

## Points of Agreement

- David's band of marginal men fits a well-known Levantine social pattern.
- Abiathar's close association with David is consistent across Samuel and Kings (2 Sam 15:24–29; 1 Kgs 2:26–27).
- Regional geography (Adullam, Moab, the forest of Hereth) is coherent.

## Points of Conflict

- **The Nob massacre** has no external confirmation. It functions as the fulfilment of the curse on Eli's house (2:31–33) and to make Saul a slaughterer of priests.
- **Moab as refuge** conflicts with later hostility; the motivation may be related to Ruth's tradition of Moabite ancestry, which is itself late.

## Assessment

The most historically valuable element of the chapter is its unflattering portrait of David as the head of a band of debtors and malcontents. Writers trying to glorify him would hardly invent this, which suggests a genuine memory. Halpern and McKenzie draw on such details to reconstruct a David who rose as a warlord on Judah's frontier. The massacre at Nob, by contrast, serves the narrative's theological and apologetic aims.
