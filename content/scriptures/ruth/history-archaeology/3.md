## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not verifiable.** The chapter describes a private night-time encounter that by its nature could leave no external record. Its customs, winnowing at night and a marriage gesture with a garment, fit the ancient Israelite setting, but the scene is shaped with deliberate ambiguity and wordplay.

## What the Text Claims

- Naomi sends Ruth, washed and anointed, to the **threshing floor** where Boaz is **winnowing barley** at night (3:1–5).
- Ruth uncovers his feet and lies down. At midnight he wakes, and she asks him to "spread your wing [*kanaf*] over your servant, for you are a redeemer" (3:6–9).
- Boaz agrees but says there is a **nearer redeemer** who must have the first right (3:10–13).
- He sends her home before dawn with six measures of barley (3:14–17).

## External Evidence

### Threshing floors and winnowing

Threshing floors were open, flat areas, often on exposed ground near a village, where grain was threshed and then winnowed by tossing it into the evening breeze. Winnowing in the late afternoon and evening, when the sea breeze rises in the Judean hills, is a well-known practice in the region and was recorded by 19th- and 20th-century ethnographers of Palestine. Owners or workers sleeping beside the grain to guard it is realistic. Threshing floors also appear in the Bible as places of public gathering and ritual (2 Sam 24:18–25; 1 Kgs 22:10).

### "Spread your wing over"

The phrase "spread your *kanaf* (wing/garment corner) over" is a marriage idiom. Ezekiel 16:8 uses it for YHWH's covenant-marriage with Jerusalem. Ruth's request picks up Boaz's own words in 2:12, where he blesses her for taking refuge under YHWH's "wings." Commentators have compared it with symbolic gestures of marriage and protection in other cultures, but no exact ancient Near Eastern legal parallel is known.

### The nearer redeemer

The existence of a closer kinsman with prior rights fits the ordered sequence of redeemers in Leviticus 25:48–49 (brother, uncle, cousin, other near relative). The way the redeemer duty is linked with marriage, however, has no basis in the written laws (see the chapter 4 page).

## Points of Agreement

- Night winnowing, sleeping at the threshing floor, and measuring out grain fit the agricultural reality of the region.
- The marriage idiom is attested elsewhere in biblical Hebrew.
- A hierarchy of kin responsible for redemption is consistent with Israelite law.

## Points of Conflict

There is nothing to contradict. The scene's carefully ambiguous language ("feet" as a possible euphemism, "lie down," "know") is literary, building suspense and moral tension. It is the work of a skilled narrator.

## Assessment

Chapter 3 tells us about ancient custom more than about events. Its setting is credible and its idioms are genuine. As a historical source, however, it has the same limitation as the rest of the book: no independent evidence is possible for an intimate scene, and its shaping, with echoes of chapter 2 and of the Genesis stories of Lot's daughters and Tamar, points to deliberate literary composition.
