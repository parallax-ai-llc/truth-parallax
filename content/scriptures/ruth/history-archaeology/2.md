## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed (realistic setting, unverifiable events).** The chapter's agricultural details are accurate to life in ancient Judah. Its events, a private encounter between a Moabite widow and a landowner, cannot be checked against any external source.

## What the Text Claims

- Ruth gleans behind the reapers in a field belonging to **Boaz**, a relative of Elimelech (2:1–3).
- Boaz greets the reapers with a blessing in YHWH's name and gives Ruth protection, water, and a share of the meal (2:4–14).
- He tells his workers to leave extra stalks for her; she beats out about an **ephah of barley** (2:15–17).
- Naomi identifies Boaz as "one of our redeemers" (*go'alenu*, 2:20).
- Ruth gleans until the end of the barley and **wheat harvests** (2:23).

## External Evidence

### Gleaning law

Israelite law commands landowners to leave the edges of fields and the fallen gleanings for the poor, the resident foreigner, the orphan, and the widow (Lev 19:9–10; 23:22; Deut 24:19–22). Ruth, a foreign widow, fits two of these categories. No ancient Near Eastern law code outside the Bible has an exact equivalent, though concern for widows and orphans is a standard royal ideal in Mesopotamian and Ugaritic texts. The chapter thus presupposes the biblical gleaning laws in practice. Since the dating of those laws is itself debated, this does not fix the date of the story.

### The harvest calendar

The **Gezer Calendar** (10th century BCE), a short Hebrew inscription on a limestone tablet, lists the agricultural year by months, including "a month of barley harvest" followed by "a month of harvest and measuring" (generally understood as the wheat harvest). Ruth's sequence, barley then wheat over roughly seven weeks (2:23), matches this calendar and the seven weeks between Passover and Shavuot (Deut 16:9).

### The ephah

An ephah was a dry measure, roughly 13–22 liters by modern estimates (ancient measures varied). An ephah of barley from one day's gleaning is a generous amount, and the story clearly means it to show Boaz's generosity.

### Reapers and threshing

Harvest scenes with reapers cutting grain with sickles and gleaners following are known from Egyptian tomb paintings. Iron Age flint and iron sickle blades are common finds in Israelite village sites. The **Meṣad Ḥashavyahu ostracon** (late 7th century BCE) records a harvest worker complaining that his garment had been seized, a glimpse of the labor relations behind Ruth 2.

## Points of Agreement

- Gleaning by the poor, reaping in teams, the roasted grain (2:14), and the harvest timetable are all realistic.
- The greeting "YHWH be with you" (2:4) fits ancient Hebrew blessing formulas; Iron Age inscriptions from Kuntillet 'Ajrud and Khirbet el-Qom use similar blessings by YHWH.
- Danger to a lone woman in the fields (2:9, 22) reflects a realistic social concern.

## Points of Conflict

There are no direct conflicts with the external record. The difficulty is not contradiction but the absence of any independent evidence for the people involved, together with the chapter's literary shaping (Ruth "happens" to come to Boaz's field, 2:3), which serves the theme of hidden providence.

## Assessment

Chapter 2 is among the most realistic chapters in the Hebrew Bible in its depiction of everyday rural life. It is a good example of how a story can be historically authentic in texture without being historically verifiable in its events. The accuracy of the setting shows that the author knew agricultural life in Judah well. It does not show that these specific events happened.
