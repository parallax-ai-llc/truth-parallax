## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The legal scene at the gate reflects real Israelite institutions, and the narrator's explanatory gloss (4:7) is important evidence that the book was written long after its setting. The legal arrangement combines customs in ways no biblical law describes, and the closing genealogy is most likely a secondary addition derived from Chronicles or a common source.

## What the Text Claims

- Boaz sits at the **city gate**, calls the nearer redeemer and ten elders, and offers him Naomi's field (4:1–4).
- When told that acquiring the field also means acquiring Ruth "to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance," the redeemer declines (4:5–6).
- The transaction is confirmed by a man **removing his sandal** and giving it to the other, "the custom in former times in Israel" (4:7–8).
- Boaz marries Ruth; their son **Obed** is the father of Jesse, the father of **David** (4:13–17).
- A genealogy from **Perez to David** closes the book (4:18–22).

## External Evidence

### The city gate as a court

Excavated Iron Age gates at sites such as **Tel Dan**, **Beersheba**, and **Gezer** have chambers and benches along their walls, and the plaza at Dan's outer gate has a bench and a canopied platform. These finds illustrate the gate as a place of public business and judgment, as in Ruth 4:1–2, Deuteronomy 21:19, and Amos 5:15.

### Levirate marriage and redemption

| Ruth 4 | Biblical law |
|--------|--------------|
| A kinsman (not a brother) is expected to marry the widow | Deut 25:5–10 applies to brothers living together |
| Marriage is linked with redeeming family land | Lev 25:25 concerns land redemption only; no law connects it to marriage |
| Naomi, a widow, has land to sell | Pentateuchal inheritance laws (Num 27:1–11) do not provide for widows to inherit |
| The redeemer removes his own sandal to confirm a transaction | In Deut 25:9 the refused widow pulls off the brother's sandal and spits in his face as a public shaming |

Levirate-type rules are found elsewhere in the ancient Near East, including the **Middle Assyrian Laws** (Tablet A §§30, 33) and the **Hittite Laws** (§193), and the Genesis story of Tamar (Gen 38) involves a father-in-law. The custom was evidently broader and more flexible than Deuteronomy's rule. Scholars divide over whether Ruth preserves older customary law that differed from the written codes or freely adapts legal institutions for the story.

### The sandal gloss

The narrator explains the sandal custom as a practice of "former times" (4:7). This is one of the clearest internal clues to date: the author writes for readers to whom the custom needed explaining. Sandals as a symbol of claiming land appear in Psalms 60:8 and 108:9 ("upon Edom I cast my shoe"). E. R. Lacheman drew attention to texts from **Nuzi** (15th–14th century BCE) in which shoes feature in legal transfers, though the relevance of these parallels is debated.

### The genealogy

Verses 18–22 open with "These are the generations [*toledot*] of Perez," a formula typical of the Priestly material in Genesis. The list of ten names matches **1 Chronicles 2:5–15**. Most scholars consider it secondary because verse 17 already traces the line from Obed to David. The list is also chronologically compressed: three generations (Boaz, Obed, Jesse) span the gap from the judges to David, and Salmon and Boaz bridge the period from the wilderness to the judges.

### David's Moabite connection

**1 Samuel 22:3–4** reports that David, fleeing Saul, asked the king of Moab to shelter his father and mother. This is the only independent biblical hint of a Moabite link in David's family. Some scholars take it as support for a genuine tradition behind Ruth; others think it may have inspired the story. Neither reading can be proved.

## Points of Agreement

- Public legal transactions before elders at the gate are archaeologically and textually well attested.
- Levirate and redemption customs existed in Israel and the wider ancient Near East.
- A Moabite connection in David's family has some independent support in 1 Samuel.

## Points of Conflict

| Ruth 4 | Historical consideration |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Presented as a judges-period event | The narrator explains its custom as old, pointing to a much later date of writing |
| Combines levirate and redemption | No biblical law combines them |
| Ten-generation genealogy from Perez to David | Duplicates 1 Chr 2; probably secondary and chronologically compressed |

## Assessment

Chapter 4 is the book's most historically informative chapter. Its gate scene fits real Israelite legal life. Its gloss on the sandal custom suggests that the book was written well after the events it narrates. Its genealogy ties the story to David, possibly drawing on a real memory of Moabite ancestry, but in a form that most scholars regard as an editorial addition.
