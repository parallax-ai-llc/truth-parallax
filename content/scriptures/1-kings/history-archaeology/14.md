## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate to high for Shishak; low for the prophetic story.** Sheshonq I's Levantine campaign is confirmed by Egyptian evidence, making 14:25–26 the earliest event in Kings with independent support. The Egyptian evidence also differs from the biblical picture: Jerusalem is not on the surviving list, and the campaign focused on the north and the Negev.

## What the Text Claims

- Jeroboam's son Abijah falls ill; his disguised wife consults the blind prophet Ahijah, who predicts the child's death, the end of Jeroboam's house, and Israel's exile "beyond the River" (14:1–18).
- Jeroboam's other acts are written in "the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel" (14:19–20).
- Rehoboam, son of an Ammonite mother, Naamah, reigns 17 years; Judah builds high places, pillars, and Asherim (14:21–24).
- In Rehoboam's fifth year, **Shishak king of Egypt** comes up against Jerusalem and takes the treasures of the Temple and palace, including Solomon's gold shields, which Rehoboam replaces with bronze (14:25–28).
- Rehoboam's acts are in "the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah"; there was continual war between him and Jeroboam (14:29–31).

## External Evidence

### The Bubastite Portal at Karnak

Sheshonq I (Hebrew *Šîšaq*), founder of Egypt's 22nd (Libyan) Dynasty, carved a triumphal relief on the **Bubastite Portal** of the temple of Amun at Karnak. It shows the king smiting enemies and the god leading rows of name-rings, each representing a conquered place. About 150 names survive, many damaged. Readable names include Gaza, Gezer, Aijalon, Beth-horon, Gibeon, Mahanaim, Penuel, Beth-shean, Shunem, Rehob, Taanach, Megiddo, and many sites in the Negev, including Arad.

### The Megiddo stele

A fragment of a monumental stele bearing Sheshonq I's name was found at **Megiddo** by the University of Chicago expedition in the 1920s, though not in a secure stratum. It confirms that Sheshonq reached Megiddo and erected a victory monument there.

### Dating

Egyptian chronology places Sheshonq I at about 945–924 BCE (Kitchen) or slightly later. His campaign is usually dated c. 925 BCE. However, Egyptologists partly rely on the biblical synchronism with Rehoboam's fifth year to date this campaign. The match between the two is therefore **partly circular**, though the general 10th-century date is secure on Egyptian grounds.

### Destruction layers

Many sites show destructions that excavators attributed to Sheshonq. Since the dates of these destructions depend on which chronology is used, attributions shift with the High/Low Chronology debate. At Megiddo, for example, a stele fragment rather than a dated destruction is the firmest evidence.

## Points of Agreement

- An Egyptian pharaoh named Shishak/Sheshonq invaded the land in the late 10th century, as the text says.
- The timing, early in the reigns of Rehoboam and Jeroboam, fits Egyptian chronology.
- Rehoboam's surrender of treasure is consistent with Jerusalem's absence from the list: a city that paid tribute may not have been listed as conquered. This is Kitchen's explanation (**conservative position**), though others note that part of the list is lost.

## Points of Conflict

| Biblical account | Egyptian evidence |
|------------------|-------------------|
| Campaign aimed at Jerusalem | Jerusalem not among surviving names |
| Judah the victim | Most names lie in the territory of **Israel** (Jezreel Valley, Gibeon area, Transjordan) and the Negev |
| Jeroboam sheltered by Shishak (11:40) | Sheshonq's armies attacked the very towns Jeroboam fortified (Penuel, cf. 12:25) |
| 2 Chr 12:2–9 adds 1,200 chariots, Libyans, Sukkiim, Kushites | Later elaboration, though the Libyan dynasty detail fits |

## Assessment

The chapter's Shishak notice is short, factual, and annalistic in tone, which suggests it came from a Judahite court record. Its essential claim is confirmed. Its perspective, however, is narrowly Judahite. It mentions only the loss of Temple treasure and says nothing about the much wider campaign in the north. Finkelstein has argued that Sheshonq's real target was a highland polity centered on Gibeon and Bethel, perhaps a remnant of the Saulide kingdom. Others see the campaign as aimed at controlling trade routes and the Aravah copper industry. The prophetic oracle of Ahijah, which foresees exile "beyond the River," reflects knowledge of the Assyrian deportations of 722 BCE and was composed long after.
