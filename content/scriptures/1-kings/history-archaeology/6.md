## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate for the design, unverifiable for the builder.** The Temple's plan and decoration match Iron Age temples in Syria and the Levant closely, so the description reflects a real type of building and probably the Jerusalem Temple as later known. No archaeological remains of the First Temple have been found, and the chronological framework (480 years after the Exodus) is schematic.

## What the Text Claims

- Building begins in the 480th year after the Exodus, in Solomon's fourth year, in the month Ziv (6:1).
- The house is 60 cubits long, 20 wide, and 30 high, with a porch (*ʾulam*) in front (6:2–3).
- Side chambers in three stories surround the building (6:5–10).
- Stone was dressed at the quarry so no iron tool was heard on site (6:7).
- The interior is paneled with cedar and overlaid with gold, carved with cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers; the inner sanctuary (*dĕbîr*) is a 20-cubit cube containing two olive-wood cherubim 10 cubits high (6:14–35).
- The work takes seven years, ending in the month Bul of Solomon's eleventh year (6:37–38).

## External Evidence

### No remains of the First Temple

The Temple Mount has never been excavated, and the Herodian platform covers whatever remains. No foundation, inscription, or architectural element can be attributed to Solomon's Temple. The famous **ivory pomegranate** inscribed "belonging to the house of [YHW]H" was declared a forgery in its inscription by an Israel Antiquities Authority committee in 2004, a judgment some epigraphers still contest. The **Jehoash inscription**, which purported to describe Temple repairs, is widely regarded as a forgery. Neither can be used as evidence.

### The tripartite temple type

The plan of porch, main hall, and inner shrine on a single axis is well known in the Bronze and Iron Age Levant:

- **ʿAin Dara** (northern Syria), excavated by Ali Abou Assaf, was in use in phases from c. 1300 to c. 740 BCE. It has a porch with two columns, an antechamber, a main hall, and a raised shrine, surrounded by a corridor of side rooms. John Monson (2000) argued it is the closest parallel to Solomon's Temple, sharing date range, plan, scale, side chambers, and decoration with lions, cherubim-like creatures, and palmettes. Huge carved footprints on its threshold suggest a deity entering the shrine.
- **Tell Tayinat** (ancient Kunulua), in the ʿAmuq plain, has a 9th–8th-century temple with a porch and two columns on lion bases.
- Earlier long-room temples at **Hazor**, **Alalakh**, and **Ebla** show the type's long Syrian background.
- At **Tel Moza**, near Jerusalem, an Iron IIA temple excavated from 2012 shows that a Judahite temple outside the capital existed around the 10th–9th centuries.

### Month names

The months Ziv (6:1, 37) and Bul (6:38), and Ethanim (8:2), are not the Babylonian names later used by Jews (Nisan, Tishri, etc.). Bul and Ethanim are attested in **Phoenician** inscriptions; Bul appears, for example, in the inscription of Eshmunazar II of Sidon. Their use suggests an early or Phoenician-influenced source, since later writers would naturally have used the Babylonian calendar.

### The 480 years

The figure 480 equals 12 generations of 40 years and is widely regarded as schematic. Taken literally, it puts the Exodus c. 1446 BCE, a date favored by some conservative scholars but contradicted by the archaeological record of Late Bronze Age Canaan (see the Exodus and Joshua pages).

## Points of Agreement

- The Temple's plan, dimensions, side chambers, and decorative program are consistent with Iron Age temple architecture in Syria and Phoenicia. A writer of the exilic period could still have known the Jerusalem Temple, which stood until 586 BCE, so the description may reflect its final form rather than its first.
- At about 27 × 9 m internally, the building is modest by Near Eastern standards and comparable to ʿAin Dara and Tayinat. It functioned as a royal chapel attached to the palace, as was common in the region.
- Gold overlay on wood and ivory is attested in Phoenician and Syrian art.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| Built by Solomon c. 960 BCE | Plausible but unproven; no remains, and Jerusalem's 10th-century scale is debated |
| 480 years after the Exodus | Schematic; does not fit external evidence for a literal 15th-century Exodus |
| Entire interior overlaid with gold, even the floor (6:21–22, 30) | Probably idealized; gilding of selected elements is more likely |

## Assessment

The description in chapter 6 is not fantasy. It describes a temple of a well-known Levantine type, with features that archaeology has confirmed in neighboring cultures. What cannot be confirmed is the date and builder. Most scholars accept that the Jerusalem Temple was a royal sanctuary founded early in the monarchy, and Solomon remains the natural candidate. Finkelstein and others allow that the building may have been more modest at first and enlarged in the 9th–8th centuries. The text likely reflects the Temple as the exilic writers remembered it, framed by a symbolic chronology.
