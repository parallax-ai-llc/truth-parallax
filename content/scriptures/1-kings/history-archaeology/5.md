## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate.** A treaty with a king of Tyre for Lebanese timber and Phoenician craftsmen fits everything known about the Iron Age Levant, and Josephus's Tyrian sources place a King Hiram in the 10th century. The labor figures are inflated, and the chapter follows a standard temple-building pattern.

## What the Text Claims

Note: verse numbers follow the English and Catholic versification (5:1–18); in Hebrew Bibles this is 5:15–32.

- Hiram king of Tyre, a friend of David, sends envoys to Solomon (5:1).
- Solomon asks for cedar from Lebanon cut by Sidonian workers, in exchange for wages (5:2–6).
- Hiram's men bring the logs down to the sea and float them in rafts to an agreed place; Solomon pays 20,000 kors of wheat and 20 kors of oil a year (5:7–12).
- Solomon raises a levy (*mas*) of 30,000 Israelites, working in Lebanon in shifts of 10,000 a month, under Adoniram (5:13–14).
- He also has 70,000 burden-bearers and 80,000 stonecutters, with 3,300 overseers; large dressed stones are quarried for the Temple's foundations (5:15–18).

## External Evidence

### Hiram of Tyre in Phoenician sources

No contemporary inscription of Hiram survives. Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.106–127), however, quotes the Hellenistic historians **Menander of Ephesus** and **Dius**, who claimed to use Tyrian archives. They list Hiram son of Abibaal as reigning 34 years and say the Jerusalem Temple was built in his twelfth year. Menander's king list runs from Hiram to the founding of Carthage, which classical sources date to c. 814 BCE. Working back gives Hiram a reign roughly in the mid-10th century, though the details of the list and the exact years are debated. This is an independent if late tradition broadly consistent with the biblical setting.

### Lebanese cedar as royal material

Kings across the Near East sought cedar from Lebanon for temples and palaces. Mesopotamian rulers from Gudea of Lagash to the Neo-Assyrian kings boast of bringing cedar for their buildings. The Egyptian **Report of Wenamun** (c. 1075 BCE) describes an envoy sent to Byblos to buy timber for the barque of Amun. The prince of Byblos, Zakar-Baal, insists on payment, consults his father's records of earlier deliveries, and has the logs cut and taken to the shore. The parallels with 5:6–11, including hard bargaining and logs prepared at the coast, are close.

### Forced labor

The Hebrew *mas* (forced labor) corresponds to Akkadian *massu*. An **Amarna letter (EA 365)** from Biridiya, ruler of Megiddo in the 14th century BCE, reports that he alone supplied corvée workers to cultivate fields at Shunem for Pharaoh. Labor service was a standard obligation in Canaanite city-states long before Israel.

### Dressed stone

Ashlar masonry, finely dressed rectangular blocks, appears in Iron Age Israel at royal sites such as Samaria, Megiddo, and Jezreel. Its use there is often credited to Phoenician influence. These examples are dated by most scholars to the 9th century, and by some to the 10th, depending on the chronology adopted.

## Points of Agreement

- Phoenician craftsmanship and timber supply for an Israelite royal building program is entirely plausible and fits the close Omride ties with Tyre in the 9th century (16:31).
- Payment in agricultural produce suits an inland highland state trading with a coastal city that had little farmland.
- Rafting logs along the coast is attested in Assyrian reliefs from Khorsabad showing timber being transported by sea.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| 30,000 + 70,000 + 80,000 laborers (5:13–15) | Far beyond the likely population of 10th-century Israel and Judah; 2 Chr 2:17–18 adds a census of foreigners |
| Israelites conscripted (5:13) | Contradicts 9:22, which says Solomon made no Israelites slaves; the tension suggests different sources |
| Hiram "always a friend of David" (5:1) | The Tyrian chronology makes a Hiram active under both David and Solomon difficult; see 2 Sam 5:11 |

## Assessment

Victor Hurowitz showed that the biblical account of the Temple (1 Kgs 5–8) follows a pattern found in Mesopotamian and Northwest Semitic building inscriptions: divine approval, gathering of precious materials (especially cedar), organization of labor, construction, dedication, and blessing. The chapter therefore uses a royal literary form. Within that form, the Phoenician connection is the most credible element and is supported by Tyrian tradition and by the general cultural picture. The labor figures belong to the genre of royal exaggeration. The forced levy itself, however, is a believable memory, and it becomes the grievance that breaks the kingdom in chapter 12.
