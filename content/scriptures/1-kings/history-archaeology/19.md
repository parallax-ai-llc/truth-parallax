## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter is a theological narrative modeled on Moses at Sinai. Its interest for historians lies in the figures it names: Hazael of Damascus and Jehu of Israel are firmly attested in Assyrian and Aramaic inscriptions, though the chapter's account of how they came to power is inconsistent with 2 Kings.

## What the Text Claims

- Jezebel threatens Elijah, who flees to Beersheba and into the wilderness, where an angel feeds him (19:1–8).
- He travels forty days and forty nights to **Horeb**, the mountain of God (19:8).
- YHWH is not in the wind, earthquake, or fire, but in "a sound of sheer silence" (19:11–12).
- Elijah is commanded to anoint **Hazael** king of Aram, **Jehu son of Nimshi** king of Israel, and **Elisha** son of Shaphat as his successor (19:15–18).
- Elijah throws his cloak over Elisha, who is plowing with twelve yoke of oxen (19:19–21).

## External Evidence

### Hazael

Hazael of Damascus is one of the best-documented Levantine kings of the 9th century:

- An inscription of **Shalmaneser III** on a statue from Assur says that Hadadezer "passed away" and that "Hazael, son of a nobody, seized the throne." The phrase marks him as a usurper.
- Shalmaneser's annals record campaigns against Hazael in 841 and 838 BCE.
- The **Tel Dan Stele**, probably erected by Hazael, boasts of killing the kings of Israel and of the House of David.
- Inscribed booty items, including an ivory from Arslan Tash and horse trappings later dedicated at Greek sanctuaries on Samos and at Eretria, name "our lord Hazael."

### Jehu

Jehu appears on Shalmaneser III's **Black Obelisk** (841 BCE), where he, or an envoy, bows before the Assyrian king and is named as "Jehu son of Omri" (*Iaua mar Ḫumri*). His dynasty ruled Israel for nearly a century.

### Horeb

Horeb is the Deuteronomic name for Sinai. Its location is unknown. The identification with Jebel Musa in southern Sinai dates from the Byzantine period. No archaeological evidence links any site with Moses or Elijah.

## Points of Agreement

- Hazael and Jehu both seized power in the 840s BCE, transforming the politics of the region. The chapter correctly associates them with the end of the Omride period.
- Hazael's description as "son of a nobody" in Assyrian texts fits the biblical portrayal of him as a usurper (2 Kgs 8:7–15).
- The plowing scene with twelve pairs of oxen suggests a prosperous farming family, a plausible social setting.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Kings 19 | Elsewhere |
|------------|-----------|
| Elijah to anoint Hazael | In 2 Kgs 8:7–15 Elisha, not Elijah, prompts Hazael's usurpation; no anointing is described |
| Elijah to anoint Jehu | In 2 Kgs 9:1–6 a young prophet sent by Elisha anoints him |
| Jehu son of Nimshi | 2 Kgs 9:2 makes him son of Jehoshaphat son of Nimshi; Assyrians call him "son of Omri," meaning a king of Israel's land |
| 40 days and nights to Horeb | Distance from Beersheba to southern Sinai takes far less; the number echoes Moses (Exod 24:18; 34:28) |

## Assessment

The chapter presents Elijah as a new Moses: fleeing a hostile ruler, fed miraculously in the wilderness, journeying forty days to the mountain of God, and receiving a revelation. It also counters storm-god imagery. YHWH is not found in wind, earthquake, or fire, the traditional manifestations of Baal. The commission to anoint Hazael and Jehu shows that the Elijah and Elisha traditions were originally separate and later harmonized. Marsha White has argued that parts of the Elijah narrative were shaped as propaganda for Jehu's coup. The chapter tells us how prophetic circles interpreted the violent changes of the 840s, but not what Elijah actually did.
