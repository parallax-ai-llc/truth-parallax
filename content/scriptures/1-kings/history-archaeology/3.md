## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** A diplomatic marriage with an Egyptian princess is possible in the Third Intermediate Period but unattested. The dream at Gibeon follows a well-known royal genre, and the judgment between two mothers is an international folk tale. The chapter tells us more about ideals of kingship than about events.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon makes a marriage alliance with Pharaoh and brings his daughter to the City of David (3:1).
- The people sacrifice at high places because the Temple is not yet built; Solomon offers a thousand burnt offerings at "the great high place" at Gibeon (3:2–4).
- YHWH appears to Solomon in a dream and grants him wisdom, along with riches and honor he did not request (3:5–15).
- Solomon resolves a dispute between two prostitutes over a living child by ordering it cut in two (3:16–28).

## External Evidence

### An Egyptian princess

In the 14th century BCE, Amenhotep III refused a Babylonian request for a princess, stating that a daughter of the king of Egypt had never been given to anyone (Amarna letter EA 4). In the weaker Third Intermediate Period this rule may have relaxed. Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen propose that the pharaoh was **Siamun** of the 21st Dynasty, pointing to a relief from Tanis showing Siamun smiting an enemy. Kitchen interprets the enemy as a Philistine and links the scene to the capture of Gezer (9:16). The interpretation of the relief is disputed, and no Egyptian text records a marriage to an Israelite king. Some critical scholars suggest that the motif enhances Solomon's prestige and may draw on later diplomatic practice.

### Gibeon

Gibeon is securely identified with **el-Jib**, where James Pritchard's excavations (1956–62) found jar handles inscribed *gbʿn*, a large rock-cut pool, and an extensive wine industry, mostly of the later Iron Age. No "great high place" has been identified, either at el-Jib or at nearby Nebi Samwil, a traditional candidate. The prominence of Gibeon here, rather than Jerusalem, is awkward for the Deuteronomist, who apologizes for it (3:2–3). This tension suggests an older tradition that the editor did not invent.

### The royal dream

Kings receiving divine favor in a dream is a common genre:

- The Egyptian **Dream Stele** of Thutmose IV describes how the sphinx appeared to the prince in a dream and promised him the throne.
- In the Ugaritic epics, **Kirta** receives instructions from El in a dream after offering sacrifices.
- Mesopotamian and Hittite kings report dream revelations confirming their rule.

The pattern of sacrifice, sleep, divine appearance, and a granted request resembles dream incubation at a sanctuary.

### The judgment of the two mothers

The story of a ruler discovering the true mother by threatening the child belongs to a widespread folk motif. Versions appear in Indian Buddhist literature (the *Mahosadha Jātaka*) and elsewhere. Hugo Gressmann pointed out the parallels in the early 20th century. The tale may have reached Israel through oral tradition or arisen independently. Either way, it was attached to Solomon to illustrate his wisdom.

## Points of Agreement

- Diplomatic marriages were standard international practice, and an Egyptian alliance with a highland ruler is not impossible in the 10th century.
- Worship at local high places before the 7th century is historically accurate. Archaeology shows many local shrines in Iron Age Israel and Judah, such as at Arad and Tel Moza.
- The ideal of the king as wise judge, especially protecting the vulnerable, is a stock feature of ancient Near Eastern royal ideology, as in the prologue to the Code of Hammurabi.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| Marriage to Pharaoh's daughter | No Egyptian record; contrary to earlier Egyptian practice, though possible later |
| 1,000 burnt offerings (3:4) | Conventional large number, typical of royal display language |
| Judgment of the two mothers | International folk tale, not a court record |
| "Riches and honor" above all kings (3:13) | Anticipates the idealized portrait of chs. 4 and 10 |

## Assessment

Chapter 3 introduces Solomon as the ideal wise king. It uses familiar materials: a diplomatic marriage, a dream revelation at a sanctuary, and a wisdom tale. None of these can be confirmed. The Gibeon tradition and the apology for worship at high places look like older elements preserved despite the editor's discomfort. The marriage to Pharaoh's daughter frames Solomon's later downfall in chapter 11, where foreign wives lead him astray. That suggests its literary role mattered at least as much as its historical basis.
