## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The visit of the Queen of Sheba is a legend that fits the Arabian incense trade of the 8th–7th centuries better than the 10th. The figures for gold, shields, and chariots are idealized. A few details, such as horse trading with Kue (Cilicia) and Phoenician long-distance voyages, reflect real Iron Age commerce.

## What the Text Claims

- The queen of Sheba visits Solomon with a great caravan of camels carrying spices, gold, and precious stones, tests him with riddles, and praises his wisdom (10:1–13).
- Solomon's annual income is 666 talents of gold, apart from trade revenue (10:14–15).
- He makes 200 large and 300 small gold shields and an ivory throne overlaid with gold, flanked by lions (10:16–20).
- Silver is "as common as stones" in Jerusalem; ships of Tarshish bring gold, silver, ivory, apes, and baboons or peacocks every three years (10:21–27).
- He has 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen and imports horses from Egypt and Kue, selling chariots and horses to Hittite and Aramean kings (10:26–29).

## External Evidence

### Saba and its queens

Sheba is **Saba**, a kingdom in southwestern Arabia (Yemen) centered on Marib. Sabaean monumental inscriptions begin around the 8th century BCE. Assyrian records mention Sabaean rulers: Itamru paid tribute to Sargon II (c. 715 BCE), and Karibilu sent gifts to Sennacherib. No Sabaean source names a queen. Assyrian texts do name **queens of the Arabs** in northern Arabia, such as Zabibe and Samsi under Tiglath-pileser III (8th century). The motif of an Arabian queen bringing gifts may draw on that later world.

### Camels and the incense route

The caravan of camels (10:2) presupposes domesticated camels in long-distance trade. Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef (2013) found that domesticated camel bones appear in the Aravah copper sites only from the last third of the 10th century BCE. Camel caravans were thus just beginning in Solomon's era. The organized incense trade from South Arabia flourished especially from the 8th century under Assyrian interest. The story is not impossible for the 10th century, but it fits the 8th–7th better.

### Copper and wealth in the south

Erez Ben-Yosef's work at **Timna** and Thomas Levy's at **Khirbet en-Nahas** in Faynan show large-scale copper production peaking in the 10th century, controlled apparently by a local, largely nomadic polity identified by many with early Edom. This shows real wealth and trade in the region in Solomon's time. There is no evidence that Jerusalem controlled it, and Ben-Yosef himself treats the polity as Edomite.

### Silver, Tarshish, and the west

Lead-isotope analysis of silver hoards from the southern Levant (Tzilla Eshel and colleagues, 2019) shows silver from Sardinia and Iberia arriving in the 10th–9th centuries, consistent with early Phoenician voyages to the western Mediterranean. "Ships of Tarshish" probably refers to a type of large long-distance ship, named after a western destination often identified with Tartessos in Spain. Phoenician trade could have brought exotic goods to an inland ally, but there is no evidence of Israelite participation in the 10th century.

### Horses from Kue

"Kue" (10:28) is **Que**, the plain of Cilicia in southern Anatolia, attested in Assyrian inscriptions and in the Phoenician–Luwian Karatepe inscription. Cilicia was known for horses. The Hebrew traditionally read as "from Egypt" (*miṣrayim*) has been understood by Hayim Tadmor and others as a northern land, Muṣri, in Anatolia, since Egypt was not a horse-breeding center. The detail fits Iron Age trade networks well.

## Points of Agreement

- Arabian trade, Phoenician seafaring, Cilician horses, and the use of ivory in royal furniture are real features of the Iron Age world.
- Ivory-inlaid furniture is attested at Samaria, Nimrud, and Arslan Tash. Thrones flanked by lions are a common royal motif in Levantine and Assyrian art.
- The economic boom of the copper industry in the Aravah in the 10th century shows that the south was not economically empty.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Evidence |
|-------|----------|
| A Sabaean queen visits c. 950 BCE | Sabaean state attested from c. 8th century; no queen of Saba known |
| 666 talents of gold a year (10:14) | Over 20 tonnes; no support. Alan Millard compares Egyptian temple gifts of Osorkon I (conservative position) |
| Silver as common as stones (10:27) | Hyperbole; Levantine silver hoards are modest |
| 1,400 chariots | Compare Ahab's 2,000 at Qarqar (853 BCE), which reflects the 9th-century Omride kingdom |

## Assessment

Chapter 10 is the climax of the golden-age portrait and the least historical part of the Solomon story. Its details were drawn from a real world of Arabian, Phoenician, and Anatolian commerce, but that world is best attested in the 9th–7th centuries. Finkelstein and Silberman suggest that the portrait of Solomon's wealth reflects the international trade of Judah under Assyrian domination, projected back onto the founder of the Temple. The Queen of Sheba became an enduring figure in later Jewish, Christian, Islamic (Qur'an 27), and Ethiopian tradition (the *Kebra Nagast*), but she has no independent historical footprint.
