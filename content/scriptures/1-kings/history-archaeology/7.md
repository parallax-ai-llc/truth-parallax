## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate for the objects, low for the scale.** The bronze furnishings, columns, and stands have good parallels in Phoenician, Cypriot, and Syrian craft. The palace complex is not attested in Jerusalem, and the closest archaeological candidates for "Solomonic" palaces, at Megiddo, are redated by many to the 9th century.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon builds his own palace over thirteen years (7:1).
- The palace complex includes the House of the Forest of Lebanon (100 × 50 × 30 cubits, with cedar pillars), the Hall of Pillars, the Hall of the Throne or Judgment, his residence, and a house for Pharaoh's daughter, all of costly dressed stone (7:2–12).
- Hiram, a bronze-worker from Tyre whose mother was from Naphtali, casts two bronze pillars named **Jachin** and **Boaz** for the Temple porch (7:13–22).
- He makes the "molten sea," a bronze basin 10 cubits across and 30 in circumference, resting on twelve bronze oxen (7:23–26).
- He makes ten wheeled bronze stands with basins, and other vessels (7:27–47).
- The bronze is cast in clay ground in the Jordan plain between Succoth and Zarethan (7:46).

## External Evidence

### Palaces

No palace of Solomon has been found in Jerusalem. Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure" has been proposed as David's palace, not Solomon's, and is disputed (see the 2 Samuel overview).

At Megiddo, **Palace 1723** and **Palace 6000** were attributed to Solomon by Yadin and others, partly on the basis of 1 Kgs 9:15. David Ussishkin compared Palace 1723 to the **bit-hilani**, a north Syrian palace type with a columned portico and a broad reception hall, known from Zincirli and Tell Halaf. Under the Low Chronology these palaces date to the 9th century. Under Amihai Mazar's chronology a late 10th-century date remains possible. The description of a hall with rows of cedar pillars (7:2–3) fits Iron Age columned halls, but no specific building matches it.

### Two freestanding pillars

Freestanding pairs of pillars at temple entrances are well attested. Herodotus (2.44) saw two pillars, one of gold and one of emerald, in the temple of Heracles (Melqart) at Tyre. The temples at **ʿAin Dara** and **Tell Tayinat** had two columns in their porches. Small clay shrine models from the Levant, including some from Khirbet Qeiyafa, show pillars flanking the entrance. The names Jachin ("he establishes") and Boaz ("in strength") may be the opening words of dedicatory inscriptions, but their meaning is debated.

### The molten sea and the stands

The bronze sea, about 4.5 m across, would have been a major casting project. Its stated proportions (10 cubits across, 30 around) use the rough ratio of 3, normal in ancient practical measurement. Its 2,000-bath capacity (7:26) differs from 2 Chr 4:5 (3,000).

The ten **wheeled stands** (*mĕkōnôt*) have striking parallels in Late Bronze and early Iron Age **Cyprus**: four-sided bronze stands on wheels, with openwork panels showing animals and figures, such as examples from Enkomi and Kourion. These show that the biblical description reflects real Eastern Mediterranean bronze craft.

### Casting in the Jordan Valley

The clay ground of the central Jordan Valley was suitable for casting. Metalworking installations have been reported from Iron Age levels at Tell Deir Alla, often identified with Succoth, and Tell es-Saʿidiyeh is a proposed site for Zarethan. These identifications are possible but not certain.

## Points of Agreement

- Phoenician involvement in bronze craft and palace architecture is consistent with Levantine practice.
- Palace complexes combining royal residence, reception halls, and a temple are well known in the region.
- The furnishings have genuine parallels in Cypriot and Syrian metalwork.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| Palace larger than the Temple, built over 13 years | No remains in Jerusalem; scale questioned given Jerusalem's size |
| Megiddo palaces as Solomon's work | Dated by many to the Omride period |
| Hiram's mother from Naphtali (7:14) vs. Dan (2 Chr 2:14) | Minor discrepancy, showing variant tradition |
| Capacity of the sea: 2,000 baths vs. 3,000 | Internal biblical variant |

## Assessment

Chapter 7 describes objects and architecture that fit the Iron Age Levant well, especially the Phoenician and Cypriot bronze industry. This suggests the writer drew on knowledge of the Jerusalem Temple's furnishings, which were famous enough to be listed again when the Babylonians carried them off (2 Kgs 25:13–17). The palace complex is another matter. It has no archaeological trace, and its supposed counterparts elsewhere may belong to the Omrides. The chapter is credible as a description of what the Temple contained by the late monarchy, and less so as a record of Solomon's own building program.
