## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed to moderate.** The existence of two separate kingdoms, Israel in the north and Judah in the south, from the late 10th century is historically certain, and Dan does have an Iron Age royal sanctuary. Whether the north "seceded" from a prior united kingdom is the key question, and the account of Jeroboam's cult is shaped by southern polemic.

## What the Text Claims

- Rehoboam goes to Shechem to be made king; the northern tribes ask him to lighten Solomon's heavy yoke (12:1–5).
- Rejecting the elders' advice, he threatens heavier burdens; Israel rejects the house of David, stones Adoram the overseer of forced labor, and makes Jeroboam king (12:6–20).
- A prophet forbids Rehoboam to fight (12:21–24).
- Jeroboam fortifies Shechem and Penuel (12:25).
- To prevent pilgrimage to Jerusalem, he sets up two golden calves at **Bethel** and **Dan**, declaring "Here are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt," appoints non-Levite priests, and moves the autumn festival to the eighth month (12:26–33).

## External Evidence

### Two kingdoms

From the 9th century, Assyrian records and the Mesha Stele refer to **Israel** (the House of Omri) as the larger northern kingdom. The Tel Dan Stele mentions both a king of Israel and a king of the "House of David." The division into two states is therefore secure for the 9th century. The question is whether they were ever united. The Bible says they were. Finkelstein and others argue that Israel and Judah developed separately from distinct highland societies, and that the "united monarchy" was a later Judahite construction. Moderate archaeologists (Amihai Mazar, Faust) accept a brief, loose union under David and Solomon.

### Tel Dan

Avraham Biran's excavations at Tel Dan uncovered a large sacred precinct (Area T) with a monumental platform of ashlar stone, a large horned altar, and auxiliary rooms. Biran assigned the first phase of the platform to Jeroboam I in the late 10th century and later phases to Ahab and Jeroboam II. The dating of the earliest phase depends on the chronology debate, and some scholars assign it to the 9th century or to an Aramean phase. Either way, Dan was an important royal cult center of the northern kingdom.

### Bethel

Bethel (Beitin) was excavated by W. F. Albright and James Kelso, but the results were poorly published. Finkelstein and Lily Singer-Avitz (2009) re-examined the material and argued that Bethel was sparsely occupied in Iron IIA and reached its peak in the 8th century. No temple or calf has been found. Amos (7:10–13) confirms that Bethel was a royal sanctuary in the 8th century.

### Bull iconography

A bronze bull figurine from the "**Bull Site**" in the northern Samarian hills (Iron I), published by Amihai Mazar, shows bull imagery in early Israelite worship. In Canaanite tradition, El was called "Bull," and the storm god was associated with the bull. Many scholars think Jeroboam's calves were meant as pedestals for an invisible YHWH, parallel to the cherubim in Jerusalem, not as idols.

### Penuel and Shechem

Penuel and Mahanaim, east of the Jordan, appear in Sheshonq I's list at Karnak (see chapter 14), which places an Egyptian campaign in the very area Jeroboam fortified.

## Points of Agreement

- Royal sanctuaries at Dan and Bethel, on the northern and southern borders of Israel, are historically credible.
- Shechem (Tell Balata) was the traditional center of the northern highlands, already a major city in the Amarna period. A covenant-making assembly there fits its role.
- Grievances over forced labor are a credible cause of political rupture.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| The north secedes from a unified kingdom | Debated; Israel and Judah may always have been distinct |
| The calves are idolatrous "gods" | Polemical; the calves were probably YHWH's pedestals |
| The calves' slogan (12:28) | Matches Exod 32:4, 8. Many scholars think Exod 32 was written as a polemic against Jeroboam's cult |
| LXX account (3 Kgdms 12:24a–z) | A different version of Jeroboam's rise, including marriage to an Egyptian princess, shows the story circulated in several forms |

## Assessment

The northern kingdom of Israel was real, powerful, and independent of Judah. Its cult centers at Dan and Bethel are archaeologically and textually supported. The narrative of the division, however, is told from a Judahite point of view. It portrays the north as rebellious and its religion as apostate, providing the yardstick ("the sin of Jeroboam") against which every later northern king is judged. Historians generally accept the core: Jeroboam established an independent kingdom in the north in the late 10th century, with its own royal sanctuaries. Whether this was a secession or the emergence of a separate state is the more fundamental debate.
