## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** This is a prophetic legend that names King Josiah three centuries in advance. It was composed with knowledge of Josiah's destruction of the Bethel altar (2 Kgs 23:15–18) and is designed to link the beginning and end of the northern cult. It has no independent historical control.

## What the Text Claims

- A man of God from Judah comes to Bethel as Jeroboam stands at the altar and proclaims that a son of David's house named **Josiah** will sacrifice the priests of the high places on it (13:1–2).
- The altar splits and its ashes pour out; Jeroboam's hand withers when he stretches it out, and is restored at the prophet's prayer (13:3–6).
- Forbidden to eat or drink in Bethel, the man of God is deceived by an old prophet of Bethel, eats with him, and is killed by a lion on the way home; the lion stands by the body without eating it (13:7–28).
- The old prophet buries him in his own tomb and asks to be buried beside him (13:29–32).
- Jeroboam continues to appoint priests for the high places (13:33–34).

## External Evidence

### No external record

No extra-biblical source refers to the man of God, the old prophet, or any event at Bethel in Jeroboam's reign. Archaeology at Bethel (see chapter 12) is too poorly published to say anything about an altar, and the 10th-century settlement there seems to have been small.

### Prophecy after the event

The naming of Josiah (13:2) is the clearest sign of the chapter's date. 2 Kings 23:15–18 tells how Josiah, c. 622 BCE, destroyed the altar at Bethel, burned bones from tombs on it, and spared the tomb of "the man of God who came from Judah," along with that of the prophet from Samaria. The two passages are closely linked. Historians apply the same principle used for Daniel: a prediction that names a person centuries ahead was written after that person acted (*vaticinium ex eventu*). Chapter 13 was composed by or for the Josianic edition of Kings.

### Literary character

Alexander Rofé classified stories like this as prophetic "legenda," narratives meant to teach a lesson about the authority of the prophetic word. Here the lesson is double: the word against Bethel is irrevocable, and a prophet who disobeys a divine command, even when deceived, is punished. The talking prophet, the miraculous lion, and the self-splitting altar are hallmarks of the genre.

## Points of Agreement

- Bethel was the chief royal sanctuary of the northern kingdom (Amos 7:13 calls it "the king's sanctuary"), and southern prophets did denounce it. Amos of Tekoa in Judah is the historical example from the 8th century (Amos 7:10–17).
- Josiah's reform reaching Bethel is plausible after the Assyrian collapse in the late 7th century, when the area around Bethel may have come under Judahite control.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| Josiah named c. 930 BCE | Written after Josiah's reform (c. 622 BCE) |
| "Cities of Samaria" (13:32) | Samaria was founded by Omri (16:24) decades later; the term reflects the Assyrian province |
| Miraculous signs (altar, hand, lion) | Legendary motifs typical of prophetic tales |

## Assessment

Chapter 13 has value for understanding how the Deuteronomistic History was built rather than for reconstructing the 10th century. The editor framed the entire history of the northern kingdom with a prophecy at its start and fulfillment at its end. The anachronistic reference to "the cities of Samaria" confirms a later perspective. The tale of the old prophet may derive from a local Bethel tradition about a revered tomb that Josiah spared, which the editor reinterpreted. Historically, the story reflects the conflict between Judahite and Israelite sanctuaries and the propaganda of Josiah's reign, not an event of Jeroboam's time.
