## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The gods named as objects of Solomon's apostasy are genuine Iron Age deities, and the notes on adversaries in Edom, Damascus, and the north may preserve real memories of a weakening kingdom. The figures for wives and the theological framework are Deuteronomistic.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon has 700 wives and 300 concubines, including Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, Sidonian, and Hittite women (11:1–3).
- In old age he worships Astarte of the Sidonians, Milcom of the Ammonites, and Chemosh of Moab, building high places for them east of Jerusalem (11:4–8).
- YHWH announces that the kingdom will be torn away, except one tribe for David's sake (11:9–13).
- Adversaries arise: **Hadad the Edomite**, who had fled to Egypt and married Pharaoh's sister-in-law; **Rezon son of Eliada**, who seized Damascus; and **Jeroboam son of Nebat**, an Ephraimite supervisor of forced labor (11:14–28).
- The prophet Ahijah of Shiloh tears a garment into twelve pieces and gives Jeroboam ten (11:29–39).
- Jeroboam flees to **Shishak** of Egypt until Solomon's death (11:40).
- Solomon's other acts are in "the Book of the Acts of Solomon"; he reigned forty years (11:41–43).

## External Evidence

### The gods

- **Chemosh** is the national god of Moab in the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE), where Mesha credits Chemosh with Moab's oppression by Omri and its liberation.
- **Milcom** is attested as the Ammonite god in Ammonite personal names and seals, and probably in the Amman Citadel Inscription.
- **Astarte** (ʿAshtart) is a major Phoenician goddess, attested in inscriptions from Sidon and elsewhere.

The list therefore reflects real regional religion. 2 Kings 23:13 says Josiah destroyed these same shrines on the Mount of Olives. This suggests the tradition about them was known in Josiah's time, possibly as a polemical explanation for shrines that existed then.

### Hadad and Rezon

No external source names Hadad the Edomite or Rezon. The Hadad story echoes other accounts of a foreign prince sheltering in Egypt (compare Jeroboam, and Joseph or Moses). Damascus emerges as a major Aramean power in 9th-century Assyrian sources, ruled by Hadadezer and then Hazael. An Aramean seizure of Damascus in the 10th century is plausible but undocumented.

### Shishak

Jeroboam's flight to **Shishak** links to the Sheshonq I evidence discussed in chapter 14. A pharaoh supporting a rival to Solomon fits Sheshonq's later campaign in the north.

### The "Book of the Acts of Solomon"

The first citation of a source in Kings (11:41). Its nature is unknown. Some scholars think it was a genuine court chronicle, others a later compilation of stories and lists about Solomon.

## Points of Agreement

- Political marriages with neighboring royal houses were standard and would have brought foreign cults to the court. Royal chapels for foreign wives are a natural result.
- The Mesha Stele confirms that Chemosh was understood to act in history just as YHWH does in Kings.
- A kingdom facing breakaways on its periphery after a founder's reign is a common historical pattern.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| 700 wives and 300 concubines (11:3) | Royal hyperbole; meant to illustrate excess |
| Hadad married into Pharaoh's family (11:19) | Unattested; repeats the motif of marriage to Egyptian royalty |
| Reign of 40 years (11:42) | A conventional generation, as for David |
| One tribe left "for David's sake" (11:13, 32) | Arithmetic problem (10 + 1 of 12); Deuteronomistic theological explanation of the division |

## Assessment

Chapter 11 turns the golden age into a theological lesson: foreign wives brought foreign gods, and apostasy caused the kingdom's division. This explanation belongs to the Deuteronomistic editors, who measured every king by loyalty to YHWH alone and to Jerusalem. Within it, however, are notes that look like genuine historical memory. A kingdom that lost Edom and Damascus and faced revolt from its labor administrators is a much more realistic picture than chapters 4 and 10. If the lists of adversaries come from an older source, they suggest that Solomon's realm was smaller and less secure than the idealized portrait implies.
