## Overview

This page does not read 1 Kings as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical inscriptions, and independent historical sources say about the kings and events 1 Kings describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

1 Kings covers roughly a century, from David's death and Solomon's accession (conventionally c. 970 BCE) to the death of Ahab of Israel and the accession of his son Ahaziah (c. 853 BCE). It falls into three blocks: Solomon's reign (chapters 1–11), the division of the kingdom and the early kings of Israel and Judah (12–16), and the Omride period dominated by the prophet Elijah (17–22).

The book's historical value changes sharply across that century. **For Solomon, the narrative is largely unconfirmed and in places contradicted by archaeology. From the late 10th century onward, and especially in the 9th century, it begins to intersect with Egyptian, Assyrian, and Moabite sources, and its skeleton of kings and dates proves broadly accurate.** Around that skeleton sit prophetic legends whose purpose is theological rather than historical.

## Composition and Sources

1 Kings belongs to the Deuteronomistic History (Deuteronomy–2 Kings), identified by Martin Noth in 1943. Frank Moore Cross later argued for a main edition under Josiah (late 7th century) and an exilic revision. The book judges every king by one standard: loyalty to YHWH alone and to worship in Jerusalem. Northern kings all fail because of Jeroboam's sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan.

The editor names his sources. Solomon's reign draws on "the Book of the Acts of Solomon" (11:41). Later kings are referred to the "Book of the Chronicles (literally 'the matters of the days') of the Kings of Israel" or "of Judah" (e.g., 14:19, 14:29, 15:31, 16:27, 22:39). These were probably court annals or king lists, not the canonical Chronicles. Nadav Na'aman and others think they supplied regnal lengths, mothers' names, building notices, and wars, which explains why these details are often accurate. Prophetic story cycles (Ahijah, the man of God from Judah, Elijah, Micaiah) came from different, more popular circles and were inserted around the annalistic frame.

The Septuagint (3 Kingdoms) preserves a notably different text in places, with a different order of chapters 20–21 and long additions after 2:35, 2:46, and 12:24. Some of these may reflect an older Hebrew edition.

## Solomon and the United Monarchy

### The biblical claims

1 Kings presents Solomon as ruler from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt (4:21), married to Pharaoh's daughter, builder of the Temple, a great palace, and fortified cities (9:15), owner of 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen (10:26), and receiver of 666 talents of gold a year (10:14).

### What archaeology shows

**No contemporary inscription names Solomon.** As with David, this silence is expected for the 10th century, when Egypt and Assyria were weak and no Levantine king of the period appears in their records.

The material evidence is more pointed. As argued on the 2 Samuel page, Jerusalem in the 10th century was at most a modest highland town. Eilat Mazar's Large Stone Structure and her "Solomonic" wall on the Ophel are contested, and nothing in Jerusalem can be tied securely to Solomon's Temple or palace. The Temple Mount itself has never been excavated.

### The "Solomonic gates" and the Low Chronology

1 Kings 9:15 says Solomon built Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer. In the 1950s and 1960s Yigael Yadin noticed near-identical six-chambered gates at all three sites and dated them to Solomon. This was once the strongest archaeological support for the united monarchy.

That consensus broke down for three reasons:

- **Typology.** Six-chambered gates were later found at Ashdod, Lachish, and Khirbet el-Mudayna in Moab, some clearly later than Solomon, so the plan is not a royal Solomonic blueprint.
- **Stratigraphy.** David Ussishkin argued that the Megiddo gate belongs with a later stratum than the palaces Yadin associated with it.
- **Chronology.** Israel Finkelstein's Low Chronology (1996) lowered the Iron I/IIA transition by about 75–100 years. The pottery found with the gates matches that of Jezreel, an Omride site destroyed in the 9th century. On this view the gates and palaces are **Omride, c. 880–840 BCE**.

Amihai Mazar's Modified Conventional Chronology, supported by radiocarbon work at Tel Rehov, allows a long Iron IIA (c. 980–840 BCE) that can accommodate 10th-century building. Ongoing radiocarbon studies have narrowed the gap between the camps to a few decades, but have not settled whether the monuments are Solomon's. William Dever and the renewed Gezer excavations argue for the 10th century at Gezer.

### Copper, trade, and an "invisible" state

Nelson Glueck called Timna and Tell el-Kheleifeh "King Solomon's Mines" and Ezion-geber. Later work redated Timna's main mining to Egyptian New Kingdom activity and Tell el-Kheleifeh to the 8th–6th centuries. Erez Ben-Yosef's radiocarbon program at Timna and Thomas Levy's at Khirbet en-Nahas then showed a peak of copper production in the **10th century**, run apparently by a mainly nomadic Edomite polity. This proves sophisticated organization in the region in Solomon's time, but not Israelite control of it.

## The Divided Kingdom and Its Synchronisms

### Sheshonq I

1 Kings 14:25–26 says that "Shishak king of Egypt" attacked Jerusalem in Rehoboam's fifth year. Sheshonq I, founder of the 22nd Dynasty, left a list of conquered places on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak, and a fragment of his victory stele was found at Megiddo. This is **the first event in 1 Kings confirmed by an outside source**. The list, however, does not include Jerusalem in its surviving parts and concentrates on the north and the Negev.

### The Omrides

Omri receives six verses in 1 Kings (16:23–28). Outside sources show he founded one of the most powerful dynasties of the 9th-century Levant:

- **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE): Omri "oppressed Moab many days," and his son continued. 1 Kings never mentions this conquest.
- **Assyrian texts** call Israel *Bīt-Ḫumri*, "House of Omri," for over a century.
- **Kurkh Monolith** of Shalmaneser III: at Qarqar (853 BCE), "Ahab the Israelite" fielded **2,000 chariots** and 10,000 foot soldiers in a coalition with Damascus and Hamath. 1 Kings says nothing about Qarqar and instead portrays Ahab at war with Damascus.
- **Samaria**: a royal acropolis with fine ashlar masonry and, from later strata, the Phoenician-style Samaria ivories (cf. 22:39).
- **Jezreel**: an Omride fortified enclosure excavated by Ussishkin and Woodhead.

### Chronology

The regnal data of 1 Kings are a puzzle: totals for Israel and Judah do not match, and synchronisms sometimes conflict. Edwin Thiele's influential system (a **conservative position**) resolves them with co-regencies and shifts between accession and non-accession dating. It yields Solomon c. 970–931, the division in 931/930, and Ahab's death in 853. Gershon Galil's alternative system differs in details. Both systems are anchored by Assyrian dates: Ahab at Qarqar in 853 and Jehu's tribute in 841. The fact that the biblical figures can be fitted to these fixed points at all suggests they derive from genuine records. Egyptian chronology for Sheshonq (c. 945–924 BCE) itself partly depends on the biblical synchronism, so the agreement there is partly circular.

## Prophets, Baal, and Asherah

The Elijah narratives (chapters 17–19, 21) are **prophetic legend**: miracle stories shaped to present Elijah as a new Moses and to condemn Baal worship. The Baal of Jezebel's court was probably a Phoenician deity; the drought, storm, and fire motifs echo the Ugaritic Baal myths. Josephus, citing the Tyrian historian Menander, records a drought under Ithobaal (biblical Ethbaal), Jezebel's father, which is an intriguing but slight parallel.

The book treats Asherah worship as foreign apostasy. Inscriptions from **Kuntillet ʿAjrud** (c. 800 BCE) bless "by YHWH of Samaria and his Asherah," suggesting that many Israelites regarded Asherah as YHWH's consort. 1 Kings therefore records the religious variety of the period but judges it by later Deuteronomistic standards.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Chronicles** (4th c. BCE) rewrites Solomon's reign, removing his apostasy (1 Kgs 11), and adds detail to Shishak's invasion.
- **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 8; *Against Apion* 1) adds Tyrian annals on Hiram and Ithobaal, which supply a rough 10th-century date for Hiram.
- **Maximalist and conservative scholars** (Kitchen 2003; Provan, Long, and Longman 2003) accept Solomon's kingdom as substantially historical and attribute the gates to him. Alan Millard has argued that the gold figures are plausible by ancient standards.
- **Moderate critics** (Amihai Mazar, Dever, Faust) accept a real but modest 10th-century kingdom and see the Solomon story as greatly embellished.
- **Low Chronology and minimalist scholars** (Finkelstein, Na'aman, Liverani) see the united monarchy as a later construction; the first real Israelite state is Omri's, and the Solomon portrait draws on Omride and Assyrian-period models.

## Reliability Assessment

1 Kings is a book of **two historical halves**. Its Solomon story contains plausible details, including Phoenician involvement in building, temple architecture with real Iron Age parallels, and old administrative lists. Its picture of an empire with extraordinary wealth is **not supported** and probably reflects later idealization. On the Low Chronology, much of the archaeology once used to support it belongs to the Omrides.

From the division of the kingdom onward, the annalistic framework, consisting of names, reign lengths, wars, and building projects, is **largely reliable** where it can be tested. The book's selectivity is its main weakness. It barely mentions Omri, omits Qarqar, and arranges events to illustrate prophetic judgment. Some war stories in chapters 20 and 22 may have been transferred from later kings. The Elijah material is valuable for the religious conflicts of the 9th century, but not as a record of events.
