## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The Elijah cycle consists of prophetic legends whose purpose is theological. Elijah himself may be a historical prophet of Ahab's time, and Josephus preserves a Tyrian tradition of a drought under Jezebel's father. The miracles of this chapter, however, are beyond historical inquiry and the episodes cannot be verified.

## What the Text Claims

- Elijah the Tishbite, from Gilead, announces to Ahab that there will be no dew or rain except by his word (17:1).
- He hides by the brook Cherith, east of the Jordan, where ravens bring him bread and meat (17:2–7).
- He goes to **Zarephath**, a town belonging to Sidon, where a widow's jar of flour and jug of oil do not run out (17:8–16).
- He revives the widow's dead son, and she confesses that he is a man of God (17:17–24).

## External Evidence

### Elijah

No inscription mentions Elijah. His name, *ʾĒliyyāhû*, "my God is YHWH," is a well-formed Yahwistic name of the period. Whether a real prophet lies behind the stories, and how much of them goes back to him, cannot be determined.

### A drought under Ithobaal

Josephus (*Antiquities* 8.324) quotes Menander of Ephesus as saying that in the reign of Ithobaal of Tyre (biblical Ethbaal, Jezebel's father) there was a lack of rain lasting about a year, which ended when the king offered supplications. Josephus himself connected it with Elijah's drought. This is an intriguing independent tradition from Tyrian annals, but it is brief, comes to us second-hand through Josephus, and does not mention Israel. Droughts were frequent in the Levant, so the parallel is suggestive rather than confirmation.

### Zarephath

Zarephath is **Sarafand** (ancient Sarepta) on the Lebanese coast between Tyre and Sidon. James Pritchard excavated it from 1969 to 1974, uncovering a Phoenician town with pottery workshops and an Iron Age shrine. An ivory plaque from the site bears a dedication to "Tanit-Ashtart." The excavations confirm a real Phoenician town in the Sidonian sphere, but nothing connected with Elijah.

### Gilead and Tishbe

"Tishbite" (17:1) is traditionally linked with a place called Tishbe in Gilead. The location has not been securely identified, and the Hebrew may mean "of the settlers of Gilead."

## Points of Agreement

- A drought during the Omride period is supported by Menander's report and is climatically plausible.
- Phoenician-Israelite contact in the 9th century is well established (Jezebel's marriage, Samaria's Phoenician-style art). A prophet traveling into Sidonian territory fits the period's open borders.
- The conflict between YHWH-alone prophets and a court with Phoenician religious ties fits what is known of the Omrides.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| Ravens feed Elijah; flour and oil never run out | Miracle legends, not open to historical verification |
| Resurrection of the widow's son | A miracle story paralleled almost exactly by Elisha's in 2 Kgs 4:18–37, suggesting a shared narrative pattern |
| Drought lasts into the third year (18:1) | Menander's report suggests about one year; the biblical figure may be stylized |

## Assessment

Chapter 17 opens the Elijah cycle, a collection of stories told in prophetic circles about a champion of YHWH against Baal. Their parallels with the Elisha stories (food that multiplies, a raised child) show that both prophets attracted standard miracle motifs. A historian can take three things from the chapter: the Omride period was remembered as a time of religious conflict, a drought in this era is independently reported, and northern Israel was closely tied to Phoenicia. The stories themselves are legend shaped to present YHWH, not Baal the storm god, as the true lord of rain and life.
