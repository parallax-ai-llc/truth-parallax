## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The list of officials and the twelve districts have the dry form of administrative documents, and many scholars think they preserve genuine early material. The surrounding claims of rule from the Euphrates to Egypt, vast provisions, and unparalleled wisdom are idealized and in places anachronistic.

## What the Text Claims

Note: verse numbers follow the English and Catholic versification (4:1–34); in Hebrew Bibles 4:21–34 is numbered 5:1–14.

- A list of high officials: priests, scribes, a recorder, an army commander, an officer over the district governors, a "friend of the king," a palace steward, and Adoniram over the forced labor (4:1–6).
- Twelve district governors each supply the court for one month (4:7–19).
- Judah and Israel are as numerous as the sand and live in security, "each under his vine and fig tree, from Dan to Beersheba" (4:20, 25).
- Solomon rules all kingdoms "from the River" to Philistia and Egypt, "beyond the River from Tiphsah to Gaza" (4:21, 24).
- Daily provisions include 30 kors of fine flour and 100 sheep; Solomon has 40,000 stalls of horses (4:22–28).
- His wisdom surpasses Egypt's and that of named sages; he speaks 3,000 proverbs and 1,005 songs about trees, beasts, birds, reptiles, and fish (4:29–34).

## External Evidence

### The officials

The offices listed are typical of Levantine and Egyptian courts. Tryggve Mettinger compared them with Egyptian titles. The office "over the house" (*ʾăšer ʿal habbayit*) is attested in Judah in the 8th–7th centuries on seals and bullae and in the **Royal Steward inscription** from a tomb in Silwan, which uses the same title. These attestations are later than Solomon. They confirm the title as Israelite and Judahite, not its use in the 10th century.

One detail looks archival. Adoniram over the forced labor appears here, as Adoram under David (2 Sam 20:24), and under Rehoboam (12:18). A real official might plausibly have served across reigns, though a career spanning all three is long.

### The districts

Albrecht Alt (1913) argued that the district list is an authentic administrative document. He noted its form: several governors are named only by patronymic ("Ben-hur," "Ben-deker"), as if the start of the document was damaged. The districts partly follow tribal lines and partly cut across them, incorporating former Canaanite cities such as Megiddo, Taanach, and Beth-shean (4:12). Judah appears to be absent, or appears only in a short note (4:19). This could mean that Judah was exempt, which would help explain northern resentment in chapter 12. Other scholars, including Nadav Na'aman, have argued that the list reflects later northern administration, possibly of the 9th or 8th century.

### "Beyond the River"

The phrase "beyond the River" for the region west of the Euphrates (4:24) is a Neo-Assyrian and especially Persian administrative term (Aramaic *ʿabar nahărā*). It appears to be written from the Mesopotamian side. This suggests that verse 24 at least was written in or after the Assyrian period.

### Wisdom and lists of nature

Albrecht Alt also suggested that Solomon's speaking of "trees, beasts, birds, reptiles, and fish" reflects the genre of **onomastica**, Egyptian lists of natural phenomena such as the *Onomasticon of Amenope*. Egyptian wisdom literature had a direct influence on Proverbs (22:17–24:22 closely parallels the *Instruction of Amenemope*). The idea of a royal patron of wisdom fits Near Eastern practice.

## Points of Agreement

- A state of any size required officials, provisioning, and labor obligations. The general picture is plausible for an emerging monarchy.
- The district list's incorporation of the Jezreel and Beth-shean valleys fits a period when Israel had absorbed the old Canaanite lowland cities.

## Points of Conflict

| Claim | Evidence |
|-------|----------|
| Rule from the Euphrates to Egypt (4:21, 24) | No support; uses later "Beyond the River" terminology |
| 40,000 stalls (4:26) | 2 Chr 9:25 has 4,000; neither figure is confirmed. Compare 1,400 chariots in 10:26 |
| Population "as the sand of the sea" (4:20) | Settlement surveys suggest a 10th-century highland population of only tens of thousands (Broshi and Finkelstein) |
| Security "from Dan to Beersheba" (4:25) | Idyllic formula; many archaeologists date Dan's incorporation into Israel to the late 10th or 9th century |

## Assessment

Chapter 4 combines two kinds of material. The lists of officials and districts look like real administrative records, though scholars disagree whether they come from Solomon's time or from the later northern kingdom. They are among the strongest candidates in the Solomon story for genuinely early sources. Around them the editor built a portrait of an imperial golden age, using later terminology and conventional superlatives. The contrast between the two is a useful guide to the book's composition.
