## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter is an etiology. It explains a circle of stones at the sanctuary of Gilgal by linking it to the Jordan crossing. Gilgal was a real cult site of the monarchic period, but its location is unknown and nothing external supports the founding story.

## What the Text Claims

- Twelve men, one from each tribe, take stones from the riverbed (4:1–8).
- Joshua sets up twelve more stones in the middle of the Jordan, and "they are there to this day" (4:9).
- About 40,000 armed men from Reuben, Gad and half-Manasseh cross ahead of the people (4:12–13).
- The people camp at Gilgal on the tenth day of the first month, and the stones are set up there. Children who ask about them are to be told about the crossing, which is compared with the Red Sea (4:19–24).

## External Evidence

### Gilgal

Gilgal is named as an important sanctuary in the 8th century BCE. Hosea and Amos condemn worship there (Hos 4:15; 9:15; 12:11; Amos 4:4; 5:5), and Saul is made king there (1 Sam 11:15). The name probably means "circle (of stones)." No site has been securely identified. Proposals east of Jericho include Khirbet el-Mafjar and Khirbet en-Nitla, but excavations at the latter found no early remains.

Adam Zertal identified several large stone enclosures in the Jordan Valley and the hills shaped like a foot or sandal. He interpreted them as early Israelite assembly sites of the *gilgal* type. Most archaeologists regard the interpretation as speculative.

### Standing stones

Standing stones (*massebot*) were common throughout Canaan and early Israel. Examples include the row at Gezer, the stones at the Arad shrine and the large stone at Shechem. A circle of twelve stones at a sanctuary would fit this widespread practice. This makes the stones plausible as a real cult feature that later needed an Israelite explanation.

## Points of Agreement

- Gilgal near Jericho was a genuine Israelite sanctuary in the monarchic period.
- Stone monuments as memorials and cultic markers are well attested in the Levant.
- The date of the crossing (the tenth day of the first month, 4:19) matches the spring flood noted in 3:15.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 4 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Two sets of twelve stones (riverbed and Gilgal) | Signs of two combined traditions; the riverbed set is invisible and cannot be checked |
| 40,000 Transjordanian warriors | Implausibly large for Late Bronze or Iron I highland populations, which survey data put in the low tens of thousands for the whole region |
| Stones explain a memory of crossing | Probably the stones came first, and the story grew to explain them |

## Assessment

The question-and-answer formula ("When your children ask you...," 4:6, 21) also appears in Exodus 12:26–27 and 13:14 and Deuteronomy 6:20. It shows the story's purpose: to teach later generations at a cult site. The chapter's numbers and its twelve-tribe structure reflect a later ideal of all Israel acting as one.

Archaeologically, the absence of a firm identification for Gilgal leaves the claim untestable. Survey estimates of the total highland population in Iron I make a fighting force of 40,000 from Transjordan alone implausible.
