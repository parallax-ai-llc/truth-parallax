## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Shechem was a real and ancient cult centre, and its role in Israel's early identity is plausible. The covenant ceremony itself is a literary composition, and its treaty form is debated as either Late Bronze or Neo-Assyrian. The speech's admission that Israel's ancestors worshipped other gods is a striking and probably old tradition.

## What the Text Claims

- Joshua gathers all the tribes at Shechem (24:1).
- God recalls the history: "your ancestors lived beyond the River (Euphrates)... and served other gods." It continues with Abraham, Jacob, Egypt, the exodus and the conquest (24:2–13).
- "Choose this day whom you will serve." The people pledge loyalty to the LORD and put away foreign gods (24:14–24).
- Joshua makes a covenant, writes statutes in "the book of the law of God," and sets up a large stone under the oak at the sanctuary as a witness (24:25–27).
- Joshua dies at 110 and is buried at Timnath-serah (24:29–30). Joseph's bones are buried at Shechem on land Jacob bought for 100 *qesitah* (24:32). Eleazar is buried at Gibeah of Phinehas (24:33).

## External Evidence

### Shechem (Tell Balata)

Shechem was excavated by Ernst Sellin (1913–34) and by G. Ernest Wright's Drew–McCormick expedition (1956–73). It was a major Middle Bronze city with a massive **"fortress temple"** (*migdal*). The temple was rebuilt in the Late Bronze Age and had a large standing stone (*massebah*) in front. Wright linked it to the temple of El-berith or Baal-berith in Judges 9:4, 46. Lawrence Stager later defended a similar identification. The standing stone recalls the "great stone" of 24:26, though the link is interpretive.

### Shechem in the Amarna letters

In the 14th century BCE, **Lab'ayu** of Shechem built a highland domain and was accused by his neighbours of giving land to the *'Apiru*. Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem complains about this in **EA 289**. Notably, Joshua never narrates Shechem's capture, yet Israel holds an assembly there. Some scholars, following Mendenhall, have suggested that the Shechem covenant remembers the peaceful integration of local groups into Israel.

### Treaty form

In 1954–55 George Mendenhall argued that Joshua 24 follows the structure of **Hittite vassal treaties** of the 14th–13th centuries BCE: preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, deposit of the document, witnesses, and blessings and curses. He took this as evidence of an early date. Conservative scholars such as Kitchen still hold this view. Dennis McCarthy and others replied that the parallels are partial and that the **Neo-Assyrian loyalty oaths** of the 7th century, such as Esarhaddon's succession treaty, offer equally close or closer analogies. This would suit a Josianic composition.

### Joseph's tomb

The traditional Tomb of Joseph near Nablus is a later structure, with the present building largely Ottoman. Archaeology cannot confirm the burial.

## Points of Agreement

- Shechem was a central Bronze and Iron Age cult site in the highlands, with a monumental temple and standing stone.
- The admission that the ancestors "served other gods beyond the River" (24:2, 14) runs against later orthodoxy and probably preserves an old tradition.
- The archaic unit *qesitah* (24:32) appears elsewhere only in Genesis 33:19 and Job 42:11.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 24 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| All twelve tribes assemble under Joshua | Projects later all-Israel unity back to the beginning |
| Treaty form proves Late Bronze origin (conservative view) | Neo-Assyrian treaties offer comparable parallels |
| Joshua dies aged 110 | The ideal Egyptian lifespan, also given to Joseph (Gen 50:26) |
| A written "book of the law" (24:26) | Reflects Deuteronomistic concerns |

## Assessment

Joshua 24 probably rests on an old tradition linking Shechem with covenant-making and with the incorporation of diverse groups into Israel. That would fit the Amarna picture of a highland territory where uprooted people and local populations mixed. In its present form, the chapter is a carefully composed summary of Israel's story and an appeal to exclusive loyalty.

The treaty parallels show that the author knew the conventions of ancient political covenants. They cannot settle the date by themselves. The chapter's real historical value lies in what it reveals about Israel's sense of being a people formed by choice rather than by descent alone.
