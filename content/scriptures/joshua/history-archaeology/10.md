## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The chapter contains a fragment of ancient poetry and real Shephelah geography. The swift campaign that destroys all the southern cities does not match the archaeology. The sites were either unoccupied, or destroyed at very different times, or held by Egyptian-aligned rulers.

## What the Text Claims

- Adoni-zedek of Jerusalem leads the kings of Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish and Eglon against Gibeon (10:1–5).
- Joshua marches from Gilgal by night and routs them, and the descent of Beth-horon becomes a scene of slaughter. Great hailstones kill more than the sword (10:6–11).
- Joshua says, "Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and Moon, in the valley of Aijalon." The text cites the "Book of Jashar" (10:12–14).
- The five kings hide in a cave at Makkedah and are executed. Stones placed at the cave's mouth remain "to this day" (10:16–27).
- Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron and Debir are destroyed, and Horam king of Gezer is defeated (10:28–39). Joshua takes "the whole land" in one campaign (10:40–42).

## External Evidence

### Amarna-age rulers

The Amarna letters (c. 1360–1330s BCE) name rulers of several of these cities:

- **Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem** (EA 285–290) pleads for Egyptian troops against the *'Apiru*.
- **Lachish** has several rulers, including Zimreddi and Shipti-Ba'lu.
- **Milkilu of Gezer** appears frequently.

None is called Adoni-zedek, Japhia, Horam or by any other name in Joshua 10. The letters show Egyptian vassals in the 14th century, not an Israelite conquest.

### Lachish

At Lachish (Tell ed-Duweir), Late Bronze Level VII ended c. 1200 BCE. Level VI, a prosperous Canaanite town with strong Egyptian links, was violently destroyed c. 1150–1130 BCE, according to David Ussishkin. A bronze object bearing the cartouche of Ramesses III shows that Lachish was still under Egyptian influence until then. The site was then abandoned for two centuries. Neither destruction fits 1400 BCE, and the later one is too late even for a 13th-century conquest. The destroyers are unknown.

### Other cities

- **Jarmuth** (Tel Yarmut): a huge Early Bronze city, with only modest Late Bronze occupation.
- **Hebron** (Tel Rumeida): strong Middle Bronze walls, but scant Late Bronze remains.
- **Debir:** Albright's identification with Tell Beit Mirsim (destroyed in the late 13th century) has largely given way to **Khirbet Rabud**, proposed by Moshe Kochavi, which shows Late Bronze occupation without a clear conquest horizon.
- **Libnah, Makkedah and Eglon:** identifications remain uncertain.

### The sun and moon

John Holladay (1968) argued that the poetic couplet reflects Mesopotamian astral omens, in which the sun and moon standing together on a certain day was a sign of victory. Colin Humphreys and Graeme Waddington (2017) proposed an annular solar eclipse of 1207 BCE. This is speculative and depends on reading the Hebrew verb as "be dark."

## Points of Agreement

- **The Book of Jashar** is cited again in 2 Samuel 1:18. It was evidently a real collection of old poetry, and the couplet in 10:12–13 may be among the oldest lines in the book.
- **The route** from Gibeon down the Beth-horon ascent to Azekah and the Aijalon valley is geographically precise.
- **Placing a foot on the necks of defeated kings** (10:24) matches Egyptian and Assyrian royal imagery of trampling enemies.
- The book itself admits that Jerusalem and Gezer were not captured (15:63; 16:10).

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 10 | Archaeological record |
|----------|----------------------|
| Lachish captured in one day (10:32) | Level VI destroyed c. 1150–1130 BCE, under Egyptian influence until then |
| Hebron and Jarmuth destroyed | Little or no Late Bronze occupation |
| Kings of Jerusalem, Gezer and Lachish defeated | Amarna rulers with different names, loyal to Egypt |
| "All the land" taken at once (10:40–42) | Contradicted by 13:1–6, 15:63 and Judges 1 |

## Assessment

Joshua 10 combines an old poetic fragment, a local Benjaminite battle tradition near Gibeon, an etiology of a cave at Makkedah, and a stylised list of southern conquests. Each conquest repeats the same formula ("he left none remaining").

K. Lawson Younger Jr. has shown that such formulas belong to ancient Near Eastern conquest rhetoric and were not meant as literal tallies (conservative position). Critical scholars agree on the rhetoric but conclude that the list as a whole reflects the geography of later Judah. Lachish and Libnah, for example, were key cities in Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE (2 Kgs 18:14; 19:8), and they do not reflect a Late Bronze campaign.
