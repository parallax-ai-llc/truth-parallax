## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The defeat at Ai depends on a city that did not exist when Israel emerged (see chapter 8). The Achan story is a moral and etiological tale explaining a heap of stones in the Valley of Achor. Some details of material culture are realistic but cannot be dated.

## What the Text Claims

- Achan son of Carmi, of the tribe of Judah, takes items devoted to destruction from Jericho (7:1).
- Joshua sends 3,000 men against Ai, "near Beth-aven, east of Bethel." They are routed and 36 are killed (7:2–5).
- God reveals the cause, and Achan is identified by lot, tribe by tribe and family by family (7:10–18).
- He confesses to taking "a beautiful mantle from Shinar," 200 shekels of silver and a gold bar weighing 50 shekels (7:21).
- Achan and his family are stoned and burned in the Valley of Achor, under a great heap of stones that remains "to this day" (7:24–26).

## External Evidence

### Ai and Bethel

Ai is placed "near Beth-aven, east of Bethel" (7:2). Bethel is usually identified with Beitin, and Ai with nearby **et-Tell**, which had no Late Bronze occupation. Hosea uses "Beth-aven" ("house of wickedness") as a scornful nickname for Bethel (Hos 4:15; 5:8; 10:5). This may suggest an 8th-century or later author.

### The Valley of Achor

Achor ("trouble") is often identified with the **el-Buqei'a** basin west of the Dead Sea. Frank Moore Cross and J. T. Milik surveyed Iron II forts there. Hosea 2:15 and Isaiah 65:10 use the valley as a symbol, and the Copper Scroll from Qumran names it. The name plays on Achan, whom 1 Chronicles 2:7 calls "Achar, the troubler (*'oker*) of Israel."

### Material details

- **A mantle from Shinar:** Shinar is Babylonia (Gen 10:10; 11:2). Fine Mesopotamian textiles were traded across the Near East in every period, including the Late Bronze Age, so the detail fits many centuries.
- **Weighed silver and gold bars:** Before coinage (c. 6th century BCE in the Levant), payment was made in weighed silver. Silver hoards of cut pieces (*Hacksilber*) are well known from Late Bronze and Iron Age sites.

## Points of Agreement

- Using the lot to identify a culprit (7:14–18) matches Israelite practice (1 Sam 10:20–21; 14:40–42) and wider ancient Near Eastern divination.
- Stone cairns marking burials or events are common in the Judean wilderness.
- Weighed precious metal as wealth is accurate for pre-coinage societies.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 7 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Ai is a city defended against 3,000 men | Et-Tell was a ruin c. 2400–1200 BCE, then a small village |
| Achan belongs to Judah | Judah's genealogy (1 Chr 2:6–7) is a later construction |
| Collective punishment of the family | A theological teaching about *herem* and the purity of the camp |

## Assessment

The chapter turns a defeat into a lesson. Israel fails because one man broke the ban, so success depends on total obedience. This is the core of Deuteronomistic theology (Deut 7:25–26; 13:17).

The heap of stones "to this day" shows the story's etiological function. A cairn in a valley named "trouble" was explained by a tale of a troubler of Israel. Realistic details such as Babylonian cloth and weighed silver cannot be pinned to any single period.
