## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Jericho's walls did collapse and the city did burn, but around **1550 BCE**. That is a century and a half before the earliest biblical date for the conquest and three centuries before the date most historians would consider. At the time of any Israelite arrival, Jericho was at most a small, unwalled settlement.

## What the Text Claims

- Israel marches around Jericho once a day for six days, with priests carrying the ark and blowing rams' horns, and seven times on the seventh day (6:1–16).
- At the shout, "the wall fell down flat," and the army goes straight in (6:20).
- Everything living is devoted to destruction (*herem*), except Rahab's family (6:21–25).
- The city is burned, and its metal goes to the treasury of the LORD (6:24).
- Joshua curses anyone who rebuilds Jericho: the cost will be his firstborn and his youngest son (6:26).

## External Evidence

### Garstang (1930–36)

John Garstang found a double mudbrick wall that had collapsed and burned. He dated it c. 1400 BCE, which seemed to confirm the biblical date. Kathleen Kenyon later showed that this wall belonged to the **Early Bronze Age**, about a thousand years earlier.

### Kenyon (1952–58)

Kenyon excavated with stratigraphic methods and reached these conclusions:

- The last major fortified city, **Middle Bronze City IV**, was massively destroyed by fire **c. 1550 BCE**. She found collapsed mudbrick at the foot of its stone revetment wall and thick burned debris.
- After a gap, a modest occupation existed in the **14th century BCE**. It left a floor, a juglet and some tombs, but no recognisable wall, and much of it had eroded away.
- In the late 13th century the site was abandoned.

She attributed the 1550 destruction to the Egyptian campaigns that expelled the Hyksos and their allies.

### Wood's re-dating (conservative position)

In 1990 Bryant Wood argued that the City IV destruction should be dated c. 1400 BCE. His arguments drew on Garstang's pottery, Egyptian scarabs from 18th-dynasty rulers found in tombs, and a radiocarbon date. He pointed out that the destruction matches several features of the story:

- Full jars of grain suggest a short siege at harvest.
- A collapsed wall formed a ramp into the city.
- There was a burn layer.

Piotr Bienkowski replied that the pottery is characteristic of the Middle Bronze Age. He also argued that the scarabs date tombs, not the destruction, and that scarabs of earlier pharaohs circulated for centuries.

### Radiocarbon and later excavation

Radiocarbon dates on short-lived charred cereals from the destruction layer, published by Hendrik Bruins and Johannes van der Plicht in 1995, place the destruction in the **mid-16th century BCE**. The Italian–Palestinian expedition led by Lorenzo Nigro has also confirmed the end of the Middle Bronze city and the very limited Late Bronze occupation.

## Points of Agreement

- Jericho was an ancient walled city whose walls collapsed and which was burned. The memory of a ruined fortress at a famous site is real.
- Joshua's curse matches 1 Kings 16:34, where Hiel of Bethel rebuilds Jericho in Ahab's reign at the cost of his sons. Jericho does reappear as an Iron II town.
- The grain stores in the destruction layer suggest it happened in spring, the season the text implies.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 6 | Archaeological record |
|----------|----------------------|
| Walls collapse when Israel arrives | Last city walls destroyed c. 1550 BCE |
| A burned, fortified city c. 1400 or c. 1230 BCE | 14th century: small, unwalled, mostly eroded; 13th century: no occupation |
| A king and army defend the city | Jericho does not appear in the Amarna letters |

## Assessment

Jericho is the classic case of a biblical story meeting archaeological data. Most archaeologists, including Amihai Mazar and Israel Finkelstein, accept Kenyon's results and conclude that the story is an **etiology**. Israelites living near the huge ruined mound explained its destruction through a tradition of miraculous conquest. The procession, the trumpets and the seven days give the account the form of a liturgy, not a siege report.

Wood's re-dating has found little support outside conservative circles. Even if it were accepted, it would only shift the destruction to c. 1400 BCE, which conflicts with the Amarna-age evidence for Egyptian control.
