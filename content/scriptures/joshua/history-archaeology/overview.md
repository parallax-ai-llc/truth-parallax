## Overview

This page does not read Joshua as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the conquest and settlement Joshua describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

Joshua tells how Israel, led by Moses' successor, crossed the Jordan, destroyed the Canaanite cities in a few swift campaigns (chapters 1–12), divided the land among the twelve tribes (chapters 13–21), and renewed its covenant with God at Shechem (chapters 22–24).

Few biblical books have been tested so directly by archaeology, and few have fared as poorly. The two set-piece victories, Jericho and Ai, took place at sites that were unoccupied or nearly so in the Late Bronze Age. Most historians now conclude that early Israel emerged largely from within Canaan during the Iron I period (c. 1200–1000 BCE), not through a sudden invasion. The book's geography, however, is often accurate. Its town and boundary lists are valuable documents, but for the later kingdom of Judah rather than for the age of Joshua.

## Composition and Date

Martin Noth (1943) identified Joshua as part of a **Deuteronomistic History** running from Deuteronomy to 2 Kings. Its theology of total obedience, the destruction (*herem*) of the Canaanites, and worship of one God at one place are those of Deuteronomy. Frank Moore Cross argued for a first edition under King **Josiah** (c. 640–609 BCE), whose northward expansion and religious reform the figure of Joshua seems to mirror. Exilic and post-exilic editors added further layers, including priestly material in the allotment chapters. Thomas Römer and others place much of the shaping in the Babylonian and Persian periods.

The book draws on older materials. These include local hero stories from Benjamin (chapters 2–9), a scrap of early poetry from the "Book of Jashar" (10:12–13), and administrative lists. Many stories end with an **etiology**, explaining a ruin, a heap of stones or a group of people still visible "to this day" (e.g. 4:9; 7:26; 8:28–29; 9:27). Albrecht Alt and Martin Noth argued that such tales were told *because* of the ruins, not as eyewitness memories.

The Greek Septuagint and the Qumran scroll 4QJosh<sup>a</sup> differ from the Hebrew text in places, including where the Mount Ebal altar passage (8:30–35) is placed. This shows the book was still fluid in the Second Temple period.

## The Date of the Conquest

1 Kings 6:1 places the exodus 480 years before Solomon's fourth year, giving c. 1446 BCE and a conquest c. 1406 BCE. Most scholars treat 480 as a schematic figure (12 generations of 40 years). W. F. Albright and his school preferred the **late 13th century**, because several sites (Bethel, Tell Beit Mirsim, Lachish, Hazor) showed destruction layers around that time.

Neither date works well. In the 15th–14th centuries Canaan was an Egyptian province. The **Amarna letters** (c. 1360–1330s BCE) show local kings at Jerusalem, Shechem, Gezer, Lachish and Hazor writing to Pharaoh, with no mention of Israel or a conquest. The later destructions are spread over more than a century (c. 1250–1130 BCE). They fit the general collapse of Late Bronze Age civilisation better than a single campaign.

## The Main Historical Problems

### Jericho

John Garstang (1930–36) believed he had found walls that collapsed c. 1400 BCE. Kathleen Kenyon (1952–58) showed that those walls were Early Bronze Age and that the last fortified city (City IV) was destroyed **c. 1550 BCE**, probably during Egypt's campaigns at the end of the Middle Bronze Age. Only scant 14th-century remains survive, and there was no walled city in the 13th century. Bryant Wood (1990) argued for re-dating the destruction to c. 1400 BCE (conservative position). Radiocarbon dates on charred grain and later Italian–Palestinian excavations have supported Kenyon.

### Ai

Ai is usually identified with **et-Tell**, near Bethel. Excavations by Judith Marquet-Krause (1933–35) and Joseph Callaway (1964–72) found a large Early Bronze city destroyed c. 2400 BCE. Nothing was built there again until a small unwalled village was founded c. 1200 BCE, so there was no city for Joshua to take. Wood and the Associates for Biblical Research propose **Khirbet el-Maqatir** instead (conservative position), but this rests on contested identifications and dating.

### Hazor

Hazor, "the head of all those kingdoms" (11:10), was destroyed by fire in the 13th century BCE. Yigael Yadin and Amnon Ben-Tor attribute this to the Israelites. Sharon Zuckerman (2007) argues for an internal revolt after a period of decline. This is the strongest point of contact between Joshua and archaeology, but it remains contested.

### Other sites

Gibeon (el-Jib), Arad, Heshbon, Jarmuth and Hebron show little or no Late Bronze occupation. Lachish was destroyed twice, c. 1200 and c. 1150–1130 BCE, and neither matches the account well. The book itself admits that Jerusalem, Gezer, Megiddo, Beth-shean and the coast were not taken (13:1–6; 15:63; 16:10; 17:11–13), and Judges 1 says the same.

## Models of Israel's Emergence

- **Conquest (Albright, Wright, Yadin):** a military invasion broadly as Joshua describes, supported by 13th-century destructions. This model has largely been abandoned, because the destructions are not clustered in time and do not match the sites.
- **Peaceful infiltration (Alt, Noth):** semi-nomadic groups gradually settled the sparsely populated highlands.
- **Peasant revolt (Mendenhall 1962; Gottwald 1979):** Canaanite peasants and outcasts (compare the *'Apiru* of the Amarna letters) withdrew from oppressive city-states and formed an egalitarian Yahwistic society.
- **Indigenous emergence (Finkelstein 1988; Dever 2003):** surveys reveal roughly 250–300 new small, unwalled villages in the central highlands during Iron I. Their pottery, building methods and farming techniques (terraces, cisterns, collared-rim jars, four-room houses) continue Canaanite traditions. Finkelstein sees local pastoralists settling down, part of a long cycle. Dever calls the settlers "proto-Israelites," mostly displaced Canaanite farmers. The absence of pig bones in these villages is one of the few distinctive markers.

Most historians allow that a small group carrying traditions of Egypt may have joined this population, but not that there was a unified conquest.

### The Merneptah Stele and the 'Apiru

The **Merneptah Stele** (c. 1208 BCE) lists "Israel" among Pharaoh's defeated enemies in Canaan. The name is marked with the sign for a people, not a city-state. An entity called Israel therefore existed in the land by the late 13th century, but the stele says nothing about how it got there. The ***'Apiru*** of the Amarna letters were a social class of uprooted people, not an ethnic group. The once-popular equation of 'Apiru with Hebrews is now regarded as at best a loose sociological analogy.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Conservative scholarship** (Kitchen 2003; Hoffmeier; Hess; Wood) notes that Joshua says only three cities were burned: Jericho, Ai and Hazor (6:24; 8:28; 11:13). Most conquered towns would therefore leave no destruction layer. K. Lawson Younger Jr. (1990) showed that ancient conquest accounts routinely use hyperbole ("left none remaining"), so Joshua 10–11 need not be read literally. Wood re-dates Jericho, and the Associates for Biblical Research relocate Ai.
- **Critical scholarship** answers that the problem is not only missing burn layers but missing *occupation*. Jericho, Ai, Gibeon, Arad and Heshbon were not functioning cities at the relevant time. The Iron I settlement pattern, meanwhile, shows continuity with Canaan rather than invasion.
- **Mount Ebal:** Adam Zertal interpreted an Iron I structure on Mount Ebal as the altar of Joshua 8:30–35. Others see a watchtower or farmstead. A small lead "curse tablet" from Zertal's spoil heaps, announced in 2022, has been widely disputed by epigraphers (see chapter 8).

## Reliability Assessment

As a record of a swift, unified conquest of Canaan, Joshua is **not historically reliable**. The key sites either were unoccupied or were destroyed at the wrong time. The book itself, like Judges 1, also preserves a different memory of slow, partial settlement.

As a source on the **geography and administration of the later kingdom of Judah**, Joshua is valuable. Its boundary and town lists, its clan names and its knowledge of Transjordan reflect real conditions of the 8th–7th centuries BCE.

As **theology**, the book uses the past to address a later audience. Josiah's Judah, and then the exiles, were told that the land was God's gift and that keeping it depended on loyalty to the covenant.
