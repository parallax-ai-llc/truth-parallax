## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The crossing is told as a liturgical procession that mirrors the Reed Sea miracle, and no independent evidence supports it. Natural blockages of the Jordan near Adam are historically documented. They show that the story's geography is realistic, not that the event took place.

## What the Text Claims

- Israel moves from Shittim to the Jordan (3:1).
- Priests carry the ark ahead, and the people keep a distance of about 2,000 cubits (3:3–4).
- The crossing will prove that God will drive out the "seven nations" of Canaan (3:10).
- When the priests' feet touch the water, the Jordan, which "overflows all its banks throughout the time of harvest," stops. The water stands "in a heap far off, at Adam, the city beside Zarethan," and the people cross on dry ground (3:13–17).

## External Evidence

### Natural damming of the Jordan

The town of Adam is generally identified with **Tell ed-Damiyeh**, near where the Jabbok joins the Jordan, about 30 km north of Jericho. There the river runs between high, unstable marl banks. Medieval and modern sources record landslides that blocked the river:

- An Arabic chronicle reports that in **1267 CE** a collapse of the banks near Damiya stopped the Jordan for several hours.
- During the **1927** Jericho earthquake, landslides near Damiya are reported to have blocked the river for about a day. John Garstang publicised the account in connection with Joshua.

Geophysicist Amos Nur discusses these events and the Jordan Valley's history of earthquakes. Such episodes make the story's geography plausible. They are not independent evidence that Israel crossed this way.

### Seasonal flooding

The note that the Jordan overflows its banks at harvest (3:15) is accurate. Snowmelt from Mount Hermon and late winter rains raise the river in March–April, when barley is harvested in the Jordan Valley.

## Points of Agreement

- Adam, Zarethan and Shittim are real toponyms in the right relationship to one another. Zarethan is often placed at Tell es-Sa'idiyeh or nearby, though this is uncertain.
- The spring high water and the unstable banks near Damiya match known conditions.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 3 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| A miraculous stop of the river at flood | Modeled on the Reed Sea (Exod 14; cf. Josh 4:23); no external witness |
| An organised nation of tribes crosses with the ark | No evidence of a large migration from Transjordan in the Late Bronze or early Iron Age |
| "Seven nations" of Canaan (3:10) | A stereotyped Deuteronomistic list; Girgashites and Hivites are barely attested outside the Bible |

## Assessment

The chapter reads like the script for a procession. There are priests, the ark, measured distances, ritual purification (3:5) and a crowd that follows. Hans-Joachim Kraus and others proposed that the story grew out of an annual festival at the **Gilgal** sanctuary, where the crossing was re-enacted. That proposal remains a hypothesis, but it explains the liturgical tone better than an eyewitness report does.

The deliberate parallel with the exodus presents Joshua as a second Moses and the entry into Canaan as a second founding event. The accurate local details reflect knowledge of the Jordan Valley. They do not confirm the event itself.
