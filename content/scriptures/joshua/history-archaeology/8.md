## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Ai, identified with et-Tell, was an empty ruin from c. 2400 to c. 1200 BCE, so there was no city to capture. The Mount Ebal passage is a "floating" unit that appears at different places in different ancient texts. An Iron I structure on Mount Ebal exists, but whether it was an altar, and any link to Joshua, remain disputed.

## What the Text Claims

- Joshua sets an ambush west of Ai, lures its defenders out, and burns the city (8:1–19).
- All 12,000 inhabitants are killed. Joshua "made it a heap (*tel*) forever, a desolation to this day" (8:24–28).
- The king of Ai is hanged, and a heap of stones is raised over his body at the city gate (8:29).
- Joshua builds an altar of uncut stones on Mount Ebal, writes a copy of the law on stones, and reads the blessings and curses before the people at Ebal and Gerizim (8:30–35).

## External Evidence

### Et-Tell

Judith Marquet-Krause (1933–35) and Joseph Callaway (1964–72) excavated et-Tell. They found a large fortified **Early Bronze Age** city with a temple and palace, destroyed around 2400 BCE. Nothing was built there for over a thousand years. Around 1200 BCE a small, unwalled agricultural village appeared, typical of Iron I highland settlements. It was abandoned in the 11th century.

Callaway, who began as a supporter of the conquest model, concluded that the evidence could not be reconciled with Joshua 8. The name "Ai" means "the ruin," and Alt and Noth read the story as an etiology for the great ruin mound. That reading fits the text's own "heap forever... to this day."

### Proposed alternatives

- **Albright** suggested that the story originally referred to nearby Bethel (Beitin), which he believed was destroyed in the 13th century, and was later transferred to the ruin of Ai.
- **Khirbet el-Maqatir (conservative position):** Bryant Wood, Scott Stripling and the Associates for Biblical Research excavated this site, about 1 km west of et-Tell, from 1995. They claim a small Late Bronze I fortress destroyed by fire c. 1400 BCE, matching the early date for the conquest. Critics note that this depends on relocating Bethel as well. They also note that the Late Bronze remains are limited, and that the site's main phases are much later.
- **The Iron I village:** some scholars have suggested that a memory of conflict over the small Iron I village at et-Tell was inflated into a city conquest. This cannot be tested.

### Mount Ebal

In 1980–89 **Adam Zertal** excavated a structure on the northeastern slope of Mount Ebal (el-Burnat). It was a rectangular stone platform filled with ash, earth and animal bones, mostly from young male sheep, goats and cattle, with a ramp. It dates to Iron I (c. 1250–1150 BCE). Zertal identified it as Joshua's altar. **Aharon Kempinski** countered that it was a watchtower, and others suggested a farmstead. Many archaeologists accept a cultic function without the Joshua link. Ralph Hawkins (2012) argues for a cultic interpretation at length.

### The 2022 "curse tablet"

In March 2022 Scott Stripling and colleagues announced a folded lead tablet, about 2 cm square, found in 2019 by wet-sifting Zertal's spoil heaps. They claimed that tomographic scans revealed a proto-alphabetic curse text containing the divine name "YHW," and published it in *Heritage Science* (2023). Many epigraphers and archaeologists dispute the claim:

- The letters are not visible on published images.
- The tablet's original context is lost.
- The reading requires a very early date for both the script and the divine name.

It cannot be treated as established evidence.

## Points of Agreement

- Ambush tactics (8:2–22) are realistic and are described in many ancient military accounts.
- Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim do flank Shechem, and Deuteronomy 11:29 and 27 already link them to blessing and curse.
- An Iron I cultic installation on Ebal is plausible, though not certain.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 8 | Archaeological and textual record |
|----------|------------------------------------|
| A city of 12,000 people burned | Et-Tell unoccupied c. 2400–1200 BCE; Iron I village unwalled and small |
| King of Ai | No Late Bronze polity at the site |
| Ebal altar right after Ai | The Septuagint places 8:30–35 after 9:2; the Qumran scroll 4QJosh<sup>a</sup> places it at Gilgal, before 5:2 |

## Assessment

The capture of Ai is the clearest example of an **etiological legend** in Joshua. The great ruin near Bethel, whose name means "the ruin," needed an explanation, and the conquest supplied one. Conservative attempts to move Ai to Khirbet el-Maqatir have not convinced most archaeologists.

The Ebal passage was inserted at different points in different manuscripts. This shows that it was a late editorial addition tying Joshua to Deuteronomy 27. Zertal's structure remains an interesting Iron I site, but it cannot confirm the biblical scene, and the curse tablet claim remains unverified.
