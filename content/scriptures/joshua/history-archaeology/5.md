## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter gathers cultic etiologies, including a place named for foreskins, a pun on the name Gilgal, the first Passover in the land and a theophany. Its practices have genuine ancient roots, but its events cannot be verified and are shaped by later priestly and Deuteronomistic concerns.

## What the Text Claims

- The Canaanite kings lose heart when they hear of the crossing (5:1).
- Joshua circumcises the generation born in the wilderness with flint knives at Gibeath-haaraloth, the "hill of foreskins" (5:2–8).
- God says, "Today I have rolled away (*galloti*) the reproach of Egypt," and so the place is called Gilgal (5:9).
- Israel keeps Passover at Gilgal, eats the produce of the land, and the manna stops (5:10–12).
- A "commander of the army of the LORD" appears to Joshua with a drawn sword (5:13–15).

## External Evidence

### Circumcision in the ancient Near East

Circumcision was practised widely in the region. A relief in the 6th-dynasty tomb of Ankhmahor at Saqqara (c. 2300 BCE) depicts the operation in Egypt. Herodotus (2.104) reports that the Egyptians, Phoenicians and "Syrians of Palestine" circumcised. Jeremiah 9:25–26 lists Egypt, Edom, Ammon and Moab among the circumcised. The Philistines, by contrast, are consistently called uncircumcised in the Bible. Circumcision was therefore not unique to Israel. The link with "the reproach of Egypt" (5:9) is a theological interpretation.

### Flint knives

Using flint blades for ritual long after metal was available is well attested (compare Exod 4:25). Ritual conservatism of this kind is common. The Septuagint adds that the flint knives were buried with Joshua at Timnath-serah (LXX Josh 21:42 and 24:31). This shows that the tradition attached to particular objects and places.

## Points of Agreement

- The use of flint in ritual circumcision reflects genuinely archaic practice.
- Passover and unleavened bread as a spring festival tied to the barley harvest fit the agricultural calendar of the Jordan Valley.
- The armed divine figure echoes ancient Near Eastern ideas of gods fighting for their people, as in Assyrian royal inscriptions where Ashur goes before the king.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 5 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Mass circumcision of an entire army just before battle | Militarily implausible (compare Gen 34:25); a theological statement of renewed covenant |
| "Gilgal" from *galal* ("to roll") | A folk etymology; *gilgal* more likely means "stone circle" |
| Manna ceases on the day after Passover | A symbolic transition from wilderness to land, linked to priestly calendar texts (Exod 16:35) |

## Assessment

Joshua 5 is a cluster of explanations for places and rites at Gilgal, written up to show Israel ready for holy war: circumcised, keeping Passover, and led by a heavenly commander.

The commander's instruction to remove sandals on holy ground (5:15) repeats Exodus 3:5. This makes Joshua a second Moses. The episode is unfinished, since the commander delivers no message. Many scholars think a longer tradition was cut short when it was edited into the book.

Historically the chapter is valuable for what it shows about Israelite ritual, not about events in the Late Bronze Age.
