## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The story assumes a walled city with a king at Jericho. Excavations show that Jericho had no such city at any date proposed for the conquest. The tale is best read as a folk narrative explaining why a non-Israelite clan lived among Israel.

## What the Text Claims

- Joshua sends two spies from Shittim to Jericho, where they lodge with Rahab, a prostitute (2:1).
- The king of Jericho demands that she hand them over. She hides them under stalks of flax on her roof (2:2–7).
- She says the people's hearts have melted since the drying of the Red Sea and the defeat of Sihon and Og (2:9–11).
- Her house is "in the wall," and she lets the spies down by a rope (2:15). A scarlet cord is to mark her house for protection (2:18–21).

## External Evidence

### The site of Jericho

Jericho is Tell es-Sultan, beside the spring of 'Ain es-Sultan. It has been excavated by Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger (1907–09), John Garstang (1930–36), Kathleen Kenyon (1952–58) and an Italian–Palestinian expedition (from 1997).

Kenyon showed that the last fortified city, Middle Bronze City IV, was destroyed **c. 1550 BCE**. After that there was only a thin, badly eroded occupation in the 14th century BCE, with no city wall that can be identified. In the late 13th century the site was empty.

That record leaves no room for the city in this story. A city with a king, gates shut at dusk (2:5) and houses built into its wall does not fit the Late Bronze remains.

### Houses on the wall

The German excavators found houses built against the lower city wall on the north side, and Kenyon found similar houses. Both belong to the Middle Bronze city destroyed c. 1550 BCE. Conservative scholars such as Bryant Wood link them to Rahab's house, but only by re-dating that destruction to c. 1400 BCE (see chapter 6).

### Silence in the Amarna letters

The Amarna letters (c. 1360–1330s BCE) include correspondence from Jerusalem, Shechem, Gezer and other nearby rulers. No letter comes from a king of Jericho, and none mentions the city.

## Points of Agreement

- **Flax drying on the roof** (2:6) fits the farming year. Flax was pulled in early spring, the season of the crossing (3:15; 4:19). The Gezer Calendar (10th century BCE) lists a month of flax harvest.
- **Sending spies before an attack** was standard practice in the ancient Near East. The Mari letters (18th century BCE) often report on scouts and informers.
- **Hiding in the hills to the west** (2:16, 22) is realistic, since that terrain is full of caves and gullies.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 2 | Archaeological record |
|----------|----------------------|
| A walled city with a king | No Late Bronze fortifications; at most a small unwalled settlement in the 14th century |
| Houses built into the wall | Known only from the Middle Bronze city destroyed c. 1550 BCE |
| Rahab has heard of Sihon and Og | Heshbon, Sihon's capital, had no Late Bronze occupation (see chapter 12) |

## Assessment

Joshua 2 is a well-crafted story with a clear etiological purpose. Joshua 6:25 says Rahab's family "has lived in Israel to this day," so the tale explains how a Canaanite clan came to live within Israel.

Rahab's confession (2:9–11) is written in Deuteronomistic language (compare Deut 4:39). Her role as an outsider who recognises Israel's God is a theological theme, not a historical record.

The realistic details, such as the flax harvest and the hill country, show that the storyteller knew the Jericho area. They are not evidence for a Late Bronze city.
