## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter admits that much of the land was not conquered, and it describes Transjordan with real Iron Age toponyms, several of them confirmed by the Mesha Stele. Its Philistine pentapolis is anachronistic for a Late Bronze setting, and its tribal borders are idealised.

## What the Text Claims

- Joshua is old, and "very much land remains to be possessed" (13:1).
- This land includes the five Philistine cities ruled by "lords" (*seranim*), the Geshurites, the Avvim, the land of the Gebalites (Byblos) and Lebanon as far as Lebo-hamath (13:2–6).
- Moses' allotments east of the Jordan go to Reuben (13:15–23), Gad (13:24–28) and half-Manasseh (13:29–31), with Medeba, Dibon, Bamoth-baal, Baal-meon, Jahaz, Kiriathaim, Heshbon and Aroer listed.
- Israel killed "Balaam son of Beor, the diviner" (13:22).
- Geshur and Maacah were not driven out (13:13). Levi receives no inheritance (13:14, 33).

## External Evidence

### The Mesha Stele

The Moabite king Mesha's inscription (c. 840 BCE) names many of the same towns: Medeba, Dibon, Baal-meon, Kiriathaim, Ataroth, Nebo, Jahaz, Aroer and Bezer. It states that "the men of Gad had lived in the land of Ataroth from of old" and that Omri's Israel had occupied Medeba. The tribal geography of 13:15–28 reflects real Iron II conditions north of the Arnon, an area contested between Israel and Moab.

### The Deir Alla inscription

At Tell Deir Alla in the Jordan Valley, plaster inscriptions of c. 800 BCE describe a vision of **"Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods."** This is an independent attestation of the Balaam tradition (Num 22–24; Josh 13:22) east of the Jordan. It does not confirm his death at Israel's hands.

### The Philistines and the "land that remains"

The Philistines settled on the southern coast c. 1175 BCE, after the Sea Peoples' wars with Ramesses III. Their five cities and their *seranim* (a non-Semitic title) cannot belong to a 15th-century setting, and they barely fit even a 13th-century one.

The boundaries of "the land that remains," running from Gaza to Byblos and Lebo-hamath, correspond closely to the Egyptian province of Canaan in the late 2nd millennium BCE. Aharoni, de Vaux and Na'aman have noted this. It suggests that the ideal map of the promised land (compare Num 34) inherited an old Egyptian administrative concept.

## Points of Agreement

- Israelite presence in Gilead and the Mishor plateau is confirmed by the Mesha Stele.
- The Balaam tradition is independently attested at Deir Alla.
- Geshur and Maacah remained independent Aramean kingdoms (13:13), consistent with 2 Samuel 3:3 and 10:6.
- Iron I villages with four-room houses and collared-rim jars appear on the Transjordanian plateau (e.g. Tall al-'Umayri).

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 13 | Historical record |
|----------|------------------|
| Philistine pentapolis already existing | Philistines arrive c. 1175 BCE |
| Tribal borders neatly allotted by Moses | The Mesha Stele shows Moab controlling much of this area in the 9th century |
| Heshbon as Sihon's royal city | No Late Bronze settlement at Tell Hesban |

## Assessment

Joshua 13 marks the shift from conquest to allotment and quietly contradicts 11:23. Much of the land was never taken. Its descriptions of Transjordan are among the most historically grounded parts of Joshua, but they reflect the 9th–8th centuries BCE, when Israel and Moab fought over the same towns.

The "land that remains" preserves the outline of Egypt's old Canaanite province. This suggests that Israel's idea of its promised borders grew out of the political map of the Late Bronze Age.
