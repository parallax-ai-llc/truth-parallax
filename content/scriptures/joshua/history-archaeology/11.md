## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Hazor was the greatest city of Canaan, and it was destroyed by a violent fire in the 13th century BCE. This is the closest match between Joshua and archaeology. However, who destroyed it is debated, Judges 4 places a "Jabin king of Hazor" later, and the wider northern conquest is not supported.

## What the Text Claims

- Jabin king of Hazor gathers a northern coalition: the kings of Madon, Shimron and Achshaph, and peoples from the hill country to the coast, with "very many horses and chariots" (11:1–5).
- Joshua defeats them at the waters of Merom, hamstrings their horses and burns their chariots (11:6–9).
- Hazor, "formerly the head of all those kingdoms," is captured and burned. The other cities "that stood on their mounds" are not burned (11:10–13).
- Joshua makes war "a long time" (11:18), wipes out the Anakim except in Gaza, Gath and Ashdod (11:21–22), and "the land had rest from war" (11:23).

## External Evidence

### Hazor in ancient texts

Hazor (Tell el-Qedah) covered about 80 hectares, far larger than any other Canaanite city.

- The **Mari** archives (18th century BCE) mention its king. One is named **Ibni-Addu**, and many scholars, following Abraham Malamat, connect the name with the biblical "Jabin" as a possible dynastic name.
- In the **Amarna letters**, the ruler of Hazor is one of the few Canaanite rulers called "king" (EA 227–228). The king of Tyre accuses him of siding with the *'Apiru* (EA 148).

The phrase "head of all those kingdoms" is therefore a genuine reflection of Hazor's standing in the Bronze Age.

### The destruction

Yigael Yadin (1955–58, 1968) and Amnon Ben-Tor (from 1990) found that the last Canaanite city, the upper city's Stratum XIII and the lower city's Stratum 1A, ended in an intense fire. The ceremonial palace burned so hot that its mudbricks vitrified. Basalt statues of gods and of a king were deliberately decapitated and mutilated. The date is the 13th century BCE, often put c. 1250–1200, although the exact date is debated.

- **Yadin and Ben-Tor** attribute the destruction to the Israelites. Ben-Tor argues that Egyptians, Sea Peoples or rival Canaanites are unlikely. Egypt would not have desecrated Egyptian-style statues, and no rival had the strength.
- **Sharon Zuckerman** (2007) argues that Hazor was already declining and that the destruction resulted from internal social revolt, with the mutilation of statues forming part of "termination" rituals.

### Other sites

Achshaph and Shimron appear in Egyptian topographical lists and the Amarna letters. Madon and the "waters of Merom" are uncertainly located.

## Points of Agreement

- Hazor was pre-eminent and was destroyed by fire, and the text specifically singles it out for burning (11:13).
- The other northern cities "on their mounds" survived. Megiddo, for example, continued as a Canaanite city into the 12th century.
- Chariotry as the Canaanites' main military strength is accurate for the Late Bronze Age.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 11 | Historical and archaeological record |
|----------|---------------------------------------|
| Israel destroys Hazor | Destroyers debated: Israelites (Ben-Tor), internal revolt (Zuckerman), or others |
| Jabin killed and Hazor burned under Joshua | Judges 4–5 has Jabin of Hazor oppressing Israel later; the stories are probably variant traditions |
| Conquest c. 1406 BCE (early chronology) | Hazor flourished through the 14th century; the main destruction is 13th century |
| The whole land taken (11:16–23) | Contradicted by 13:1–6 and Judges 1 |

## Assessment

Hazor is where a conquest tradition and the archaeological record come closest. A mighty Canaanite city fell violently in the late Bronze Age, and later Israelites, who lived in a modest Iron I village on the ruins, remembered its fall. Whether that memory reflects a real Israelite attack or a later claim on a famous ruin cannot be decided from the evidence.

The doubling of Jabin in Judges 4 suggests that the tradition was fluid. Hazor's destruction also fits the general collapse of the Late Bronze city-state system. It therefore cannot be used to confirm the rest of Joshua's northern campaign.
