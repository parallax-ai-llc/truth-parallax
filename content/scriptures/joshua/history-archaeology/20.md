## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (legal institution).** This chapter is law, not narrative history. It shows how an ancient custom of asylum was turned into a system of designated cities. The text combines the laws of Deuteronomy and Numbers and probably reflects a late, partly idealised institution.

## What the Text Claims

- Someone who kills another person unintentionally may flee to a city of refuge. The elders at the gate will protect them from the "avenger of blood" until trial, and they may go home after the death of the high priest (20:1–6).
- The cities are Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem and Kiriath-arba (Hebron) west of the Jordan, and Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead and Golan east of it (20:7–9).

## External Evidence

### Asylum in the ancient world

Sanctuaries offering refuge to fugitives are known across the ancient Mediterranean. Greek temples held the right of *asylia*, and in the Bible fugitives grasp the horns of the altar (1 Kgs 1:50–53; 2:28–34). Moshe Greenberg argued that Israel's refuge cities moved this protection from altars to towns, perhaps as a consequence of cult centralisation, when local altars were abolished.

### Blood vengeance

Kin-based revenge for homicide is found in many tribal societies. Hittite laws allowed compensation in place of revenge for some killings. The biblical system, which distinguishes intentional from unintentional killing and protects the latter, has broad parallels in ancient Near Eastern law. No exact counterpart to designated refuge cities is known.

### The cities

Several of the six cities are attested outside the Bible:

- **Bezer** is named on the Mesha Stele, where Mesha says he rebuilt it "for it was in ruins."
- **Shechem** and **Hebron** were major centres from the Bronze Age.
- **Kedesh** (Tel Kedesh) and **Ramoth-gilead** were important Iron Age towns.
- **Golan** gave its name to the region.

No text or inscription outside the Bible mentions a city functioning as a place of refuge.

## Points of Agreement

- The custom of asylum and the problem of blood vengeance are well attested in the ancient Near East and Mediterranean.
- The six cities are spread evenly across the country, three on each side of the Jordan, which suits their purpose.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 20 | Historical and textual analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Six refuge cities designated by Joshua | No evidence of the institution in practice in any historical narrative |
| Release at the high priest's death (20:6) | A priestly (P) element, as in Numbers 35:25–28 |
| Full text in the Hebrew | The Septuagint lacks most of 20:4–6, suggesting these verses were added later |

## Assessment

Joshua 20 is a harmonising text. It joins the Deuteronomic version of the law, centred on elders, the gate and the place of asylum (Deut 19:1–13), with the priestly version, centred on the congregation and the high priest (Num 35:9–34). The Septuagint's shorter text shows the combination still taking place.

The institution may have roots in older customs of sanctuary at local shrines. As a network of six cities administered nationally, it is best understood as a legal ideal of the late monarchy or the exile.
