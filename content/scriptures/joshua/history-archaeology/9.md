## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The story probably preserves a real memory that the Gibeonite towns were a distinct, non-Israelite enclave with a special status in Benjamin. Its setting in the conquest is doubtful, however, because Gibeon has yielded no Late Bronze town. The ending is an etiology for later temple servants.

## What the Text Claims

- Kings west of the Jordan form a coalition against Israel (9:1–2).
- The people of Gibeon, wearing worn-out clothes and carrying dry bread, pretend to come from a distant land and obtain a treaty sworn by Israel's leaders (9:3–15).
- Three days later the deception is discovered. Gibeon leads a league of four towns: Gibeon, Kephirah, Beeroth and Kiriath-jearim (9:16–17).
- Because of the oath, the Gibeonites are spared, but they become "hewers of wood and drawers of water" for the house of God "to this day" (9:21–27).
- They are called Hivites (9:7).

## External Evidence

### Gibeon (el-Jib)

James Pritchard excavated el-Jib in 1956–62. The identification with Gibeon was confirmed by 31 jar handles inscribed *gb'n*, found mostly in a great rock-cut pool. The finds show a prosperous Iron II town with a wine industry, a huge cylindrical pool with a spiral staircase and a tunnel to the spring. Pritchard found Late Bronze pottery only in some tombs, with **no Late Bronze town** on the tell. Gibeon does not appear in the Amarna letters.

### Kiriath-jearim and the league towns

Kephirah (Khirbet el-Kefireh) and Kiriath-jearim (Deir el-'Azar) are known sites. Recent excavations at Kiriath-jearim led by Israel Finkelstein and Thomas Römer (2017–19) found its major phase in the 8th century BCE. Beeroth is not securely located.

### The Hivites

"Hivites" appear only in the Bible. They have sometimes been linked to the Hurrians or to Anatolian groups, but there is no direct extra-biblical evidence for either.

## Points of Agreement

- **A distinct Gibeonite group.** Other biblical texts agree: Saul's massacre of the Gibeonites and David's restitution (2 Sam 21:1–9) assume they were a protected non-Israelite group bound by an old oath.
- **Treaty oaths.** Swearing by the gods made a treaty binding even if it was obtained by deceit. This fits ancient Near Eastern treaty practice, as in Hittite vassal treaties with their divine witnesses.
- **Geography.** The four towns form a coherent cluster northwest of Jerusalem.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 9 | Archaeological and textual record |
|----------|------------------------------------|
| Gibeon a "great city, like one of the royal cities" (10:2) | No Late Bronze town; prosperity in Iron II |
| Servants "for the house of God... at the place he would choose" (9:27) | Assumes the Jerusalem temple and Deuteronomic centralisation |
| Coalition of all Canaanite kings (9:1–2) | A stereotyped Deuteronomistic framing |

## Assessment

Behind Joshua 9 lies a genuine social fact. A group of towns in Benjamin had a separate identity and a special, lower status, which was later linked to service at the temple. The *Nethinim* of Ezra 2:43–58 may be part of the same tradition. The story explains that status by an ancient trick that Israel was bound to honour.

The archaeology of el-Jib suggests that the setting of the tale reflects Iron Age Gibeon rather than a Late Bronze city. The absence of an LB settlement makes a literal conquest-era treaty unlikely.
