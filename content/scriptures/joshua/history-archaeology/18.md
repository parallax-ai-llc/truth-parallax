## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Shiloh was a real and important Iron I site. It had earlier cultic activity, which makes it a plausible early sanctuary. The written survey of the land and the Benjaminite town list, however, reflect administrative practice and geography of the later Judahite kingdom.

## What the Text Claims

- The whole congregation assembles at Shiloh and sets up the tent of meeting there (18:1).
- Seven tribes have not yet received land. Joshua sends three men from each tribe to survey the land and write a description in seven parts, then casts lots "before the LORD" at Shiloh (18:2–10).
- Benjamin's boundary runs between Judah and Joseph, from the Jordan past Bethel and Beth-horon to Kiriath-jearim, then back along the Valley of Hinnom to Jerusalem (18:11–20).
- Its towns form two groups: a Jordan Valley and Bethel group, and a Gibeon and Jerusalem group (18:21–28).

## External Evidence

### Shiloh

Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun) was excavated by a Danish expedition (1926–32), by Israel Finkelstein (1981–84) and more recently by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling (from 2017).

Finkelstein found three main phases:

- **Middle Bronze:** a fortified site.
- **Late Bronze:** no real settlement, but a deposit of animal bones and votive vessels on the slope, suggesting an isolated cult place.
- **Iron I:** a prominent site with pillared buildings and many collared-rim storage jars, destroyed by fire c. 1050 BCE.

This Iron I Shiloh fits its role in 1 Samuel 1–4 as Israel's central sanctuary before the Philistine wars, and Jeremiah 7:12 remembers its destruction. No structure that can be identified as a tabernacle has been found.

### The Benjaminite list

Alt grouped Benjamin's towns with Judah's districts (see chapter 15). The inclusion of **Bethel** (18:22), which belonged to the northern kingdom, suggests a date after Israel's fall in 722 BCE, when Judah under Josiah extended its control northward (2 Kgs 23:15). Na'aman argues for this date. Mizpah (Tell en-Nasbeh) and Gibeon (el-Jib) were flourishing Iron II towns.

## Points of Agreement

- Shiloh was a significant Iron I highland site with earlier cultic associations.
- Benjamin's boundary follows real topography and agrees with Judah's northern boundary in 15:5–11.
- Gibeon, Ramah, Mizpah and Jerusalem were Benjaminite and Judahite centres in Iron II.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 18 | Historical and archaeological record |
|----------|---------------------------------------|
| The tent of meeting erected at Shiloh | No physical evidence; the elaborate tabernacle is a priestly (P) construct |
| A written survey "in a book" (18:9) | Reflects later scribal administration; Iron I highland literacy was minimal |
| Bethel in Benjamin | Bethel was a northern Israelite royal sanctuary until 722 BCE |

## Assessment

Joshua 18 joins a priestly framework (the congregation, the tent of meeting) to a geographical document. The choice of Shiloh as the place of allotment preserves a genuine memory of its importance before the monarchy, which archaeology confirms.

The surveyors' written description and the town list reflect a world of royal scribes and administrative districts. This points to the 7th century BCE, when Judah's scribes put Israel's tribal map in writing.
