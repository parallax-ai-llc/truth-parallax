## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The story addresses a theological problem: may Israelites outside the land west of the Jordan build their own altar? That problem belongs to the period of cult centralisation and later. Its vocabulary is largely priestly. No altar of this kind has been identified.

## What the Text Claims

- Joshua releases Reuben, Gad and half-Manasseh to return east of the Jordan (22:1–8).
- At Geliloth near the Jordan, they build "an altar of great size" (22:9–10).
- The western tribes assemble at Shiloh for war. Phinehas the priest and ten chiefs accuse them of rebellion, recalling the sin of Peor and Achan (22:11–20). They suggest that Transjordan may be "unclean" land (22:19).
- The eastern tribes reply that the altar is not for sacrifice but a "witness" (*'ed*) to their share in the LORD (22:21–29). The dispute is resolved (22:30–34).

## External Evidence

### Transjordanian Israel

Israelite presence east of the Jordan is historically attested. The Mesha Stele refers to Gad, and Iron I villages on the plateau show material culture similar to that of the western highlands (e.g. Tall al-'Umayri, excavated by Larry Herr and colleagues). The Jordan was a real cultural boundary, however, and Gilead's relationship to the rest of Israel was often uncertain (compare Judg 12:1–6).

### Cult centralisation

The demand that Israel sacrifice only at one chosen place comes from Deuteronomy 12. It was put into practice by Hezekiah and Josiah (2 Kgs 18:4, 22; 23:8–9). Before then, local altars were normal (1 Sam 7:17; 1 Kgs 18:30). Excavated sanctuaries at Arad and elsewhere show that legitimate Yahwistic worship existed outside Jerusalem into the 8th century BCE. The crisis in Joshua 22 presupposes centralisation and so cannot predate the late monarchy.

### Language

The terms "congregation" (*'edah*), "chiefs" (*nesi'im*), "trespass" (*ma'al*) and the role of Phinehas (Num 25) are characteristic of the priestly writings. Many scholars therefore date the chapter to the exile or the Persian period. Its concern with whether Jews outside the land belong to the community fits that period.

## Points of Agreement

- Israelite tribes did live east of the Jordan.
- Tension between eastern and western groups is a recurring theme in Israel's traditions.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 22 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Single legitimate altar already assumed | Centralisation is a late-monarchic reform; earlier worship was decentralised |
| Assembly at Shiloh led by Phinehas | Shiloh's role and Phinehas reflect literary construction |
| Transjordan possibly "unclean" (22:19) | A later ideological view of land and purity |

## Assessment

Joshua 22 is best read as a parable about identity and worship. It answers the question of how communities outside the central land, east of the Jordan or later in the diaspora, can remain part of Israel without breaking the rule of one altar. The answer is a memorial rather than a sacrificial altar.

The narrative has no external confirmation. Its location is vague, since "Geliloth" means "circles" or "districts," and its concerns are those of the late monarchy or after.
