## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (theological speech).** Joshua's farewell is a composed address in Deuteronomistic language. It does not record events. It reveals the concerns of its authors in the late monarchy or the exile, especially fear of losing the land.

## What the Text Claims

- When Joshua is old, he gathers Israel's leaders (23:1–2).
- God has fought for Israel, and the remaining nations will be driven out if Israel stays loyal (23:3–10). "One of you puts to flight a thousand" (23:10).
- Intermarriage with the remaining nations will make them "a snare and a trap... until you perish from this good land" (23:12–13).
- Just as every good promise has been fulfilled, so every threat will come true if Israel serves other gods (23:14–16).

## External Evidence

No extra-biblical source bears on the speech directly. Its value lies in comparison and dating.

### Noth's speeches

Martin Noth noted that the Deuteronomistic History marks its major transitions with speeches by leading figures: Joshua 1 and 23, Samuel in 1 Samuel 12 and Solomon in 1 Kings 8, with narrator commentary in 2 Kings 17. The style is the same throughout. It repeats "the LORD your God," "this good land," and phrases such as "turn aside to the right hand or the left" and "cling to the LORD."

### Treaty language

The speech's structure resembles ancient Near Eastern treaties: a review of the overlord's benefits, demands for exclusive loyalty, and curses for disloyalty. The closest parallels to Deuteronomistic covenant curses are the **Succession Treaty of Esarhaddon** (672 BCE). Copies were found at Nimrud and at Tell Tayinat. The threat of being driven from the land fits a 7th–6th century setting, after Assyrian and Babylonian deportations had made exile a real possibility.

## Points of Agreement

- The speech admits that Canaanite peoples remained in the land (23:4, 7, 12), agreeing with 13:1–6 and Judges 1.
- The treaty pattern of loyalty, blessing and curse is well attested in the ancient Near East.

## Points of Conflict

| Joshua 23 | Historical and literary analysis |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Joshua addresses all Israel's leaders | A composed speech in later Deuteronomistic idiom |
| Remaining nations a threat through intermarriage | A concern prominent in Deuteronomy 7:3 and in the Persian period (Ezra 9–10; Neh 13) |
| Threat of perishing from the land | Presupposes the experience of exile (722 and 586 BCE) |

## Assessment

Joshua 23 explains in advance why Israel will later lose the land. The cause is disloyalty, especially through mixing with other peoples and their gods. It is a sermon to readers who knew of the fall of Samaria and Jerusalem.

The tension with 21:43–45, where everything has been fulfilled, and the near-duplicate assembly in chapter 24 suggest that this speech belongs to a separate editorial layer. Many scholars think it was written to replace or precede the older Shechem tradition.
