## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable for most of the chapter (list and legal pledge), moderate for its institutional details.** The list of signatories appears to be compiled from other lists in Ezra-Nehemiah. The pledges themselves closely mirror Nehemiah's reforms in chapter 13 and reveal a stage of legal interpretation in which Pentateuchal law was being applied, supplemented, and adjusted.

*Note: English versions number this chapter 10:1–39; in the Hebrew text it is 10:2–40.*

## What the Text Claims

- A sealed document is signed by Nehemiah the governor, priests, Levites, and heads of the people (10:1–27).
- The rest of the people swear to walk in God's law (10:28–29). Specific commitments:
  - No marriage with the peoples of the land (10:30);
  - No buying from them on the Sabbath or holy days; leaving the land fallow and remitting debts in the seventh year (10:31);
  - An annual **third of a shekel** for the Temple service (10:32–33);
  - A rota of **wood offerings** by lot (10:34);
  - Firstfruits, firstborn, and tithes brought to the Temple storerooms (10:35–39).

## External Evidence

### Relation to the Pentateuch

The pledges show interpretation of the Torah rather than simple obedience:

- **Temple tax:** Exodus 30:11–16 prescribes a one-time half-shekel at a census. Here it is an annual third of a shekel. Later Jewish practice (e.g. Matthew 17:24; Josephus) returned to an annual half-shekel, possibly harmonizing with Exodus.
- **Wood offering:** no Pentateuchal law requires a wood offering by rota; the pledge infers it from the command to keep the altar fire burning (Lev 6:12–13). The later wood-offering festival appears in Qumran texts (the Temple Scroll) and in Josephus.
- **Sabbath trade:** the Pentateuch forbids work on the Sabbath but does not explicitly forbid buying from foreigners; the pledge extends the law.

David Clines described this as early "exegesis" of the Torah, the beginnings of the interpretive tradition later found in rabbinic law.

### The signatory list

Many names in 10:1–27 also appear as priestly houses in 12:1–21 and as lay families in Ezra 2 / Nehemiah 7. Williamson and others conclude that the list was compiled from those lists rather than preserving original signatures.

### Coins and the Temple economy

The Persian-period silver coinage of Yehud, small fractional denominations struck in the 4th century BCE, shows a local monetary economy capable of supporting regular payments to the Temple, though the coins postdate Nehemiah's time.

## Points of Agreement

- The issues addressed (intermarriage, Sabbath trade, Temple support, Levitical income) are exactly those of Nehemiah 13, part of the Memoir, which is widely accepted as authentic.
- An organized Temple economy with tithes and storerooms is consistent with Elephantine and other Persian-period temple communities.

## Points of Conflict

| Nehemiah 10 | Issue |
|-------------|-------|
| Annual third-shekel tax | Differs from Exodus 30's half-shekel; suggests the Torah was not yet uniformly applied |
| Wood offering commanded "as it is written in the law" (10:34) | No such command exists in the Pentateuch |
| Signatory list | Appears to be compiled from other lists |
| Placement before the reforms of chapter 13 | Chapter 13 shows the same abuses still occurring; the pledge may originally have followed those reforms |

## Assessment

The chapter is not a record of a single verifiable event, but it is strong evidence for the shape of Judean law in the Persian period. The deviations from the Pentateuch suggest that the Torah was authoritative but still being interpreted and supplemented, which supports the view that the formation and application of the Pentateuch was an ongoing process in this era.
