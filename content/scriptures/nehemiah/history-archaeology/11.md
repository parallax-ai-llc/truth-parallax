## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The policy of moving one in ten people into Jerusalem fits the archaeological picture of an underpopulated city. The list of Jerusalem residents is plausible but has a close parallel in 1 Chronicles 9. The list of outlying towns (11:25–36) includes places that lay outside Persian-period Yehud, in territory then controlled by Idumeans, and so reflects an ideal or older map rather than contemporary reality.

## What the Text Claims

- Leaders live in Jerusalem; the rest of the people cast lots to bring one in ten into "the holy city" (11:1–2).
- Lists of Judahites, Benjaminites, priests, Levites, and gatekeepers living in Jerusalem, totalling about 3,000 men (11:3–19).
- The king's representative, Pethahiah, is "at the king's hand in all matters concerning the people" (11:24).
- Judahites live in Kiriath-arba (Hebron), Dibon, Jekabzeel, Beersheba, Ziklag, Lachish, Azekah, and other towns "from Beersheba to the Valley of Hinnom" (11:25–30).
- Benjaminites live in Geba, Michmash, Aija, Bethel, Anathoth, Lod, Ono, and others (11:31–35).

## External Evidence

### Synoecism

Moving people into a city to strengthen it (synoecism) was a known practice in the Greek world and is attested elsewhere in antiquity. The use of lots to choose a tenth is consistent with biblical practice. The archaeological evidence for a very small Jerusalem (see chapter 7) makes such a measure plausible.

### The list of Jerusalem residents

1 Chronicles 9:2–34 contains a list very similar to 11:3–19, with differences in names and numbers. Whether both draw on a common source or one copies the other is debated. The total of around 3,000 men (implying perhaps 10,000 or more people) exceeds archaeological estimates of a few hundred to around 1,500 inhabitants for Persian-period Jerusalem.

### The borders of Yehud

The distribution of **Yehud stamp impressions** and coins indicates that Persian-period Yehud stretched roughly from Bethel/Mizpah in the north to Beth-zur in the south, and from the Jordan Valley to the edge of the Shephelah.

- **Hebron (Kiriath-arba), Beersheba, and Lachish** lay outside this area. In the 4th century BCE the southern hill country and northern Negev belonged to the Idumean region. Hundreds of **Aramaic ostraca** from this area name people with Edomite and Arab names, including names compounded with the Edomite god Qos.
- **Lachish** in the Persian period had a large administrative building (the "Residency"), probably a Persian centre for the region, not a Judean town.

The Judahite town list resembles the tribal allotments in Joshua 15 more than Persian-period reality.

### "At the king's hand"

Pethahiah's role (11:24) suggests a Judean representative at the Persian court or before the satrap, a plausible office though not otherwise attested.

## Points of Agreement

- Jerusalem was underpopulated, as the text assumes.
- Benjaminite towns such as Geba, Michmash, Anathoth, and Bethel lie within Yehud's known borders.
- The structure of the resident list (laymen, priests, Levites, gatekeepers) mirrors other Persian-period registers.

## Points of Conflict

| Nehemiah 11 | Evidence |
|-------------|----------|
| Judahites settled in Hebron, Beersheba, Lachish | These areas were outside Yehud, in Idumean territory |
| About 3,000 men in Jerusalem | Archaeological estimates are much lower |
| Close parallel to 1 Chronicles 9 | Suggests a shared, possibly later, source |

## Assessment

The chapter combines a realistic measure, the repopulation of a nearly empty capital, with lists of uncertain date. The southern town list is best understood as an expression of claim to traditional Judahite territory, perhaps drawing on older tribal geography, rather than an accurate census of Persian-period settlement. Lipschits and other archaeologists see it as reflecting ideology rather than Yehud's actual borders.
