## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High for the political setting.** This chapter introduces Nehemiah's three opponents, and two of them, **Sanballat** and **Geshem**, are confirmed by independent ancient documents. The royal letters, escort, and night inspection of the walls are credible details. The scene in the king's presence is told from Nehemiah's perspective and cannot be checked.

## What the Text Claims

- In Nisan of Artaxerxes' twentieth year, the king notices Nehemiah's sadness; with the queen beside him, he grants leave to rebuild "the city of my fathers' graves" (2:1–8).
- Nehemiah receives letters to the governors of Beyond the River and to **Asaph, keeper of the king's forest** (*pardes*), for timber (2:7–8).
- He travels with army officers and cavalry (2:9).
- **Sanballat the Horonite** and **Tobiah the Ammonite servant** are displeased (2:10).
- Nehemiah inspects the walls by night, from the Valley Gate past the Dragon's Spring to the Dung Gate, the Fountain Gate, and the King's Pool (2:11–15).
- Sanballat, Tobiah, and **Geshem the Arab** mock and accuse him of rebellion (2:19–20).

## External Evidence

### Sanballat

The **Elephantine petition** of 407 BCE (TAD A4.7–A4.8) states that its authors also wrote to **Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria**. His sons acting on his behalf suggests Sanballat was then elderly, which matches a career beginning by 445 BCE. This confirms Nehemiah's dating under **Artaxerxes I** rather than Artaxerxes II. Sanballat is a Babylonian name (*Sin-uballit*, "Sin gives life"), but his sons' names are Yahwistic.

"Horonite" probably refers to Beth-horon, northwest of Jerusalem, though Horonaim in Moab and Hauran have been suggested.

### Geshem

A silver bowl from **Tell el-Maskhuta** in the eastern Nile Delta, published by Isaac Rabinowitz, bears an Aramaic dedication by **"Qaynu son of Gashmu, king of Qedar"** to the goddess Han-Ilat. Dated to the late 5th century BCE, it points to a Gashmu (Geshem) who ruled the Qedarite Arabs, a power controlling the desert trade routes south of Judah. A Lihyanite inscription from Dedan (al-'Ula) in Arabia mentioning a Gashm son of Shahr is often linked to the same man.

### Tobiah

Nehemiah's Tobiah is not named in a contemporary source. A later aristocratic **Tobiad** family centred in Ammonite territory is well attested: Tobias appears in the **Zenon papyri** (c. 259 BCE), and the name "Tobiah" is carved at **'Iraq el-Amir** in Jordan (date debated). "Servant" (*ʿebed*) may be an official title, a royal servant or governor of Ammon.

### Persian terms

*Pardes*, a Persian loanword for an enclosed royal park or estate (the origin of "paradise"), is appropriate. Royal parks and estates are attested across the empire.

## Points of Agreement

- Sanballat governor of Samaria and his period are confirmed.
- Geshem/Gashmu king of Qedar is very likely confirmed.
- Neighbouring provincial leaders (Samaria to the north, Ammon to the east, Arabs to the south) match the political geography of the Persian Levant.
- Royal letters of passage are attested: the Arsames correspondence includes a travel authorization for an official's agent journeying through several provinces.

## Points of Conflict

| Nehemiah 2 | Issue |
|------------|-------|
| Opponents portrayed simply as enemies | They were legitimate local officials, and Tobiah and Sanballat's families were Yahwistic and intermarried with Jerusalem's priests (6:18; 13:4, 28) |
| Night inspection route | Gate names and pools cannot be located with certainty; the topography of the Persian-period city is disputed |
| King's dialogue with Nehemiah | Unverifiable private scene |

## Assessment

This chapter is among the best-supported passages in the historical books. The Elephantine and Maskhuta evidence shows that the author knew the real power players of the mid-5th century. The hostility between them reflects competition among provincial elites for influence in Jerusalem, which Nehemiah frames in religious terms.
