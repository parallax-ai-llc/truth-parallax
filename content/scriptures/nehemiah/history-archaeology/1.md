## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate to high.** The setting, a Judean official at the Persian royal residence in Susa in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes, is credible and consistent with external evidence. Nehemiah himself is not named in any extra-biblical source, but the Memoir that begins here is widely accepted as a genuine Persian-period document. The prayer is a literary composition in Deuteronomistic style.

## What the Text Claims

- "The words of Nehemiah son of Hacaliah": in Kislev of the twentieth year, Nehemiah is in **Susa the citadel** (*bîrâ*) (1:1).
- His brother **Hanani** and men from Judah report that the survivors are in great trouble and Jerusalem's wall is broken down and its gates burned (1:2–3).
- Nehemiah mourns, fasts, and prays, confessing Israel's sins and recalling God's promise to gather the scattered if they return (1:4–10).
- He asks for favour "before this man"; he is "cupbearer to the king" (1:11).

## External Evidence

### Susa

Susa was one of the main Achaemenid capitals. French excavations uncovered the palace and audience hall (apadana) of **Darius I**. An inscription of **Artaxerxes II** at Susa states that the apadana built by Darius burned in the reign of Artaxerxes I, his grandfather, and that he rebuilt it. Susa as a winter residence (Kislev is roughly December) fits Greek reports of the kings' seasonal movements. The term *bîrâ* ("citadel," "fortress") is used in Aramaic documents of the period, including Elephantine, which calls its garrison town "Yeb the fortress."

### Cupbearers at the Persian court

Greek writers (Herodotus, Xenophon) describe the royal cupbearer as a trusted, high-ranking position with access to the king. Foreigners served in many court roles in the Persian Empire, as the Persepolis Fortification tablets show for administrative personnel of diverse origins. A Judean in such a post is plausible, though unverifiable.

### Hanani

A **Hananiah** appears in the Elephantine **Passover Letter** (TAD A4.1, 419 BCE), transmitting instructions to the Jewish garrison. Some scholars have speculated that he is Nehemiah's brother Hanani (1:2; 7:2). The identification is possible but unproven.

### The state of the walls

Jerusalem's walls were destroyed by the Babylonians in 587/586 BCE (2 Kgs 25:10). Nehemiah 1:3 may refer to that old destruction, or, as some scholars think, to a more recent incident, perhaps the halt to wall-building described in Ezra 4:7–23.

## Points of Agreement

- Susa as a royal residence and the date under Artaxerxes I are historically consistent.
- Persian use of courtiers from subject peoples is well attested.
- Communication between Judeans in the heartland of the empire and Jerusalem is confirmed by Elephantine-Jerusalem correspondence later in the century.

## Points of Conflict

| Nehemiah 1 | Issue |
|------------|-------|
| Kislev of "the twentieth year" (1:1); Nisan of the twentieth year (2:1) | Kislev should fall in the 19th year if the regnal year began in Nisan; perhaps a different reckoning or a scribal slip |
| The long prayer (1:5–11) | Composed in the idiom of Deuteronomy and 1 Kings 8; likely literary, not verbatim |
| Nehemiah's position | No external record of Nehemiah or his office |

## Assessment

The opening chapter fixes the Memoir in a realistic Persian court setting. The calendar oddity is minor and has standard explanations. The prayer reflects the theology of the Memoir's author or editor. The strongest support for the historical framework comes later in the book, from the confirmation of Sanballat and Geshem, but nothing in chapter 1 conflicts with known history.
