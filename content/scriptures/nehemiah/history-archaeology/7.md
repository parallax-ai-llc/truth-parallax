## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The statement that Jerusalem was "wide and large, but the people within it were few" (7:4) agrees strikingly with archaeology. The register that follows (7:6–73) duplicates Ezra 2 and is presented as the list of the first return; as such its total of 42,360 is not credible, though it likely preserves real Persian-period family and town records.

## What the Text Claims

- Nehemiah appoints Hanani and Hananiah over Jerusalem and orders the gates kept shut until the sun is hot (7:1–3).
- "The city was wide and large, but the people within it were few, and no houses had been built" (7:4).
- God moves Nehemiah to register the people by genealogy; he finds "the book of the genealogy of those who came up at the first" (7:5).
- The list of returnees follows, by families, towns, priests, Levites, temple servants, and those of uncertain descent (7:6–65).
- The total is 42,360, with 7,337 servants and 245 singers; gifts are recorded in gold drachmas (darics) and silver minas (7:66–72).
- In the seventh month the people are in their towns (7:73).

## External Evidence

### Jerusalem's population

Archaeology strongly supports 7:4:

- Excavations on the City of David ridge and the Temple Mount area show sparse Persian-period remains, often only in fills.
- The Western Hill, densely settled before 586 BCE, has yielded virtually no Persian-period occupation.
- **Israel Finkelstein** estimates the inhabited area at about 2–2.5 hectares and the population at roughly 400–500 people.
- **Oded Lipschits** proposes a larger settlement, perhaps up to around 1,500 people.

Either estimate means a city far below its Iron Age size. Chapter 11's lottery to move one in ten people into Jerusalem follows logically.

### The register and its parallels

The list is nearly identical to **Ezra 2** and also appears in **1 Esdras 5**. Individual numbers differ in many places. The items sum to 31,089 here, against a stated total of 42,360. Williamson argues that the list's original home is Nehemiah 7, where it serves the repopulation effort, and that the compiler of Ezra copied it into Ezra 2. The transition at 7:73 into the seventh-month assembly of chapter 8 parallels Ezra 2:70–3:1, showing literary dependence.

### What kind of document is it?

The list's structure, laymen by family and by town, then cultic personnel, resembles a census or tax register. Many scholars think it records the cumulative population of Yehud over several decades rather than a single caravan in 538 BCE. Charles Carter's demographic estimate for all of Persian-period Yehud (around 13,000–20,000) is lower than the list's total, and Lipschits's estimate (around 30,000) is still below it.

## Points of Agreement

- A small, underpopulated Jerusalem is confirmed archaeologically.
- Security measures (gates shut until full daylight) suit a vulnerable town.
- The names and town lists fit Persian-period Yehud.
- Genealogical registration as a test of membership reflects real community concerns of the period.

## Points of Conflict

| Nehemiah 7 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| 42,360 returned "at the first" | Exceeds archaeological estimates for Yehud's entire population |
| Itemized total 31,089 | Stated total 42,360 |
| List found as an old document | Duplicates Ezra 2 almost verbatim; the direction of borrowing is debated |
| Gold drachmas (darics) | Not minted until Darius I, after the first return |

## Assessment

Verse 4 is one of the clearest points where the biblical text and archaeology coincide: Persian-period Jerusalem was a near-empty shell. The register is valuable as a Persian-period administrative document but not as a record of a single event. Its placement here explains why Nehemiah needed to repopulate the city.
