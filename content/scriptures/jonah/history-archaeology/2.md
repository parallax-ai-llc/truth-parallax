## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry).** The chapter is a thanksgiving psalm set inside the story. It makes no historical claims that can be tested. Its interest for history lies in how the book was composed.

## What the Text Claims

- From inside the fish, Jonah prays a psalm (2:1–9).
- The psalm describes being cast into the deep, surrounded by waters and weeds, going down to the roots of the mountains, and being brought up from "the Pit" (2:3–6).
- It ends with a vow of sacrifice and the cry "Deliverance belongs to YHWH" (2:9).
- YHWH speaks to the fish, which vomits Jonah onto dry land (2:10).

## External Evidence

### A psalm built from the Psalter

Almost every line of the psalm has a close parallel elsewhere in biblical poetry. Examples often noted include:

| Jonah 2 | Parallel |
|---------|----------|
| "I called out of my distress to YHWH, and he answered me" (2:2) | Ps 120:1; cf. Ps 18:6 |
| "All your waves and your billows passed over me" (2:3) | Ps 42:7, word for word |
| "The waters closed in over me" (2:5) | Ps 69:1–2 |
| "When my life was fainting away, I remembered YHWH" (2:7) | Ps 142:3; Ps 143:4–5 |
| "Those who worship vain idols" (2:8) | Ps 31:6 |

The psalm is a song of **thanksgiving for rescue already received**, not a plea for help from within danger. Many scholars find that this sits awkwardly in its setting. Jonah gives thanks before he has been saved from the fish, and the penitence one might expect is absent. For these reasons, it is widely held that the psalm was **inserted** into the story, either by the original author drawing on existing liturgy or by a later editor. Others, including some literary critics, argue that the mismatch is deliberate irony: Jonah is pious in words while still resistant in heart.

### Manuscript evidence

The psalm is present in all ancient witnesses: the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint, and the Minor Prophets scrolls from Qumran and Wadi Murabba'at (Mur 88). If it was an insertion, it was made before the book reached its earliest attested form.

### The fish in folklore

Stories of heroes swallowed by sea monsters, or descending into the underworld and returning, are widespread in ancient folklore. The Greek tale of Heracles rescuing Hesione from a sea monster (in some later versions he is swallowed and cuts his way out) is one parallel, and Joppa itself was the setting of the Andromeda myth. These parallels do not show direct borrowing, but they place the fish episode within a common story-pattern rather than within history.

## Points of Agreement

- The cosmic imagery (the deep, "the roots of the mountains," the bars of the earth, "the Pit") matches ancient Near Eastern ideas of the underworld and primeval waters found in Ugaritic and Mesopotamian texts and elsewhere in the Psalms.
- Vows and thanksgiving sacrifices at the Temple (2:4, 7, 9) reflect real Israelite worship practice.

## Points of Conflict

There are no historical claims to test. The only "conflict" is literary: the psalm's genre and wording point to its being composed from Temple liturgy rather than spoken by a man inside a fish.

## Assessment

Chapter 2 tells us more about the book's composition than about events. The psalm's reliance on the Psalter, including some psalms generally dated late, fits a Persian-period author working with an established body of liturgical poetry. The references to the Temple (2:4, 7) also presuppose Jerusalem worship, which would be odd from a northern prophet of Jeroboam II's kingdom, though not impossible.
