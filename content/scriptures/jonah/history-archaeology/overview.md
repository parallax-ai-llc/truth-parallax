## Overview

This page does not read Jonah as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, Assyrian records, and independent historical sources say about the events Jonah describes, and how far can the book be read as history?**

The book has one firm anchor in the historical tradition. 2 Kings 14:25 names a prophet **Jonah son of Amittai** from Gath-hepher, who predicted that Jeroboam II of Israel (c. 786–746 BCE) would restore Israel's borders. Nothing argues against this being a real 8th-century figure.

The book that bears his name, however, is almost certainly not by him and not from his time. Mainstream scholarship dates it to the **Persian or early Hellenistic period (c. 5th–4th century BCE)**, three centuries or more after the prophet lived and long after Nineveh had been destroyed in 612 BCE. It is best read as a **didactic novella or parable**: a carefully built story about a real prophet's name, used to explore God's mercy toward foreign nations.

## Evidence for a Late Date

### Language

The Hebrew of Jonah has several features typical of Late Biblical Hebrew and of Aramaic influence. Commonly cited examples include *ta'am* in the sense of "royal decree" (3:7, a usage at home in Aramaic and Akkadian), the verb *mnh* (piel) for "appoint" (1:17; 4:6–8), the relative particle *she-* alongside *'asher* (1:7, 12; 4:10), and the rare word *sefinah* for "ship" (1:5). Individually each item could be explained otherwise. Together they point most scholars to the post-exilic period. Conservative scholars argue that northern Israelite dialect, which had closer contact with Aramaic, could explain some of these forms in the 8th century.

### Theology and phrasing

- **"The God of heaven"** (1:9) is a divine title characteristic of the Persian period (Ezra, Nehemiah, Daniel, and the Aramaic papyri from Elephantine).
- **Jonah 4:2** quotes the creedal formula of Exodus 34:6–7 in a form very close to Joel 2:13, and **Jonah 3:9** echoes Joel 2:14 ("Who knows? God may turn and relent"). Most scholars think Jonah draws on Joel, itself usually dated to the Persian period.
- **The psalm in chapter 2** is a mosaic of phrases found in the Psalter (e.g., Pss 18, 42, 69, 120, 142) and is widely regarded as a later insertion or as composed from existing liturgical language.

### Nineveh as a distant memory

The narrator says that Nineveh "**was** an exceedingly great city" (3:3), using a past tense that reads naturally if the city no longer existed. The book's picture of Nineveh is generic and legendary rather than drawn from first-hand knowledge (see below).

### The latest possible date

Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE) refers to "the Twelve Prophets" (Sirach 49:10), which implies that the Minor Prophets collection, including Jonah, existed by then. Jonah is attested among the Minor Prophets manuscripts from Qumran (e.g., 4QXII<sup>a</sup>, 4QXII<sup>g</sup>) and in the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever (8ḤevXIIgr). A composition date between the late 6th and the 3rd century BCE is therefore the critical range, with the 5th–4th century most commonly preferred.

## Historical Problems in the Narrative

### The "king of Nineveh"

Jonah 3:6 calls the ruler "the king of Nineveh." Assyrian monarchs styled themselves **"king of Assyria"** (*šar māt Aššur*), along with titles such as "king of the universe." No Assyrian source uses "king of Nineveh." The Hebrew Bible itself elsewhere speaks of the "king of Assyria" (e.g., 2 Kgs 15:19; 18:13). The title in Jonah reads like the phrasing of a storyteller for whom Nineveh simply stood for Assyria.

### The size of the city

Jonah 3:3 says Nineveh was "a three days' walk" across (or around). At its greatest extent, under Sennacherib (705–681 BCE), Nineveh's walls were about 12 km (7.5 miles) in circumference and enclosed roughly 750 hectares. A person could cross it in a few hours. Some defenders suggest "Nineveh" includes a wider district (Nimrud, Khorsabad, and surrounding towns) or that "three days" describes the time needed for a preaching tour. These are possible readings, but the text's own sense is of a city of fabulous size.

### Nineveh was not yet the capital

In Jeroboam II's time (first half of the 8th century), Nineveh was an important royal city, but the Assyrian capital was **Kalhu (Nimrud)**. Sennacherib made Nineveh the capital around 700 BCE. A later author, who knew Nineveh as the great Assyrian capital of the 7th century, would naturally project that status back.

### No Assyrian record of repentance

Assyrian royal inscriptions, letters, and chronicles say nothing about a king and city putting on sackcloth at the preaching of a foreign prophet or turning to Israel's God. Assyrian state religion continued centered on Ashur and the great gods until the empire fell. Conservative writers sometimes point to the troubled reign of **Ashur-dan III** (773–756 BCE): the Assyrian Eponym Chronicle records revolts, outbreaks of plague (765 and 759 BCE), and the solar eclipse of 15 June 763 BCE. D. J. Wiseman argued that such omens could have made Nineveh receptive to a prophet of doom. This is speculation; no source connects these events with Jonah.

### The fish

The swallowing and survival of a man for three days inside a "great fish" (1:17) is not a claim history or biology can test. It belongs to the genre of the story. Modern stories offered as parallels, such as the 19th-century tale of "James Bartley," have been investigated and shown to be unfounded.

## What Is Historically Grounded

- **Jonah son of Amittai** as an 8th-century northern prophet (2 Kgs 14:25).
- **Joppa** as a Mediterranean port. It is named in Egyptian sources and in Sennacherib's annals (701 BCE).
- **Tarshish** as a real, distant western destination, most often identified with **Tartessos** in southwestern Spain, a region of Phoenician trade. An inscription of Esarhaddon lists "Tarsisi" among the western lands that submitted to him.
- **Nineveh** as a great Assyrian city, eventually destroyed in 612 BCE by the Babylonians and Medes. The **Fall of Nineveh Chronicle** (ABC 3) records the siege by Nabopolassar and Cyaxares. The prophet Nahum celebrates the same fall.
- **Mourning customs** that involve animals (3:7–8) have a parallel in Herodotus (9.24), who says the Persians cut the manes of their horses and pack animals in mourning for the general Masistius. This parallel is Persian, not Assyrian, and fits the Persian-period dating.

## Nebi Yunus: The Tomb Tradition

One of Nineveh's two main mounds is called **Tell Nebi Yunus** ("mound of the prophet Jonah"), after a shrine identified as Jonah's tomb. The tradition is medieval or late antique and has no archaeological support. In July 2014 the militant group ISIS blew up the shrine. ISIS fighters then dug tunnels under the mound to loot it. After Mosul was retaken in 2017, Iraqi archaeologists led by Layla Salih, later joined by a team from Heidelberg University, documented an Assyrian palace in the tunnels. It was a royal arsenal built by Sennacherib and expanded by Esarhaddon (681–669 BCE), with inscriptions, reliefs, and colossal winged figures. These finds add to our knowledge of 7th-century Nineveh. They say nothing about Jonah himself.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Tobit** (3rd–2nd century BCE) refers to a prophecy against Nineveh. Some manuscripts attribute it to Jonah, others to Nahum (Tob 14:4). **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 9.208–214) retells the story as history, as do later Jewish and Christian writers.
- **Conservative scholarship** (e.g., D. J. Wiseman; T. Desmond Alexander) argues for a historical prophet and a historical mission. It points to 2 Kgs 14:25, the Ashur-dan III crisis, and Jesus' references to Jonah (Matt 12:39–41).
- **Critical scholarship** (e.g., Jack Sasson; James Limburg; Phyllis Trible) reads the book as a theological story. It points to the late language, the exaggerated scale (a "great" city, a "great" fish, a "great" wind, and the repentance even of cattle), the irony, and the lack of any Assyrian evidence.

## Reliability Assessment

As a record of 8th-century events, Jonah is **not historically reliable**. Its author took the name of a real prophet and set him in a legendary Nineveh. The titles, scale, and events of that Nineveh do not match Assyrian evidence, and its repentance left no trace in the records.

As a source for **Persian-period Judaism**, the book is valuable. It shows a Jewish author challenging narrow nationalism by imagining Israel's worst enemy receiving God's mercy, at a time when Ezra–Nehemiah emphasized separation from foreigners.

The book is best read as a **didactic novella or parable**. Its genre, irony, and exaggerations are signals of that, not failed attempts at history.
