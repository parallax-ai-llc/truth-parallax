## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (for events); mixed (for setting).** The prophet's name, the port of Joppa, and Tarshish as a far western destination are all historically grounded. The episode itself, with the casting of lots, the sailors' conversion, and the swallowing by a great fish, is narrative art and cannot be treated as an event.

## What the Text Claims

- YHWH sends **Jonah son of Amittai** to preach against Nineveh (1:1–2).
- Jonah goes down to **Joppa**, pays the fare, and boards a ship bound for **Tarshish** (1:3).
- A storm strikes; the sailors cry to their own gods, cast lots, and identify Jonah as the cause (1:4–7).
- Jonah confesses that he fears "YHWH, **the God of heaven**, who made the sea and the dry land" (1:9).
- The sailors throw him overboard, the sea calms, and they sacrifice to YHWH (1:15–16).
- A "great fish" swallows Jonah, who is inside it three days and three nights (1:17).

## External Evidence

### The prophet

**2 Kings 14:25** names "Jonah son of Amittai, the prophet, who was from Gath-hepher," who prophesied the expansion of Israel under **Jeroboam II** (c. 786–746 BCE). Gath-hepher is placed in Lower Galilee (cf. Josh 19:13), traditionally near modern Mashhad. No inscription mentions Jonah, but the Kings reference makes him at least as historical as most minor figures in the Deuteronomistic History.

### Joppa

Joppa (Jaffa) was a long-lived Mediterranean port. It appears in Egyptian sources (including the tale of the *Taking of Joppa* from the New Kingdom) and in **Sennacherib's annals**, which list it among the cities of Sidqa of Ashkelon captured in 701 BCE. Excavations at Tel Yafo have exposed Late Bronze and Iron Age remains. Joppa was also the setting of the Greek myth of Andromeda and the sea monster (Strabo 16.2.28; Pliny, *Natural History* 5.69 and 9.11), a reminder that the port had sea-monster folklore attached to it in antiquity.

### Tarshish

"Ships of Tarshish" in the Bible are long-distance trading vessels, often linked with Tyre (Isa 23; Ezek 27:12). The most common identification is **Tartessos** in southwestern Spain, a metal-rich region where Phoenician trade and settlement are well attested from at least the 9th–8th centuries BCE. An inscription of **Esarhaddon** (681–669 BCE) claims the submission of kings "as far as Tarsisi." Other proposals include Tarsus in Cilicia and a site in Sardinia; the reading of *tršš* on the Phoenician Nora Stone is debated. Whatever the exact location, the text uses Tarshish as the farthest point west, the opposite direction from Nineveh.

## Points of Agreement

- A prophet named Jonah son of Amittai is independently named in Kings.
- Joppa as a departure port and Tarshish as a far western trading destination reflect real Iron Age geography and Phoenician shipping networks.
- Sailors praying to many gods and casting lots to find the guilty party fit ancient Mediterranean practice; lot-casting is widely attested in both Israelite and Near Eastern sources.

## Points of Conflict

| Jonah 1 | Historical consideration |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Written as an episode in the life of an 8th-century prophet | Language and theology point to the 5th–4th century BCE |
| "The God of heaven" (1:9) | A title characteristic of Persian-period texts (Ezra, Nehemiah, Elephantine papyri) |
| Pagan sailors immediately fear and sacrifice to YHWH (1:16) | Literary motif; no historical evidence, and the scene parallels the Ninevites in ch. 3 |
| A man survives three days in a fish (1:17) | Not historically testable; belongs to the story's genre |

## Assessment

Chapter 1 is a tightly constructed story with repeated key words ("go down," "great," "fear") and a pattern of pious pagans contrasted with a disobedient prophet. The author used a real prophet's name and real geography. The aim, though, is theological and ironic rather than biographical. Nothing in the chapter can be confirmed as an event, and the fish shows that the story is not meant as a historical report.
