## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (didactic dialogue).** The chapter is a dialogue between God and the prophet that ends with an unanswered question. It makes almost no historical claims. Its value for history lies in what it shows about the book's date and purpose, and in the irony that the spared city was in fact destroyed.

## What the Text Claims

- Jonah is angry that God spared Nineveh, quoting the formula "a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from punishment" (4:2).
- He sits east of the city; God "appoints" a plant (*qiqayon*), a worm, and a scorching east wind (4:5–8).
- God asks whether he should not care about Nineveh, with "more than 120,000 persons who do not know their right hand from their left, and also many animals" (4:11).

## External Evidence

### The quoted creed

Jonah 4:2 quotes the divine attributes of **Exodus 34:6–7** in a form almost identical to **Joel 2:13**, including the final phrase "relenting from punishment," which is absent from Exodus. Most scholars think Jonah depends on Joel (or both on a shared late liturgical form). Joel is usually dated to the Persian period, which supports a late date for Jonah.

### The plant

The *qiqayon* is often identified with the castor-oil plant (*Ricinus communis*) or a kind of gourd. Its identity was a famous dispute in antiquity: Jerome translated it as "ivy" (*hedera*) in the Vulgate, and Augustine reported in a letter to Jerome that this change caused an uproar in a North African congregation used to "gourd." The episode is a reminder that even ancient readers were reading an unfamiliar botanical term.

### The population figure

The figure of 120,000 people is not unreasonable for a major Assyrian city. Assurnasirpal II's **Banquet Stela** from Kalhu (9th century BCE) claims 69,574 guests at the inauguration of his new capital, and modern estimates for 7th-century Nineveh are of a similar order to the biblical figure. The number in Jonah is most likely a round, symbolic figure (12 × 10,000), but it is not implausible.

### What happened to Nineveh

The book ends with Nineveh spared. History records the opposite outcome. In 612 BCE a coalition of the Babylonian king **Nabopolassar** and the Median king **Cyaxares** (Umakishtar) besieged and sacked Nineveh. The **Fall of Nineveh Chronicle** (ABC 3) describes the siege and the city's plundering, and the book of **Nahum** celebrates its destruction. A Persian-period author and audience knew this. The story's open ending may therefore have carried added irony: God's mercy was real, but it did not make Nineveh permanent.

### The tomb tradition

A shrine on the mound of **Nebi Yunus** at Nineveh was venerated as the tomb of Jonah by Muslims and Christians from the medieval period. There is no archaeological basis for the identification. ISIS destroyed the shrine in July 2014. Tunnels the group dug into the mound later allowed Iraqi archaeologists to document parts of an Assyrian royal palace and arsenal associated with Sennacherib and Esarhaddon. These are significant finds for Assyrian history, not evidence about Jonah.

## Points of Agreement

- Nineveh was a large city with a substantial population and herds.
- Scorching east winds (the *sharav* or *khamsin*) are a real feature of the region's climate.

## Points of Conflict

| Jonah 4 | Historical consideration |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Jonah speaks as an 8th-century prophet | His creed quotes a formula found in its closest form in Joel, a Persian-period text |
| Nineveh is spared by God's mercy | Nineveh was destroyed in 612 BCE |

## Assessment

Chapter 4 confirms that Jonah is a didactic story. Its climax is a theological question, not a historical event. The chapter's dependence on Joel's wording and its focus on God's mercy toward foreigners fit the debates of the Persian period. The author may have had Nineveh's later destruction in mind, which would deepen the story's irony.
