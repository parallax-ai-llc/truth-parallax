## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate for the royal line; uncertain for the post-exilic generations.** The list of David's sons and of the kings of Judah follows Samuel and Kings. The post-exilic descendants (3:17–24) have no parallel and are only partly corroborated. Historically, this chapter is the single most important clue to the date of Chronicles.

## What the Text Claims

- Six sons born to David at Hebron, where he reigned seven and a half years (3:1–4), following 2 Sam 3:2–5.
- Thirteen sons born in Jerusalem, four of them by "Bath-shua daughter of Ammiel" (3:5–9), following 2 Sam 5:14–16.
- The kings of Judah from Solomon to Zedekiah (3:10–16).
- The descendants of the exiled king **Jeconiah** (Jehoiachin): Shealtiel, Pedaiah, **Zerubbabel**, and his children, including a daughter **Shelomith**, followed by several further generations (3:17–24).

## External Evidence

### Jehoiachin in Babylon

The **Jehoiachin ration tablets** from Nebuchadnezzar's palace in Babylon (published by E. F. Weidner, 1939) list oil rations for "Ya'ukinu, king of the land of Yahudu" and for his **five sons**. This confirms that the exiled king had sons in Babylon, which agrees with the seven sons in 3:17–18.

### Zerubbabel and his family

Zerubbabel, governor of Yehud c. 520 BCE, is well known from Haggai, Zechariah, and Ezra. A seal impression from a Persian-period hoard published by Nahman Avigad reads "belonging to **Shelomith**, maidservant (*'mh*) of Elnathan the governor." Eric Meyers proposed that she is the Shelomith of 3:19, the daughter of Zerubbabel, married into the governor's administration. The identification is attractive but unproven. The hoard is unprovenanced, which adds caution.

### The date of the list

The genealogy continues after Zerubbabel through Hananiah, Shecaniah, Shemaiah, Neariah, and Elioenai to Elioenai's seven sons (3:21–24). The Hebrew of 3:21 is obscure, and the Greek and Latin versions read it as a direct line of descent, which gives as many as eleven generations after Jehoiachin. Even on the shorter Hebrew reading, the list comes down to roughly 400 BCE. On the longer reading, it reaches well into the 4th century. This is a key reason scholars date the book c. 400–300 BCE.

## Points of Agreement

- The kings from Solomon to Zedekiah match Kings.
- Jehoiachin's sons in Babylon are confirmed by cuneiform records.
- Zerubbabel and Hattush (3:22; Ezra 8:2) are attested in other post-exilic texts.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 3 | Other sources |
|----------------|---------------|
| Zerubbabel is the son of **Pedaiah** (3:19) | Son of **Shealtiel** (Ezra 3:2; Hag 1:1) |
| Bathsheba is "Bath-shua daughter of Ammiel" (3:5) | "Bathsheba daughter of Eliam" (2 Sam 11:3) |
| Shallum is a son of Josiah (3:15) | Shallum is the throne name of Jehoahaz (Jer 22:11) |
| Zedekiah is listed as a son of Jehoiakim (3:16) | Zedekiah is Josiah's son and Jehoiakim's brother (2 Kgs 24:17–18) |

The Pedaiah/Shealtiel discrepancy may reflect a levirate marriage or two traditions. Neither can be verified.

## Assessment

Chapter 3 draws heavily on Samuel and Kings and is broadly consistent with what is known of the Judahite royal house. The mention of Bathsheba under another name, with no reference to her story, illustrates the Chronicler's reticence about David's sins. The post-exilic extension is the most historically useful part: it shows the Davidic family surviving as a recognised lineage into the Persian period, and it dates the book itself.
