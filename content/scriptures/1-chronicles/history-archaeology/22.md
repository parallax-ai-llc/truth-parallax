## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter has no parallel in Samuel or Kings. David's stockpile of 100,000 talents of gold and 1,000,000 talents of silver is fantastical. The idea that David prepared everything for the Temple is the Chronicler's own, and it serves to make David the true founder of Jerusalem's worship.

## What the Text Claims

- David declares the threshing floor "the house of YHWH God" and the site of the altar (22:1).
- He conscripts **resident aliens** (*gerim*) as stonecutters and gathers iron, bronze "beyond weighing," and cedar from Sidon and Tyre (22:2–5).
- David was forbidden to build the Temple because "you have **shed much blood**" (22:8).
- His son will be "a man of **rest**" (*menuḥah*); his name **Solomon** (*Shelomoh*) means God will give "peace" (*shalom*) in his days (22:9).
- David has prepared **100,000 talents of gold** and **1,000,000 talents of silver** (22:14).
- David charges Solomon and the leaders of Israel to build (22:6–19).

## External Evidence

### The wealth

A talent weighed about 30–34 kg. 100,000 talents of gold is over 3,000 tonnes, and a million talents of silver over 30,000 tonnes. For comparison, Sennacherib's annals record that Hezekiah paid **30 talents of gold** and **800 talents of silver** as tribute in 701 BCE (2 Kgs 18:14 gives 300 talents of silver), and that was the price of saving his kingdom. The Chronicler's figures exceed the known treasuries of the great empires. They express the Temple's surpassing worth, not an inventory.

### Temple-building accounts

Ancient Near Eastern texts about building temples share a pattern: divine command or approval, gathering of rare materials from distant lands, mobilisation of labour, dedication, and blessing. Victor Hurowitz showed how the biblical Temple accounts follow this pattern. The **Gudea Cylinders** of Lagash (c. 2100 BCE) are a classic example: Gudea receives divine instruction and brings cedar and stone from far away. The Chronicler's account of David's preparations fits this genre.

### Conscripted foreigners

Using non-citizens for forced labour (22:2) mirrors 1 Kings 9:20–22 and 2 Chronicles 2:17–18. Corvée labour was standard throughout the ancient Near East. Here it is projected from Solomon back onto David.

## Points of Agreement

- Phoenician cedar for royal building projects is historically plausible (cf. 1 Kgs 5).
- The general shape of the account matches ancient temple-building narratives.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 22 | Kings and Samuel |
|-----------------|------------------|
| David could not build because he **shed blood** (22:8) | David was too busy with **wars** until God gave rest (1 Kgs 5:3) |
| David gathers all materials and labour | Solomon arranges materials with Hiram (1 Kgs 5) |
| Solomon is designated as a "man of rest" before his birth | Solomon's birth follows the Bathsheba affair (2 Sam 12:24) |

## Assessment

Chapter 22 is one of the Chronicler's major contributions to the David story. He solves a theological puzzle, why the greatest king did not build the Temple, by making David its architect in every sense except construction. The play on Solomon's name and the contrast between a warrior king and a king of peace are literary. The numbers belong with the inflated armies elsewhere in the book. The chapter's historical value is for the Chronicler's ideology: Temple and dynasty belong together, and both originate with David.
