## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed (secondary account).** The chapter's eight verses are best known for what they leave out. The Chronicler joins the beginning of 2 Samuel 11 directly to 2 Samuel 12:26–31, omitting David's adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah. He then harmonises the conflicting Goliath traditions.

## What the Text Claims

- "In the spring, the time when kings go out to battle," Joab ravages Ammon and besieges Rabbah, "but David remained at Jerusalem" (20:1).
- Joab strikes Rabbah; David takes the crown of their king, weighing a talent of gold, and sets it on his head (20:2).
- The people of the city are put to work, or killed, with saws, iron picks, and axes (20:3).
- Three battles with the Philistines at Gezer and Gath: Sibbecai kills Sippai of the giants; **Elhanan kills Lahmi, the brother of Goliath the Gittite**; a man with six fingers and six toes on each hand and foot is killed by Jonathan, David's nephew (20:4–8).

## External Evidence

### Rabbah of the Ammonites

Rabbah is the Amman Citadel. Its excavated remains are mostly later than the 10th century, and the Ammonite kingdom is attested from the 9th century onward. No source outside the Bible records a Judahite or Israelite conquest.

### The crown

A crown weighing a talent (about 30 kg) is too heavy to wear. Many commentators read the Hebrew *malkam* as **Milcom**, the Ammonite god, rather than "their king," so the crown belonged to a cult statue. The Greek text and 1 Kings 11:5 support the reading. Iron Age statues of Ammonite rulers or deities wearing the tall *atef*-style crown have been found around Amman.

### Gath and the "giants"

Philistine Gath (**Tell es-Safi**) was one of the largest cities in the Levant in the 10th–9th centuries BCE, as Aren Maeir's excavations have shown. It was destroyed around 830 BCE, probably by Hazael. Stories of Philistine champions from Gath fit the period before that destruction. Polydactyly (20:6) is a real congenital condition. The term "descendants of the giants" (*rapha*) connects these warriors to the legendary Rephaim and is not a physical description.

## Points of Agreement

- Verses 1–3 follow 2 Samuel 11:1 and 12:26–31, and verses 4–8 follow 2 Samuel 21:18–22.
- The prominence of Gath fits the archaeology of the 10th–9th centuries.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 20 | 2 Samuel |
|-----------------|----------|
| David remains in Jerusalem (20:1), then Rabbah falls (20:1b–3) | Between these events: Bathsheba, Uriah's murder, Nathan's rebuke, the death of the child, Solomon's birth (11:2–12:25) |
| Joab captures Rabbah alone | Joab invites David to come and take the city in his own name (12:27–29) |
| **Elhanan kills Lahmi, the brother of Goliath** (20:5) | **Elhanan kills Goliath the Gittite** (21:19) |
| Rephaim battles at Gezer | Battles at Gob (21:18) |

The Hebrew of 2 Samuel 21:19 says Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim the Bethlehemite killed Goliath. Chronicles makes the victim Goliath's brother, Lahmi, a name probably created from "the Bethlehemite" (*bêt hallaḥmî*). This removes the contradiction with 1 Samuel 17, where David kills Goliath. Most scholars think the Elhanan tradition is older and that the feat was later transferred to David.

## Assessment

Chapter 20 shows the Chronicler's editorial method at its clearest. Leaving David "in Jerusalem" and moving straight to Rabbah's fall, he passes over two chapters of Samuel in a few words. The omission is deliberate. It is consistent with his removal of all of David's family scandals, Amnon, Absalom, and Adonijah. The Goliath harmonisation shows him smoothing contradictions in his sources. Neither change reflects independent historical information.
