## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter has no parallel in Samuel or Kings. It presents an orderly public succession and a divinely written Temple plan. Both are theological constructions that replace the court intrigues of 1 Kings 1–2 and assign the Temple's design to David.

## What the Text Claims

- David assembles all the leaders of Israel in Jerusalem (28:1).
- God chose Judah, David's house, and from David's many sons **Solomon** "to sit on the throne of the **kingdom of YHWH** over Israel" (28:4–5).
- David was forbidden to build because he was "a man of war and had shed blood" (28:3).
- David gives Solomon the **plan** (*tabnit*) of the Temple courts, chambers, and treasuries, the divisions of priests and Levites, and the weight of gold and silver for each vessel (28:11–18).
- This includes the golden "**chariot** of the cherubim" that cover the Ark (28:18).
- "All this he made clear to me in **writing from the hand of YHWH**" (28:19).

## External Evidence

### The divinely revealed plan

The idea that a temple's design comes from the deity is widespread in the ancient Near East. In the **Gudea Cylinders** (c. 2100 BCE), the god Ningirsu reveals the plan of his temple to Gudea of Lagash in a dream, and a goddess shows him a tablet with the plan. The closest biblical parallel is Exodus 25:9, 40, where God shows Moses the *tabnit* of the tabernacle. The Chronicler models David on Moses: each receives a divine plan for the sanctuary and passes it on to a successor (Joshua or Solomon) who completes the work.

### The Temple layout

The description implies a Temple with courts, surrounding chambers, and treasuries, more like the Second Temple complex than the simple plan of 1 Kings 6–7. The basic tripartite form of Solomon's Temple has good Iron Age parallels in the temples at **'Ain Dara** in Syria and **Tell Tayinat** in Turkey. But nothing in this chapter is architecturally specific enough to test.

### The "chariot" of the cherubim

Calling the cherubim a "chariot" (*merkabah*, 28:18) is unique in the Hebrew Bible. It reflects the influence of Ezekiel's vision of the divine throne-chariot (Ezek 1; 10). The term became central to later Jewish *merkavah* mysticism. Its use here points to a post-Ezekiel date.

## Points of Agreement

- Solomon did succeed David, as Kings also reports.
- Divine revelation of temple plans is a genuine ancient Near Eastern motif.
- The tripartite form of Solomon's Temple has parallels in Iron Age Syria.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 28 | 1 Kings 1–2 |
|-----------------|-------------|
| Solomon chosen by God before a public assembly | Solomon placed on the throne through a court intrigue led by Nathan and Bathsheba, while David is bedridden |
| No rival claimant | Adonijah, the eldest surviving son, proclaims himself king with Joab and Abiathar |
| David gives orderly instructions on the Temple | David's last instructions include settling scores with Joab and Shimei (1 Kgs 2:5–9) |
| The throne is "the kingdom of YHWH" | The throne is David's |

## Assessment

Chapter 28 is a public, idealised transfer of power. Where 1 Kings 1–2 depicts a contested succession with violence afterwards, the Chronicler gives a solemn assembly and a divinely authorised son. The Temple plan "from the hand of YHWH" places Jerusalem's sanctuary on the same level of authority as the Mosaic tabernacle. These are statements of the Chronicler's theology, important evidence for the status of the Temple in his community but not for events in the 10th century.
