## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The northern genealogies are brief, uneven, and partly corrupt, and their military totals are inflated. Yet the Manasseh clan names match 8th-century administrative documents from Samaria, which shows that some of this material rests on old data.

## What the Text Claims

- **Issachar** had 87,000 warriors, with 22,600 counted "in the days of David" (7:1–5).
- **Benjamin** had over 59,000 warriors in three clans (7:6–12). (Benjamin receives a second, different genealogy in chapter 8.)
- **Naphtali** receives a single verse (7:13). Zebulun and Dan are missing entirely.
- **Manasseh**: clans including Asriel, Machir, Gilead, Zelophehad, Abiezer, Shemida, Shechem, and Likhi (7:14–19).
- **Ephraim**: Ephraim's sons are killed by the men of Gath while raiding their cattle; Ephraim mourns and fathers Beriah; his daughter **Sheerah** builds Lower and Upper Beth-horon and Uzzen-sheerah; the line runs to **Joshua** son of Nun (7:20–29).
- **Asher**, with 26,000 warriors (7:30–40).

## External Evidence

### The Samaria ostraca

The **Samaria ostraca**, about 100 inked potsherds found at Samaria in 1910 and dated to the early 8th century BCE, record deliveries of wine and oil to the royal capital. Many name the district of origin, and several of these districts carry clan names known from the Manasseh genealogies: **Abiezer, Helek, Asriel, Shechem, Shemida**, and the daughters of Zelophehad **Noah** and **Hoglah** (cf. Num 26:29–33; Josh 17:2–3). The ostraca show that these were real territorial units in the northern kingdom. The Chronicler's list of Manasseh (7:14–19) draws on this old tradition, probably through Numbers and Joshua.

### Military rolls

The phrase "their number in the days of David" (7:2) and the totals for each tribe resemble the census lists of Numbers 1 and 26. George Mendenhall proposed that the word *'elef* ("thousand") originally meant a clan unit or military contingent, which would yield much smaller figures. The idea is possible for early lists, but the Chronicler himself plainly understood the numbers as thousands.

### Beth-horon

Lower and Upper Beth-horon guard the main ascent from the coastal plain to the Benjamin plateau. A woman founding towns (7:24) is unusual and may preserve a genuine local tradition. The sites are identified with Beit Ur et-Tahta and Beit Ur el-Foqa.

## Points of Agreement

- Manasseh clan names correspond to districts in the 8th-century Samaria ostraca.
- The toponyms (Beth-horon, Bethel, Shechem, Gezer, Megiddo, Taanach, Dor; 7:24, 28–29) are real and correctly placed in the central hill country and northern valleys.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 7 | Problem |
|----------------|---------|
| Ephraim himself lives in Canaan and is raided by Gath (7:21–23) | Genesis and Exodus place Ephraim in Egypt; this seems to be a separate local tradition |
| Ten generations from Ephraim to Joshua (7:20–27) | Moses and Aaron are only the fourth generation from Levi (Exod 6:16–20) |
| Benjamin's genealogy here differs from chapter 8 and Genesis 46:21 | Competing traditions combined without harmonisation |
| Dan and Zebulun absent | Possibly lost through textual corruption; Dan may hide behind "Hushim" (7:12; cf. Gen 46:23) |

## Assessment

The Chronicler had less material for the northern tribes than for Judah and Levi, and what he had was fragmentary. That he included it at all reflects his conviction that the north still belonged to "all Israel," a position with contemporary relevance in the age of the Samaritans. The Manasseh clan list is a good example of an old tradition surviving into a late text. The Ephraim story is a puzzling relic that cuts across the main biblical narrative of Egypt and the exodus.
