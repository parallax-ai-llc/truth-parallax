## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low, with one important dating clue.** The chapter's offerings are inflated, its account of Solomon's accession contradicts 1 Kings 1–2, and its prayer reflects late liturgical language. The mention of **darics**, a Persian coin introduced five centuries after David, is one of the clearest anachronisms in the book and helps date it.

## What the Text Claims

- David gives from his personal treasure **3,000 talents of gold of Ophir** and 7,000 talents of refined silver (29:1–5).
- The leaders give **5,000 talents and 10,000 darics** (*'adarkonim*) of gold, 10,000 talents of silver, 18,000 of bronze, and 100,000 of iron, as well as precious stones (29:6–8).
- David blesses God in a prayer: "All things come from you, and of your own have we given you... we are strangers before you and sojourners" (29:10–19).
- Solomon is made king "**a second time**" and Zadok anointed priest (29:22).
- "**All the sons of King David** pledged their allegiance to King Solomon" (29:24).
- David reigned 40 years: seven at Hebron and 33 in Jerusalem. He died "in a good old age, full of days, riches, and honour" (29:26–28).
- His acts are written in the records of **Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet, and Gad the seer** (29:29–30).

## External Evidence

### Darics

The daric (Hebrew *'adarkon*, also *darkemon* in Ezra 2:69 and Neh 7:70–72) was the gold coin of the Persian Empire. It was introduced under **Darius I** (522–486 BCE) and was probably named after him. Coinage of any kind did not exist anywhere in the world in the 10th century BCE; the first coins appeared in Lydia in the 7th century. The Chronicler expressed the value of the gift in the currency familiar to his readers. The usage implies that darics were in common circulation, which points to a date well into the Persian period. Some have derived *'adarkon* from the Greek *drachma*, which would also be late.

### Gold of Ophir

An ostracon from **Tell Qasile** (near Tel Aviv), usually dated to the 8th or 7th century BCE, reads "gold of Ophir to Beth-horon, 30 shekels." It shows that "gold of Ophir" was a recognised category of fine gold in Iron Age Israel. The location of Ophir, in Arabia, East Africa, or India, remains unknown (cf. 1 Kgs 9:28; 10:11).

### The quantities

The gold alone (over 8,000 talents) would weigh some 250 tonnes. As noted in chapter 22, Hezekiah's tribute to Sennacherib in 701 BCE was 30 talents of gold. The totals are symbolic.

### The prayer

David's prayer contains late features. Its language of God's universal kingship, "yours is the kingdom" (29:11), its confession that Israel are "strangers and sojourners" (29:15), and its use of the word *birah* for the Temple (29:1, 19), a term used for the Persian citadel at Susa and the Jerusalem fortress in Nehemiah (Neh 2:8; 7:2), all fit the Persian period.

## Points of Agreement

- David's reign of 40 years, seven at Hebron and 33 in Jerusalem, matches 2 Samuel 5:4–5 and 1 Kings 2:11.
- Solomon succeeded David, and Zadok became chief priest (1 Kgs 2:35).
- "Gold of Ophir" is an attested Iron Age term.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 29 | Other evidence |
|-----------------|----------------|
| 10,000 darics given in David's time (29:7) | Darics were introduced under Darius I, c. 500 BCE |
| "All the sons of King David" support Solomon (29:24) | Adonijah tries to seize the throne and is later executed (1 Kgs 1–2) |
| Solomon made king "a second time" (29:22) | The first time (23:1) and second time are the Chronicler's own scheme; Kings records one hurried anointing |
| David's records written by Samuel, Nathan, and Gad | Samuel died before David became king (1 Sam 25:1); the citation refers to prophetic books, probably Samuel–Kings |

## Assessment

The close of 1 Chronicles presents David as the ideal founder: generous, pious, and succeeded peacefully by the son God chose. That portrait contrasts sharply with the troubled old age of David in 1 Kings 1. The darics are a small detail with large consequences. They show that the Chronicler wrote no earlier than the Persian period and assumed his readers knew Persian coinage. His source citation (29:29) presents Samuel–Kings as prophetic records. The chapter is not a source for the 10th century, but it shows clearly the values and institutions of the Persian-period Jerusalem community.
