## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low as Davidic history.** The chapter describes the gatekeepers, treasurers, and Levitical officials of a Temple that, in the story, had not yet been built. Its organisation reflects the Second Temple. A few details, such as treasuries holding dedicated war spoils, fit ancient temple practice in general.

## What the Text Claims

- The divisions of gatekeepers from the Korahites and Merarites, including **Obed-edom** and his 62 descendants (26:1–11).
- Gates are assigned by lot to the east, north, south, and west, with storehouses and the **parbar** on the west (26:12–19).
- Levites oversee the treasuries of the house of God and of dedicated gifts, which include spoils dedicated by **Samuel, Saul, Abner, and Joab** (26:20–28).
- Chenaniah and his sons serve as officers and judges over Israel (26:29).
- Hebronites are put in charge of affairs west and east of the Jordan; in the **fortieth year** of David's reign, capable men were found at **Jazer** in Gilead (26:30–32).

## External Evidence

### Obed-edom

In 2 Samuel 6:10–12 (and 1 Chr 13:13), Obed-edom is "the Gittite," a man of Philistine Gath with whom the Ark was left. In Chronicles he becomes a Levitical gatekeeper and musician (15:18, 21, 24; 16:38; 26:4–8). This transformation is best explained by the Chronicler's rule that only Levites could handle the Ark. A gatekeeping family claiming him as an ancestor may also lie behind the tradition.

### Gatekeepers in the post-exilic texts

The gatekeepers are numbered differently in each source: 139 in Ezra 2:42, 172 in Nehemiah 11:19, 212 in 1 Chronicles 9:22, and 93 heads of families here. In Ezra–Nehemiah they are listed separately from the Levites; in Chronicles they are Levites. This reflects the integration of these groups into the Levitical order in the Persian period.

### Temple treasuries

Temples throughout the ancient Near East served as treasuries for booty and votive offerings. Assyrian kings recorded dedicating spoils to their gods, and in the Persian period the Jerusalem Temple held both cultic and public funds (cf. Ezra 8:24–34; Neh 13:4–13; 2 Macc 3). The practice described is historically plausible, but the specific dedications by Samuel, Saul, Abner, and Joab cannot be verified.

### Parbar

The rare word *parbar* (26:18) and *parwarim* (2 Kgs 23:11) have often been explained as Persian loanwords, perhaps meaning a colonnade or open court. The etymology is uncertain. If Persian, its use in 2 Kings would be a late gloss.

## Points of Agreement

- Gatekeepers and treasurers were real Temple functionaries in the Persian period.
- Temples as treasuries for votive spoils is a widespread ancient practice.
- Jazer is a real town in Transjordan (mentioned in Num 21:32 and Isa 16:8–9).

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 26 | Other evidence |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Obed-edom is a Levite | Obed-edom is a Gittite, from Philistine Gath (2 Sam 6:10) |
| Gatekeepers are Levites | Ezra 2:42 lists them separately from the Levites |
| Gates and storehouses of the Temple assigned in David's time | The Temple did not yet exist |
| David's administration covers both sides of the Jordan | Not supported by archaeology for the 10th century |

## Assessment

Chapter 26 completes the Chronicler's survey of the Temple personnel. It shows families of gatekeepers and treasurers seeking ancestry in the Davidic age, much as the priests and singers do in chapters 24–25. The note about the fortieth year of David's reign is the only date in chapters 23–27 and gives the organisation a final Davidic seal. Historically, the chapter is best read as evidence for the structure of the Second Temple administration.
