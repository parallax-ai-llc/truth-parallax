## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter rewrites 2 Samuel 24, a cult-foundation legend for a Jerusalem altar. Its census figures are impossible, and its additions, Satan as instigator, fire from heaven, and the explicit link to the Temple site, are theological developments of the Persian period.

## What the Text Claims

- "**Satan** stood up against Israel and incited David to count Israel" (21:1).
- Joab objects and does not count **Levi and Benjamin** (21:3–6).
- Joab reports **1,100,000** men in Israel and **470,000** in Judah (21:5).
- David chooses three days of plague; the angel of YHWH stands "between earth and heaven" with a drawn sword over Jerusalem (21:7–16).
- David buys the threshing floor of **Ornan** the Jebusite for **600 shekels of gold** (21:18–25).
- YHWH answers David "with **fire from heaven**" on the altar (21:26).
- The tabernacle and altar of Moses were then at **Gibeon**, but David feared to go there (21:29–30).

## External Evidence

### The population

1.57 million fighting men would imply a population of perhaps 5–6 million. Magen Broshi and Israel Finkelstein estimated about 400,000 people for the whole land west of the Jordan at its Iron Age peak in the 8th century, and far fewer in the 10th. The figures are impossible.

### The Qumran text of Samuel

The Qumran manuscript **4QSam<sup>a</sup>** preserves a version of 2 Samuel 24:16–17 that, like Chronicles, includes David and the elders "covered in sackcloth" and the angel standing "between earth and heaven." This shows that some of what looks like the Chronicler's elaboration was already present in his text of Samuel. Other changes, such as Satan and the price in gold, are not paralleled and are probably his own.

### Satan

In earlier texts *śāṭān* means "an adversary" and appears with the article as a member of the heavenly court (Job 1–2; Zech 3:1–2). In 21:1 the word appears **without the article**, and it is debated whether it should be read as a proper name, "Satan," or as "an adversary." Either way, it reflects a post-exilic development in which the incitement to sin is attributed to a figure other than God. This is one of the clearest theological changes made within the Hebrew Bible itself.

## Points of Agreement

- The ritual danger of census-taking is attested at Mari (18th century BCE) and in Exodus 30:11–16.
- A Jebusite landowner in pre-Israelite Jerusalem fits the city's non-Israelite origins.
- The basic narrative follows 2 Samuel 24.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 21 | 2 Samuel 24 |
|-----------------|-------------|
| **Satan** incites David (21:1) | **YHWH** incites David (24:1) |
| Levi and Benjamin not counted (21:6) | No exception |
| Israel 1,100,000; Judah 470,000 | Israel 800,000; Judah 500,000 |
| **600 shekels of gold** for the whole site (21:25) | **50 shekels of silver** for the threshing floor and oxen (24:24) |
| Fire from heaven consumes the offering (21:26) | No fire; the plague simply stops |
| The site is declared "the house of YHWH God" (22:1) | No link to the Temple |

The exclusion of Levi follows Numbers 1:47–49. The fire from heaven echoes the consecration of the tabernacle altar (Lev 9:24) and Solomon's Temple (2 Chr 7:1). Twelve times fifty shekels may explain the price: fifty for each tribe. The note about Gibeon explains why David sacrificed in Jerusalem while the Mosaic altar stood elsewhere.

## Assessment

The Chronicler keeps this story of David's sin, although he omits others, because it explains the origin of the Temple site. His version turns a local etiology into a founding legend for the Second Temple's altar, authenticated by fire from heaven. 2 Chronicles 3:1 completes the process by identifying the place with Mount Moriah. Historically, the chapter reveals how the Jerusalem Temple's sanctity was being grounded in Davidic and patriarchal tradition in the Persian period.
