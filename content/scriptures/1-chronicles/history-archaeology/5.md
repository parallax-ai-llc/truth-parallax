## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter's frame, the Assyrian deportation of Israelites from Transjordan in the 8th century BCE, is historical and confirmed by Assyrian sources. The genealogies and the war against the Hagrites, with its huge plunder figures, cannot be verified and are at least partly schematic.

## What the Text Claims

- Reuben lost his birthright to Joseph, although Judah became the strongest tribe (5:1–2).
- Beerah, a Reubenite chief, was carried into exile by **Tilgath-pilneser** (Tiglath-pileser) of Assyria (5:6).
- In Saul's time the Reubenites fought the **Hagrites** and took their land east of Gilead (5:10).
- The Gadites lived in Bashan and were enrolled "in the days of Jotham king of Judah and Jeroboam king of Israel" (5:11–17).
- Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh, with 44,760 warriors, defeated the Hagrites, **Jetur**, Naphish, and Nodab, taking 50,000 camels, 250,000 sheep, 2,000 donkeys, and 100,000 captives (5:18–22).
- "**Pul** king of Assyria, even Tilgath-pilneser king of Assyria" exiled the Transjordanian tribes to Halah, Habor, Hara, and the river Gozan (5:26).

## External Evidence

### Tiglath-pileser III and "Pul"

Tiglath-pileser III (745–727 BCE) campaigned in the southern Levant in 734–732 BCE. His inscriptions record the annexation of much of the kingdom of Israel, including territory in Gilead and Galilee, and the deportation of its people. That matches the background of 5:6 and 5:26.

"Pul" (*Pulu*) was the name under which Tiglath-pileser ruled as king of Babylon, as the Babylonian King List shows. 2 Kings 15:19 and 15:29 use both names without connecting them. In 5:26 the Hebrew verb is singular ("he carried them away"), so the Chronicler may have understood the two names as one king. The deportation destinations, Halah, the Habor (Khabur) river, and Gozan (Tell Halaf), are real Assyrian provincial regions, taken from 2 Kings 17:6.

### Gad and Reuben in the Mesha Stele

The **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE) states that "the men of Gad had lived in the land of Ataroth from of old" and that the king of Israel had built Ataroth for them. This is independent confirmation that Gad was an Israelite group east of the Dead Sea in the 9th century. The stele does **not** mention Reuben, which supports the view that Reuben had ceased to be a distinct political force by then.

### The Hagrites and Jetur

The Hagrites appear in Psalm 83:6. Some scholars link them with the *Hagaranu*, a tribal group named in Assyrian inscriptions, though the identification is not certain. **Jetur** is the eponym of the **Itureans**, an Arab people prominent in Lebanon and the Golan in the Hellenistic and Roman periods. Their appearance in a list set in Saul's time may reflect the Chronicler's own world.

## Points of Agreement

- Assyrian deportations from Israelite Transjordan in the 730s BCE are historical.
- Gad's presence in Transjordan is confirmed by the Mesha Stele.
- The synchronism of Jotham and Jeroboam (II) (5:17) is chronologically possible: their reigns overlapped in the mid-8th century BCE.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 5 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| 50,000 camels and 100,000 captives (5:21) | Impossible as a raid on pastoral tribes; typical of the Chronicler's inflated numbers |
| Exile attributed to "Pul" and "Tilgath-pilneser" | One king under two names; the text may or may not recognise this |
| Reuben as a powerful tribe | Reuben is absent from the Mesha Stele and fades early from history |

## Assessment

Chapter 5 combines sketchy genealogies with a credible historical frame. The Chronicler knew from Kings that Assyria deported the eastern tribes, and he presents that exile as punishment for "prostituting themselves after the gods of the peoples" (5:25), anticipating the theological explanation of Judah's later exile. The war narrative, with its divine help and gigantic spoils, follows his favourite pattern: those who cry to God in battle win (5:20).
