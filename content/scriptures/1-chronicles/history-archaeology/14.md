## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed (secondary account).** The chapter copies 2 Samuel 5:11–25. Philistine pressure on the highlands in David's time is plausible, and Phoenician–Israelite contacts are historical in general. But the Hiram synchronism is chronologically problematic, and the Chronicler's edits reflect later religious norms.

## What the Text Claims

- **Hiram king of Tyre** sends cedar, masons, and carpenters to build David a house (14:1–2).
- A list of David's sons born in Jerusalem (14:3–7).
- The Philistines attack in the Valley of Rephaim; David defeats them at **Baal-perazim** (14:8–11).
- The Philistines leave their gods behind, and "David gave command, and **they were burned**" (14:12).
- A second victory, following divine instruction to wait for "the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees," drives the Philistines from Gibeon to Gezer (14:13–16).
- "The fame of David went out into all lands, and YHWH brought the fear of him upon all nations" (14:17).

## External Evidence

### Hiram and the Tyrian chronology

Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.117–126) cites Menander of Ephesus, who used Tyrian archives, for a king Hiram son of Abibaal who reigned 34 years and built temples in Tyre. Calculations from Menander's list usually place Hiram's accession around 980–970 BCE, which would make him a contemporary of Solomon rather than early in David's reign. Some scholars propose that Hiram's contact with David came late in David's reign; others think 2 Samuel 5:11 projects Solomon's ally back onto David. No Tyrian inscription of Hiram I survives.

### Phoenician building expertise

Phoenician masonry and timber trade are well attested. The ashlar masonry and proto-Aeolic capitals of Iron Age Israel and Judah are often linked with Phoenician influence, though most examples date to the 9th century and later.

### The Valley of Rephaim

The Valley of Rephaim lies southwest of Jerusalem. A Philistine thrust there to cut Jerusalem off from Bethlehem is geographically sensible. Gezer, at the edge of the coastal plain, makes a natural limit for pursuit.

## Points of Agreement

- Chronicles follows 2 Samuel 5:11–25 almost verbatim, apart from the edits noted below.
- Philistine–Israelite conflict in the early Iron IIA highlands is plausible, especially before the decline of Gath at the end of the 9th century.

## Points of Conflict

| 1 Chronicles 14 | 2 Samuel 5 |
|-----------------|------------|
| The Philistine gods are **burned** at David's command (14:12) | David and his men **carried them away** (5:21) |
| "From **Gibeon** to Gezer" (14:16) | "From **Geba** to Gezer" (5:25) |
| Placed after the first Ark attempt | Placed before the Ark transfer |
| Closing statement on David's universal fame (14:17) | No equivalent |

Burning the idols conforms David to the law of Deuteronomy 7:5, 25. The Aramaic Targum of 2 Samuel 5:21 also introduces the burning, a later harmonisation in the same direction.

## Assessment

Chapter 14 presents three signs of divine favour, an international ally, a growing family, and victory in war, between the failed and successful Ark processions. This framing shows that David's blessing is tied to his care for the Ark. The underlying traditions may preserve memories of real conflicts with the Philistines near Jerusalem. The Chronicler's changes, notably the burning of idols and the closing claim of David's worldwide fame, are theological additions.
