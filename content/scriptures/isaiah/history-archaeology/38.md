## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Hezekiah is a well-attested historical king, and a serious illness is entirely plausible. But the central sign — the shadow moving back ten steps — is a miracle story with no possible external confirmation. The chronological link with a promise of fifteen more years is difficult to reconcile with the dates of his reign.

## What the Text Claims

- "In those days" Hezekiah became ill to the point of death; Isaiah told him to set his house in order (38:1).
- Hezekiah prayed and wept; God added **fifteen years** to his life and promised to defend Jerusalem from Assyria (38:2–6).
- As a sign, the shadow on "the steps of **Ahaz**" went back ten steps (38:7–8).
- **"The writing of Hezekiah"**: a psalm of thanksgiving composed after his recovery (38:9–20).
- Isaiah ordered a cake of figs to be applied to the boil (38:21–22).

## External Evidence

### Hezekiah

Hezekiah is confirmed by Sennacherib's annals and by a clay bulla from the Ophel excavations in Jerusalem, published by Eilat Mazar in 2015, reading "Belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah." Nothing outside the Bible refers to his illness.

### Dating the illness

The phrase "in those days" ties the illness loosely to the Assyrian crisis (38:6). If Hezekiah died c. 687/686 BCE and lived fifteen more years, the illness would date to c. 702–701 BCE. Isaiah 36:1 places the invasion in his 14th year; 2 Kings 18:2 gives him a 29-year reign. 14 + 15 = 29, which suggests that the "fourteenth year" may originally have belonged to the illness story. The figures fit together neatly, perhaps too neatly, and may be schematic.

### "The steps of Ahaz"

The Hebrew *ma'alot* means "steps" or "stairs." It may have been a staircase in the palace whose shadow marked the time, or a purpose-built sundial. Egyptian and Mesopotamian shadow clocks are known. No archaeological trace of Ahaz's steps exists. A shadow moving backward is not a phenomenon history can confirm.

### Text and composition

- The **Psalm of Hezekiah** (38:9–20) is not in 2 Kings 20. Its form resembles individual thanksgiving psalms in the Psalter.
- Verses 21–22 appear out of order: in 2 Kings 20:7–8 they come before the sign. In the **Great Isaiah Scroll** (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>), these verses were **added by a later hand** in the space between lines and in the margin. This suggests they were missing from the scribe's original copy and were added to match the text of Kings.
- Fig poultices are a plausible ancient remedy for skin conditions.

## Points of Agreement

- Hezekiah is historical, and the story's setting (a king facing Assyria) is real.
- The narrative's medical detail (a fig poultice) is realistic.

## Points of Conflict

- The shadow's retreat is a miracle with no external confirmation.
- The chronology of the reign does not fit easily with all biblical data (see chapter 36).

## Assessment

Chapter 38 is a prophetic legend about a historical king, shared with 2 Kings 20:1–11. Isaiah's version adds a psalm attributed to Hezekiah, probably not composed by him. The textual history of verses 21–22 in the Dead Sea Scrolls offers rare manuscript evidence of how the Isaiah narratives were harmonised with Kings.
