## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High (for the setting).** The Assyrian king's boast in 10:8–11 lists cities that Assyria conquered between 740 and 717 BCE, in an accurate geographical sweep. The boast also closely echoes the language of Assyrian royal inscriptions. The march on Jerusalem in 10:28–32, however, does not match Sennacherib's actual route in 701 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Assyria is "the rod of my anger," sent against a godless nation, but its king intends to destroy (10:5–7).
- The king boasts: "Is not **Calno** like **Carchemish**? Is not **Hamath** like **Arpad**? Is not **Samaria** like **Damascus**?" (10:9).
- He claims: "I have removed the boundaries of peoples, and plundered their treasures" (10:13).
- Assyria will be punished (10:12, 16–19, 24–27). A remnant of Israel will return (10:20–23).
- An enemy advances from the north through Aiath, Migron, Michmash, Geba, Ramah, Gibeah, Anathoth, and Nob, until he shakes his fist at Zion (10:28–32).

## External Evidence

### The conquered cities

| City (10:9) | Assyrian conquest |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Arpad | Tiglath-pileser III, 740 BCE (and again by Sargon II, 720) |
| Calno (Kullani) | Tiglath-pileser III, 738 BCE |
| Damascus | Tiglath-pileser III, 732 BCE |
| Samaria | Shalmaneser V / Sargon II, 722–720 BCE |
| Hamath | Sargon II, 720 BCE |
| Carchemish | Sargon II, 717 BCE |

These conquests are all recorded in Assyrian sources. Since Carchemish fell in 717 BCE, the oracle in its present form must date after that, during the reign of **Sargon II** (722–705 BCE).

### Assyrian royal ideology

Peter Machinist's study (1983) showed that the king's speech in 10:8–14 mirrors the vocabulary of Assyrian royal inscriptions. Assyrian kings boasted of "abolishing borders," deporting peoples, plundering treasuries, and of their own strength and wisdom. Isaiah appears to have known these themes, perhaps through official proclamations or Assyrian envoys, and turned them into a parody of royal arrogance.

### The northern approach (10:28–32)

The towns named run from the north down the Benjamin plateau to Nob, overlooking Jerusalem. In 701 BCE, however, Sennacherib advanced from the coast and the Shephelah, where he besieged Lachish (Isa 36:2). Scholars suggest the poem is an imaginative, idealised picture of attack, or that it refers to another occasion, such as the Syro-Ephraimite invasion of c. 734 BCE or an Assyrian movement under Sargon II.

## Points of Agreement

- The list of conquered cities is historically accurate and correctly ordered in geography.
- The Assyrian king's arrogance is portrayed in terms that match genuine Assyrian royal rhetoric.
- The prophecy of Assyria's eventual downfall came true (Nineveh fell in 612 BCE), though long after Isaiah.

## Points of Conflict

- The march route in 10:28–32 does not match the documented campaign of 701 BCE.
- **10:20–27** (the remnant "returning" and leaning on God) is widely regarded as a post-exilic expansion. Its language depends on the name of Isaiah's son Shear-jashub ("a remnant shall return," 7:3).

## Assessment

Chapter 10 provides strong evidence that its core was composed by someone living under Assyrian domination in the late 8th century, who knew both the course of Assyria's western conquests and the style of its propaganda. The chapter is less a record of events than a theological answer to them. It is nonetheless anchored in a real and accurately perceived political world.
