## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 40 makes no historical claims about events. What history can assess is its date: the chapter presupposes that Jerusalem has already been destroyed and its people have "served their term" (40:2). This places it in the Babylonian exile, c. 550–539 BCE, about 150 years after Isaiah son of Amoz.

## What the Text Claims

- Jerusalem's "warfare is ended" and her penalty paid (40:1–2). The city has already suffered judgment.
- A voice calls for a highway in the wilderness for YHWH's return (40:3–5).
- The cities of Judah are told "Behold your God" (40:9), as if the return were near.
- Idols are crafted by goldsmiths and woodworkers (40:18–20), and YHWH alone created the stars and the earth (40:12–26).

## External Evidence

### The historical setting the chapter assumes

- **Babylonian Chronicles (ABC 5)** and **2 Kings 25** record Nebuchadnezzar's capture of Jerusalem in 597 BCE and its destruction in 586 BCE. Destruction layers in the City of David (Shiloh's excavations, 1978–1985) confirm a violent burning of the city in the early 6th century.
- **Al-Yahudu tablets.** About 200 cuneiform documents from a Babylonian settlement called Āl-Yāhūdu ("Judah-town"), dated 572–477 BCE, show deported Judeans farming crown land, bearing Yahwistic names, and staying put for generations. They confirm a real exilic community of the kind Isaiah 40–55 addresses.
- **Babylonian processional culture.** The call to build a straight road for a god (40:3) echoes the processional ways of Babylon, such as the street leading to the Ishtar Gate, along which Marduk's statue was carried at the New Year festival. The chapter turns this image toward YHWH.

### The Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsa^a)

The Great Isaiah Scroll (c. 125–100 BCE) contains all 66 chapters. Chapter 40 begins near the bottom of column XXXII after only an ordinary paragraph break, with no sign of a new book. The scroll's most visible division lies elsewhere, after chapter 33, where column XXVII ends with three blank lines.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter's picture of a ruined Jerusalem and a dispersed population matches the archaeological and cuneiform record of the 6th century.
- Idol manufacture as described (overlaying wood with gold, choosing wood that will not rot) fits Mesopotamian cult-statue practice known from Babylonian ritual texts such as the *mīs pî* ("washing of the mouth") ceremony.

## Points of Conflict

| Traditional view | Historical-critical finding |
|------------------|-----------------------------|
| Written by Isaiah of Jerusalem (c. 740–700 BCE) | Assumes the exile as present reality, not a future event |
| One book from one author | Vocabulary, style and theology shift sharply at ch. 40, and Isaiah himself is never mentioned again |
| Addressed to 8th-century Judah | Addressed to people who have already lost their land |

## Assessment

The case for a separate exilic author ("Deutero-Isaiah") goes back to Ibn Ezra's hints in the 12th century and was argued in full by J. C. Döderlein (1775) and J. G. Eichhorn. It is now the near-consensus view. The main reason is not a ban on prediction but the stance of the text: it does not predict the exile, it *assumes* it and speaks comfort into it.

**Conservative arguments.** Scholars such as **John N. Oswalt** and **J. Alec Motyer** defend single authorship. They note that 1QIsa^a shows no break at chapter 40; that Ben Sira (48:24–25, c. 180 BCE) credits Isaiah with comforting "the mourners in Zion"; that the New Testament quotes chapters 40–66 under Isaiah's name; and that phrases such as "the Holy One of Israel" run through the whole book.

**Critical reply.** The manuscript and Ben Sira evidence shows only that the book had been combined by the 2nd century BCE, which critical scholars accept. It cannot tell us who wrote chapters 40–66 some 400 years earlier. Shared themes are better explained by a later author who consciously continued the earlier Isaianic tradition (Williamson, Sommer). A minority of critical scholars (Barstad, Tiemeyer) argue that 40–55 was composed in Judah, not Babylon, but they agree on the exilic date.
