## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** Chapter 58 criticizes public fasts that coexist with oppression of workers. It promises that the community will "rebuild the ancient ruins" (58:12). These features fit the early post-exilic period, when fasts mourning Jerusalem's destruction were being observed and the city was still partly in ruins.

## What the Text Claims

- The people fast and complain that YHWH does not notice (58:3).
- They fast while oppressing workers and quarrelling (58:3–4).
- The true fast is to loose bonds, free the oppressed, feed the hungry and house the homeless (58:6–7).
- Those who do so will "build up the ancient ruins" and be called "repairer of the breach" (58:12).
- Keeping the Sabbath is commanded (58:13–14).

## External Evidence

### Fasts for the destruction

**Zechariah 7:1–7** (dated to the fourth year of Darius I, 518 BCE) records a delegation asking whether to continue the fast of the fifth month, which had been kept "these seventy years." **Zechariah 8:19** lists fasts in the 4th, 5th, 7th and 10th months, which correspond to dates in the siege and fall of Jerusalem and the murder of Gedaliah. Zechariah's answer (7:9–10) closely resembles Isaiah 58: justice and mercy matter more than fasting.

### Ruins and social conditions

- Archaeological surveys show that Jerusalem remained small and partly ruined through the Persian period. Its walls were not rebuilt until Nehemiah (c. 445 BCE).
- **Nehemiah 5** describes a debt crisis in which poorer Judeans mortgaged fields and sold children into slavery to pay taxes and buy grain. The "bonds of injustice" in 58:6 may refer to similar debt-slavery.
- **Nehemiah 13:15–22** describes conflict over trading on the Sabbath.

## Points of Agreement

- Fasts commemorating the destruction are independently attested in Zechariah, in the same era.
- Ruined cities and economic hardship are consistent with the archaeology of Persian Yehud and with Nehemiah.

## Points of Conflict

None.

## Assessment

Isaiah 58 is a credible window into the religious and social life of Persian-period Judah, c. 520–450 BCE. Its closeness to Zechariah 7–8 is among the stronger internal clues for dating Trito-Isaiah.
