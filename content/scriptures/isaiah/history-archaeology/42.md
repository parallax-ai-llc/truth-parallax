## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The chapter contains the first of the so-called Servant Songs (42:1–4) and a hymn calling distant peoples, including Kedar and Sela, to praise YHWH. It makes no event claims. Its geography and its picture of a captive people "plundered and looted" (42:22) fit the exilic setting of c. 550–539 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- YHWH's chosen servant will bring justice to the nations quietly, without crying out (42:1–4).
- The servant is given as "a covenant to the people, a light to the nations" (42:6).
- Kedar and the inhabitants of Sela are summoned to sing (42:10–12).
- Israel is a blind and deaf servant, "trapped in holes, hidden in prisons" (42:18–25).

## External Evidence

### The Servant Songs as a scholarly category

Bernhard Duhm (1892) first isolated four "Servant Songs" (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12) and argued they were a separate composition. Later scholars debate this. T. N. D. Mettinger (*A Farewell to the Servant Songs*, 1983) argued they belong to their context and that the servant is Israel throughout. Elsewhere in chapters 41–48 the servant is explicitly Israel/Jacob (41:8; 42:19; 44:1), and 42:1 is translated in the Septuagint as "Jacob my servant… Israel my chosen."

Some critical scholars have proposed that 42:1–4 originally described **Cyrus**, since the task of bringing "justice to the nations" resembles the royal ideology of the Cyrus Cylinder. This remains a minority view.

### Kedar and Sela

- **Kedar** (Qedar) was a powerful north Arabian tribal confederation, attested repeatedly in Assyrian royal inscriptions from Tiglath-pileser III to Ashurbanipal.
- **Sela** ("rock") is probably a place in Edom.
- **Nabonidus** spent about ten years (c. 552–543 BCE) at the oasis of Tayma in north Arabia. His Harran stelae list the Arabian oases he controlled (Tayma, Dadanu, Yatribu and others). Arabia was therefore very much in view in Babylonian politics when this poem was written.

## Points of Agreement

- The mention of Arabian and Edomite peoples (42:11) fits the geopolitics of Nabonidus's reign.
- Israel "plundered and looted" and imprisoned (42:22) is a fair poetic picture of deportation and life under Babylonian rule, though the Al-Yahudu tablets show many exiles lived as settled farmers rather than in prisons.

## Points of Conflict

None of substance. The language of prisoners and dungeons (42:7, 22) is metaphorical exaggeration if taken literally. Cuneiform archives show deportees with land, families and business dealings.

## Assessment

The chapter is historically useful mainly as evidence of its own time. The identity of the servant is a literary and theological question. Jewish interpretation has generally identified the servant with Israel, Christian interpretation with Jesus, and critical scholars variously with Israel, an ideal Israel, the prophet, or Cyrus. History can say only that the original audience was the 6th-century exilic community.
