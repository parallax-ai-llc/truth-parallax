## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed to high (for the setting).** The visit of envoys from **Merodach-baladan** of Babylon is historically very plausible: this Chaldean leader is well documented in Assyrian and Babylonian sources as Assyria's persistent enemy who sought allies in the west. Isaiah's prediction that Hezekiah's treasures and descendants would be taken to Babylon matches the exile of 597–587 BCE so precisely that most scholars consider it written after the event.

## What the Text Claims

- **Merodach-baladan son of Baladan, king of Babylon**, sent letters and a gift to Hezekiah after hearing of his recovery (39:1).
- Hezekiah showed the envoys his whole treasure-house and armoury (39:2).
- Isaiah predicted that all of it would be carried to **Babylon**, and some of Hezekiah's sons would become eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon (39:5–7).
- Hezekiah replied, "The word of the LORD is good," thinking there would be peace in his days (39:8).

## External Evidence

### Marduk-apla-iddina II

Merodach-baladan is the Hebrew form of **Marduk-apla-iddina II**, chief of the Chaldean tribe of **Bit-Yakin** in southern Babylonia. Assyrian and Babylonian sources, including the Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 1) and the inscriptions of Sargon II and Sennacherib, record that he:

- seized the throne of Babylon in **722/721 BCE** and held it for twelve years with Elamite support;
- was driven out by **Sargon II in 710 BCE**;
- regained Babylon briefly in **703 BCE** and was defeated by **Sennacherib** in his first campaign;
- fled to the marshes and to Elam, where he died.

His diplomacy against Assyria, including approaches to western states, fits the visit to Hezekiah. The embassy is usually dated either to c. 713–711 BCE (alongside the Ashdod revolt) or to c. 703 BCE, just before Hezekiah's own revolt.

### "Son of Baladan"

Assyrian texts call him "son of Yakin," meaning a member of the Bit-Yakin tribe. His father's name is not known from cuneiform sources. "Baladan" may be a shortened or garbled form, not a historical name.

### Hezekiah's treasures

Sennacherib's annals record that Hezekiah sent an enormous tribute to Nineveh (gold, silver, precious stones, ivory furniture). This shows that Hezekiah had real treasures to display.

### The prediction of exile

Nebuchadnezzar carried off "the treasures of the house of the LORD and of the king's house" in 597 BCE (2 Kgs 24:13), and King Jehoiachin and his family were deported to Babylon. **Babylonian ration tablets** from Nebuchadnezzar's palace (published by E. F. Weidner in 1939) list rations for "Ya'ukin, king of Judah" and his sons.

## Points of Agreement

| Isaiah 39 | External record |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Merodach-baladan, king of Babylon | Marduk-apla-iddina II, 722–710 and 703 BCE |
| Envoys sent to a western king | He sought allies against Assyria |
| Hezekiah's rich treasury | Heavy tribute paid to Sennacherib |
| Royal descendants in Babylon | Jehoiachin and sons in Nebuchadnezzar's ration lists |

## Points of Conflict

- The predicted deportation of Hezekiah's treasures to **Babylon** did not happen in his reign: it was Assyria that took his treasures. The prophecy fits 597–587 BCE, more than a century later.
- The patronymic "son of Baladan" is not supported by cuneiform evidence.

## Assessment

The embassy itself is historically credible and is one of the details in the Hezekiah narratives most clearly corroborated by Mesopotamian sources. The prophecy that follows, however, is best explained as *vaticinium ex eventu*, composed by writers who knew of the Babylonian exile. Its position at the end of chapters 36–39 is deliberate. It prepares the reader for chapter 40, which addresses a community already in Babylon.
