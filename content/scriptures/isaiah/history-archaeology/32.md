## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy/wisdom poetry).** Chapter 32 contains no datable events. Scholars debate whether it is Isaianic or a later composition. Its wisdom-style vocabulary and its hope for an outpouring of the spirit suggest that much of it is later than the 8th century.

## What the Text Claims

- "A king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in justice" (32:1).
- The fool will no longer be called noble (32:5–8).
- **Complacent women** are warned that in little more than a year the grape harvest will fail; the palace will be forsaken and the city deserted (32:9–14).
- Until "the spirit is poured upon us from on high," and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field (32:15–20).

## External Evidence

There is no direct archaeological evidence. The arguments concern date.

### The righteous king

Some scholars read 32:1 as Isaiah's hope for a just king, possibly **Hezekiah** or his successor. Others see it as a generic description of ideal kingship, of the type found in Proverbs (for example 16:12; 25:5) and in Near Eastern royal ideology. The verse names no one.

### Wisdom vocabulary

Verses 5–8 contrast the "fool" (*nabal*) and the "scoundrel" with the "noble," using vocabulary typical of Proverbs and other wisdom literature. This has led some scholars to see the chapter as the work of a later sage editing the Isaiah tradition.

### The complacent women

Verses 9–14 fit Isaiah's earlier oracle against the women of Jerusalem (3:16–4:1) and the devastation of 701 BCE, when the Judean countryside was overrun. Many regard this section as Isaianic.

### The spirit poured out

The hope that the spirit will be "poured out" (32:15) resembles Isaiah 44:3, Ezekiel 39:29, and Joel 2:28–29 (MT 3:1–2), all generally dated to the exilic or post-exilic period.

## Points of Agreement

- The threat to Jerusalem's harvest and city in 32:9–14 fits the Assyrian invasion of 701 BCE.

## Points of Conflict

- None external.
- The prediction that the palace and hill would become "dens forever" (32:14) was not fulfilled in 701 BCE. It may have been read later as a reference to 587 BCE.

## Assessment

Chapter 32 is probably composite. Verses 9–14 are plausibly Isaianic. Verses 1–8 and 15–20 are often attributed to later editors, who presented an ideal kingdom of justice and peace after the judgments of chapters 28–31. The chapter's value for the historian is mainly in showing how the Isaiah tradition was extended by later writers.
