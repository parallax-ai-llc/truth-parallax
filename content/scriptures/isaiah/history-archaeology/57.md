## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** Chapter 57 accuses some of the community of fertility rites under green trees, child sacrifice in the valleys, offerings to "the king" (*melek*) and practices associated with the dead. Whether these describe real practices in Persian-period Yehud, or recycle older pre-exilic polemic, is debated.

## What the Text Claims

- The righteous die and no one notices (57:1–2).
- "Children of a sorceress" burn with lust under every green tree and slaughter children in the valleys (57:3–5).
- Offerings are poured out among "the smooth stones of the valley" and a bed is set up on a high mountain (57:6–8).
- The accused journey "to the king [*melek*] with oil" and send envoys "down to Sheol" (57:9).
- Peace is promised to the humble and contrite (57:14–21).

## External Evidence

### Child sacrifice and "Molech"

- The Hebrew Bible associates child sacrifice with the **Tophet** in the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31). No such site has been found in Jerusalem.
- At **Carthage** and other Phoenician colonies, *tophet* precincts contain urns of burned infants and animals with dedications to Baal Hammon and Tanit. Stager and Wolff (1984) argued they show real child sacrifice. Others suggest burials of infants who died naturally. The debate continues, but most specialists now accept that at least some sacrifice took place.
- Scholars disagree whether *molek* was a god (John Day) or a type of sacrifice (Otto Eissfeldt, 1935). The *melek* of 57:9 may play on this word.

### Cult of the dead

References to Sheol, envoys sent below and the "smooth stones of the wadi" have been linked to ancestor cults and necromancy. Comparative evidence from Ugarit (the *rpum* texts, *kispum* offerings in Mesopotamia) shows such practices in the wider region (Theodore Lewis, 1989).

### Pre-exilic or post-exilic?

The vocabulary closely resembles Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Hosea on pre-exilic cults. Some scholars think 57:3–13 is an older oracle reused in Trito-Isaiah. Others argue that such practices persisted among Judeans who had never gone into exile, and that "the people of the land" were accused of them by returnees.

## Points of Agreement

- Fertility rites, high places and offerings to the dead are well attested in the Levant.
- The persistence of traditional popular religion after 586 BCE is plausible.

## Points of Conflict

There is no archaeological evidence of child sacrifice in Persian-period Yehud. Material culture of the period shows very few figurines or cult objects, a sharp contrast with the many Judean pillar figurines of the late monarchy. If the polemic describes contemporary practice, it is not yet confirmed archaeologically.

## Assessment

Chapter 57 is most likely polemic drawing on older prophetic stock language and directed at rivals within the post-exilic community. Historians should treat it as evidence of factional conflict, not as a reliable description of widespread cultic practices in the late 6th century.
