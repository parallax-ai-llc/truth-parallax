## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy/poetry).** The chapter combines a vision of universal peace (2:2–4), whose date is disputed, with an older poem about the "Day of the LORD" (2:6–22), which fits the prosperity of 8th-century Judah.

## What the Text Claims

- In "the latter days," the Temple mount will be raised above all mountains, nations will stream to it, and they will beat "swords into plowshares" (2:2–4).
- The land is "full of silver and gold," horses, chariots, and idols, and diviners "from the east" and Philistine practices are popular (2:6–8).
- A Day of the LORD will humble everything lofty: cedars of Lebanon, oaks of Bashan, towers, fortified walls, and "all the ships of Tarshish" (2:12–17).

## External Evidence

### The shared oracle with Micah

Isaiah 2:2–4 appears almost word for word in **Micah 4:1–3**, where Micah adds a further verse (4:4). Scholars are divided over whether Isaiah borrowed from Micah, Micah from Isaiah, or both from an older liturgical piece. Many date the oracle to the exilic or post-exilic period, because the pilgrimage of the nations to Zion and the language of "the latter days" are prominent in Isaiah 40–66 and in Zechariah 8. Others defend an 8th-century origin.

### Wealth and military display

The picture of a land full of silver, horses, and chariots (2:7) suits the long reign of **Uzziah** (c. 785–740 BCE). Kings and Chronicles credit him with rebuilding Elath on the Red Sea (2 Kgs 14:22) and strengthening the army and fortifications (2 Chr 26:6–15). Archaeologically, the 8th century was a period of growth in Judah, before the Assyrian destruction of 701 BCE. "Ships of Tarshish" was a stock phrase for large seagoing merchant vessels (compare 1 Kgs 10:22; 22:48).

### Foreign religious practice

Diviners "from the east" and soothsayers "like the Philistines" (2:6) fit a period of open trade and contact. Seals and figurines from 8th-century Judah show widespread use of Egyptian, Phoenician, and Syrian motifs.

## Points of Agreement

- The social and economic picture in 2:6–17 is consistent with what is known of 8th-century Judah.
- The chapter's list of "lofty" things (towers, walls, ships) reflects a real monarchic state with fortifications and trade.

## Points of Conflict

- No direct conflict. The date of 2:2–4 is debated, so it should not be assumed to come from the 8th-century prophet.
- Verse 22 ("Stop regarding man …") is absent from the Septuagint and is generally treated as a late scribal addition.

## Assessment

Chapter 2 shows the layered character of the book. A genuine 8th-century poem against pride and idolatry (2:6–21) has been introduced by a vision of world peace that could come from almost any period between the 8th and 5th centuries BCE. The chapter has no events to verify, but its social details are realistic for Judah before the Assyrian invasions.
