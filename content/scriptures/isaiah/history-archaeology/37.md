## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** That Sennacherib withdrew without taking Jerusalem is confirmed by his own annals, and his murder by his sons is confirmed by Babylonian and Assyrian sources. But the angelic slaughter of 185,000 soldiers is unsupported by any Assyrian evidence, the title "king" for **Tirhakah** in 701 BCE is anachronistic, and the narrative compresses twenty years between the withdrawal and Sennacherib's death.

## What the Text Claims

- Hezekiah mourns and sends to Isaiah, who promises that Sennacherib will hear a rumour, return home, and fall by the sword (37:1–7).
- Sennacherib, now at **Libnah**, hears that **Tirhakah king of Cush** is coming to fight him, and sends a threatening letter (37:8–13).
- Hezekiah prays; Isaiah answers with a taunt-song against Assyria (37:14–35).
- "The angel of the LORD went out and struck down **185,000** in the camp of the Assyrians" (37:36).
- Sennacherib returned to **Nineveh**, where his sons **Adrammelech** and **Sharezer** killed him while he worshipped in the house of **Nisroch**; they escaped to **Ararat**, and **Esarhaddon** reigned (37:37–38).

## External Evidence

### Sennacherib's account

Sennacherib's annals claim that Hezekiah was shut in Jerusalem, that his territory was given to the Philistine kings, and that Hezekiah later sent a huge tribute to **Nineveh**: 30 talents of gold, 800 talents of silver, precious stones, ivory furniture, his daughters, palace women, and musicians. They do **not** claim that Jerusalem was taken or Hezekiah deposed. This is unusual for a rebel leader in Assyrian records, and it supports the biblical claim that Jerusalem survived.

### The 185,000

No Assyrian source mentions a disaster, which is unsurprising since Assyrian annals did not record defeats. **Herodotus** (2.141) tells an Egyptian story in which Sennacherib's army, near Pelusium, was overrun at night by field mice that gnawed its bowstrings and shields. Some scholars connect both stories to an outbreak of disease, but this is speculation. The figure of 185,000 is implausibly large for the force likely present at Jerusalem. Most historians regard the story as legendary, a theological explanation for Sennacherib's withdrawal.

### Tirhakah

**Taharqa** became king of Egypt and Kush in **690 BCE**, eleven years after 701. In 701 BCE he would have been a young prince. Conservative scholars, notably K. A. Kitchen, argue he could have led the army as a prince and that the narrator used his later title. Critical scholars see this as one sign that the account was written decades later.

### The murder of Sennacherib

- The **Babylonian Chronicle** (ABC 1) records that in 681 BCE "Sennacherib king of Assyria was killed by his son in a rebellion."
- **Esarhaddon's** inscription (Nineveh Prism A) describes his brothers' revolt and his victory over them.
- Simo Parpola (1980) identified the main assassin as **Arda-Mulissi**, a son of Sennacherib, whose name probably lies behind "Adrammelech."
- "Nisroch" is not a known god; it may be a corruption of Nusku or Ninurta.
- "Ararat" is Urartu, a plausible refuge north of Assyria.

## Points of Conflict

| Isaiah 37 | Historical record |
|-----------|-------------------|
| 185,000 Assyrians struck down in one night | No evidence; Sennacherib reports a successful campaign |
| Tirhakah "king of Cush" in 701 BCE | Taharqa became king only in 690 BCE |
| Sennacherib dies after returning home (implied soon) | He died in 681 BCE, twenty years later, after further campaigns |

## Assessment

Since Bernhard Stade (1886), scholars have identified more than one source in 2 Kings 18:13–19:37 (Isaiah 36–37): a brief report of Hezekiah's submission (2 Kgs 18:14–16, omitted in Isaiah) and two parallel accounts of the Assyrian demands and the deliverance (often called B1 and B2). Brevard Childs's analysis (1967) remains standard.

The most widely held historical reconstruction is this: Hezekiah submitted and paid heavy tribute, and Sennacherib withdrew without destroying Jerusalem, perhaps because the city was strongly fortified, because of problems elsewhere, or because a costly siege was unnecessary once tribute was secured. The biblical narrative, composed later, interpreted this survival as a miracle. The theme of Jerusalem's inviolability grew from it and was later shattered by the Babylonian destruction of 587 BCE.

An older "two-campaign" theory (John Bright) placed the miraculous deliverance in a second, unrecorded campaign around 688 BCE to accommodate Tirhakah. It has found little support, since no evidence for such a campaign exists.
