## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High.** The chapter's description of Jerusalem's defensive preparations — houses torn down to strengthen the wall and a reservoir built "between the two walls" — matches archaeological discoveries from Hezekiah's reign remarkably well. The rebuke of a royal steward building himself a rock-cut tomb fits the elite tombs of Silwan, one of which may belong to the same man.

## What the Text Claims

- Jerusalem celebrates while its leaders flee and are captured (22:1–3).
- **Elam** and **Kir** come with chariots and horsemen (22:6).
- The people inspect the weapons in the **House of the Forest** (22:8), count the houses and **tear them down to fortify the wall** (22:10), and make **a reservoir between the two walls** for the water of the old pool (22:11).
- **Shebna**, who is "over the house" (royal steward), is rebuked for **hewing a tomb for himself on the height**; he will be replaced by **Eliakim son of Hilkiah** (22:15–25).

## External Evidence

### The Broad Wall

In the 1970s Nahman Avigad excavated a massive city wall in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter, about 7 metres thick, dated to the late 8th century BCE. It was built **over demolished houses**, whose remains were found beneath it. This wall enclosed the expanding Western Hill and is widely linked to Hezekiah's preparations against Assyria (2 Chr 32:5). Its construction matches 22:10 closely.

### Water works

Hezekiah's **Siloam Tunnel** carried water from the Gihon Spring to the Siloam pool inside the city (2 Kgs 20:20; 2 Chr 32:30). Radiometric dating published in 2003 places the tunnel c. 700 BCE. A reservoir "between the two walls" (22:11) fits a pool in the lower Tyropoeon Valley, protected by the walls of the City of David and the new western wall.

### Shebna and the Royal Steward inscription

In 1870 Charles Clermont-Ganneau removed an inscribed lintel from a rock-cut tomb in the village of **Silwan**, opposite the City of David. Nahman Avigad deciphered it in 1953. It reads: "This is [the tomb of …]**yahu who is over the house**. There is no silver and no gold here, only [his bones] and the bones of his maidservant with him. Cursed be the man who opens this." The name is damaged. Avigad suggested it could be **Shebanyahu**, the full form of Shebna. The identification is attractive but cannot be proved. The lintel is in the British Museum.

### Other details

- The **House of the Forest** (of Lebanon) is the royal armoury-hall mentioned in 1 Kings 7:2 and 10:17.
- **Elam and Kir** (22:6): Elamite archers are attested in Assyrian armies, but Elam was usually Assyria's enemy. The verse's setting is debated.

## Points of Agreement

| Isaiah 22 | Archaeological evidence |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Houses torn down to fortify the wall | Broad Wall built over demolished houses, late 8th c. BCE |
| Reservoir between two walls | Hezekiah's water system and Siloam pool |
| Royal steward's rock-cut tomb | Silwan "Royal Steward" tomb inscription |

## Points of Conflict

- None with the external record. The setting of 22:1–14 is debated: most link it to 701 BCE, though some think of 711 BCE or even 587 BCE.
- Verses 24–25, which predict Eliakim's own fall, appear to be a later addition reacting to events after his appointment.

## Assessment

Chapter 22 is one of the strongest cases in First Isaiah of text and archaeology converging. The prophet condemns the people for relying on engineering — walls, weapons, water — rather than on God. Archaeology confirms that exactly this engineering took place in Hezekiah's Jerusalem.
