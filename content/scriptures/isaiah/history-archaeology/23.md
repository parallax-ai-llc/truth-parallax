## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The lament over Phoenicia fits Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE, when **Luli**, king of Sidon, fled overseas — echoed in the call to "cross over to Kittim" (23:12). But Tyre itself was not destroyed then. The "seventy years" of Tyre's eclipse (23:15–18) is a schematic figure that cannot be matched to a known period.

## What the Text Claims

- "Wail, O ships of **Tarshish**, for Tyre is laid waste" (23:1). The news comes from **Kittim** (Cyprus).
- **Sidon**, the merchant city whose revenue was the grain of the Nile, is put to shame (23:2–4).
- "Arise, cross over to **Kittim**; even there you will have no rest" (23:12).
- "Behold the land of the **Chaldeans** … Assyria destined it for wild beasts" (23:13).
- Tyre will be forgotten for **seventy years**, then return to her trade like a prostitute, and her profits will be dedicated to the LORD (23:15–18).

## External Evidence

### Sennacherib and Luli (701 BCE)

Sennacherib's annals record that at the start of his third campaign, **Luli (Lulê), king of Sidon**, fled "far off into the midst of the sea" (understood to mean Cyprus) and died there. Sennacherib installed **Tuba'lu** (Ethbaal) as king of Sidon and received tribute from the cities of the coast. Reliefs from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh show ships escaping from a Phoenician city, often identified with this flight.

### Phoenician trade

The chapter's picture of a commercial empire is accurate. Phoenician cities, led by Tyre, traded across the Mediterranean, founding colonies in Cyprus (Kition), North Africa (Carthage, traditionally 814 BCE), and Spain. **Tarshish** is usually identified with a western Mediterranean port, often Tartessos in southern Spain. Phoenicia's dependence on Egyptian grain (23:3) is plausible but not specifically documented.

### Later sieges of Tyre

- **Nebuchadnezzar II** besieged Tyre for **thirteen years** (c. 586–573 BCE), according to Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.156) citing Tyrian records. The siege ended in a settlement, not destruction (compare Ezek 29:17–18).
- **Alexander the Great** took island Tyre in 332 BCE after a seven-month siege, building a causeway and selling the population into slavery (Arrian, *Anabasis* 2.15–24).

### "The land of the Chaldeans"

Verse 13 is textually difficult. Some scholars think "Chaldeans" replaced an original "Kittim" or reflects a gloss referring to Sennacherib's destruction of Babylon (689 BCE), offered as a warning to Tyre.

## Points of Agreement

- The flight of Sidon's king overseas in 701 BCE matches 23:12.
- The commercial reach of Phoenicia to Cyprus and Tarshish is historically accurate.

## Points of Conflict

| Isaiah 23 | Historical record |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Tyre "laid waste" (23:1) | Tyre on its island was not taken in 701 BCE or by Nebuchadnezzar |
| Forgotten for seventy years | No corresponding 70-year eclipse of Tyre is documented |
| Tyre's profits dedicated to the LORD | No historical fulfilment |

## Assessment

The core of chapter 23 is plausibly an 8th-century lament, reflecting Sennacherib's campaign against Phoenicia. It was later expanded: verses 15–18 use the "seventy years" motif also found in Jeremiah 25:11–12 and Zechariah 1:12, and probably come from the Persian period. Some scholars have argued that the chapter as a whole reflects Alexander's siege of 332 BCE, but most see the Assyrian setting as primary.
