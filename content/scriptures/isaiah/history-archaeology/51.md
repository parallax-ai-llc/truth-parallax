## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The chapter calls exiles to look to Abraham and Sarah and to YHWH's ancient victory over the sea dragon Rahab. Its value for history lies in what it reveals about Israelite religious traditions in the 6th century, including their roots in older Canaanite myth.

## What the Text Claims

- "Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you" (51:1–2). He was one man, yet became many.
- YHWH's arm "cut Rahab in pieces" and "pierced the dragon," and dried up the sea for the redeemed to cross (51:9–10).
- Jerusalem has drunk "the cup of his wrath" and must now wake up (51:17–23).

## External Evidence

### Abraham in the exile

Apart from Genesis, Abraham is rarely mentioned in pre-exilic prophecy. **Ezekiel 33:24** quotes people left in Judah claiming the land because "Abraham was only one man." Isaiah 51:2 uses the same argument for the exiles. Many scholars see this as evidence that the Abraham traditions grew in importance during the exile. No archaeological or extra-biblical evidence for Abraham as a historical person exists.

### The sea dragon

- **Ugaritic texts** (Ras Shamra, c. 1200 BCE) describe Baal defeating Yam (Sea) and *Litan* (Leviathan), and mention *Tunnan* (the biblical *tannin*, "dragon") among the defeated foes.
- **Enuma Elish**, the Babylonian creation epic recited at the New Year festival, has Marduk split the sea monster Tiamat to make heaven and earth.
- Isaiah 51:9–10 merges this cosmic battle with the crossing of the Red Sea. **Rahab** as a sea monster also appears in Psalm 89:10 and Job 26:12.

## Points of Agreement

The poem's mythic imagery is well paralleled in Canaanite and Mesopotamian literature, which shows the text drawing on a shared ancient Near Eastern heritage.

## Points of Conflict

No historical claims are made, so there are no conflicts. The Red Sea crossing referred to in 51:10 is itself unattested outside the Bible (see Exodus 14).

## Assessment

For exiles living where Marduk's victory over Tiamat was celebrated every year, the claim that YHWH, not Marduk, had defeated the dragon was pointed polemic. The chapter is good evidence of the religious world of the Babylonian exile, not of the events it alludes to.
