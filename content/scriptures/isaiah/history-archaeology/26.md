## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 26 is a psalm-like song within the "Isaiah Apocalypse." It contains no historical claims. Historically it matters because 26:19 ("your dead shall live") is one of the earliest biblical texts to speak of the dead rising, a belief that developed in the Second Temple period.

## What the Text Claims

- "We have a strong city"; its gates open for the righteous nation (26:1–2).
- God brings down the lofty city (26:5).
- "Other lords besides you have ruled over us" (26:13).
- "The dead do not live; the shades (*rephaim*) do not rise" — spoken of Israel's oppressors (26:14).
- But: "**Your dead shall live; their corpses shall rise.** O dwellers in the dust, awake and sing! … the earth will give birth to the shades" (26:19).
- God's people should hide until his wrath passes (26:20–21).

## External Evidence

There is no archaeological evidence specific to this chapter. The historical question is how it fits the development of Israelite beliefs about death.

### Israelite views of death

Most of the Hebrew Bible describes the dead as going down to **Sheol**, a shadowy underworld from which no one returns (for example Ps 88:10–12; Job 14:12). The term **Rephaim** is used for the shades of the dead. In the Ugaritic texts the *rpum* are deified ancestors or heroes associated with the underworld. Judahite tombs show care for the dead (benches, provisions), but no evidence of belief in bodily resurrection.

### Resurrection in later texts

- **Daniel 12:2** (c. 165 BCE) is the clearest Hebrew Bible text on individual resurrection: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake." It appears to echo Isaiah 26:19 ("dwellers in the dust").
- **Ezekiel 37** (6th century BCE) uses the image of dry bones coming to life, but as a metaphor for the restoration of the nation.
- **2 Maccabees 7** and parts of **1 Enoch** develop resurrection belief further in the 2nd century BCE.

### Metaphor or literal resurrection?

Scholars debate whether 26:19 refers to the national revival of Israel (like Ezek 37) or to the literal raising of the dead. Either way, the language anticipates later Jewish and Christian belief and suggests a post-exilic date.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. Read as an 8th-century text, 26:19 would be anomalous within the history of Israelite religion. Read as Persian-period (or later), it fits a gradual development toward resurrection belief.

## Assessment

Chapter 26 is a communal song and lament from a community that has known oppression by "other lords" and longs for restoration. It is best dated with chapters 24–27 to the Persian period, though some scholars place 26:19 in particular later still. Its value for the historian lies in documenting a crucial stage in Jewish thought about death and hope.
