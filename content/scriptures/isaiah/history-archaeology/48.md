## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The chapter closes the Babylon-focused half of Deutero-Isaiah with the call "Go out from Babylon, flee from Chaldea" (48:20). It reflects the expectation of an imminent mass return around 539 BCE. Cuneiform archives show that many Judeans did not go out at all.

## What the Text Claims

- YHWH announced "the former things" long ago so that Israel could not credit its idols (48:3–8).
- He now announces "new things" never heard before (48:6–7).
- "The LORD loves him; he shall perform his purpose on Babylon" (48:14), meaning Cyrus.
- The exiles are to leave Babylon with joyful shouts, as in the exodus when water came from the rock (48:20–21).

## External Evidence

### Who left, and who stayed

- **Ezra 2** and **Nehemiah 7** list about 42,360 returnees plus servants. Many scholars think this list combines several waves of return over decades, not one caravan in 538 BCE.
- **Al-Yahudu tablets** (572–477 BCE): Judeans in "Judah-town" and nearby settlements kept doing business in Babylonia for at least 60 years after Cyrus's conquest, and many of them, including children born in exile, bear Yahwistic names built on the divine element *Yahu*.
- **Murashu archive** (Nippur, c. 455–403 BCE): Jewish names still appear among clients of this business house in the late 5th century.
- **Persian-period Yehud:** Surveys (Lipschits, Carter) show a small, thinly settled province. The population increase in the early Persian period was modest.

### The "argument from prophecy"

Chapter 48 insists that YHWH predicted earlier events and now announces new ones. Critical scholars take "former things" to refer to earlier prophecies (including those of Isaiah of Jerusalem) and past events such as the exile. "New things" refers to Cyrus and the return.

## Points of Agreement

- A Persian conquest of Babylon (48:14) did follow, and Judeans were given the opportunity to return.
- A real community of Judeans living in Babylonia, the audience of the chapter, is fully confirmed by cuneiform evidence.

## Points of Conflict

| Isaiah 48 | Historical record |
|-----------|-------------------|
| A dramatic exodus-style departure from Babylon | Return was gradual and partial, and a large Judean community stayed in Babylonia for centuries |
| Miraculous provision in the desert (48:21) | No external record. This is typological poetry modelled on the exodus |

## Assessment

The chapter is a real-time appeal to exiles, many of whom were comfortable in Babylonia and did not want to leave. Its rebukes ("you are obstinate," 48:4) may reflect that reluctance. Babylonian Jewry went on to become a major centre of Judaism, producing the Babylonian Talmud a thousand years later. So the call of 48:20 was answered only in part.
