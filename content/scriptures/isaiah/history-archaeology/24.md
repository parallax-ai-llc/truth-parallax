## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 24 opens the so-called **"Isaiah Apocalypse"** (chapters 24–27), a collection of cosmic poems with no clear 8th-century setting. Mainstream scholarship dates it much later than Isaiah, most often to the Persian period, though proposals range from the late 6th to the 2nd century BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- The LORD will empty the earth and lay it waste; everyone, from priest to people and master to slave, will suffer (24:1–3).
- The earth is polluted because its inhabitants have "broken the **everlasting covenant**" (24:5).
- The "**city of chaos**" (*qiryat tohu*) is broken down (24:10).
- Praise is heard "from the ends of the earth" (24:14–16).
- God will punish "the host of heaven in heaven and the kings of the earth on the earth," and will reign on Mount Zion (24:21–23).

## External Evidence

There is no archaeological evidence for this chapter. The debate is about date and genre.

### Why most scholars date it late

- **Scope:** Judgment is universal and cosmic, not directed at a particular nation. This is unlike the oracles against the nations in chapters 13–23 and characteristic of later, proto-apocalyptic literature.
- **Theology:** The punishment of heavenly powers (24:21) and their imprisonment "in a pit" (24:22) anticipate later Jewish angelology (for example in *1 Enoch*).
- **The "everlasting covenant"** (24:5) echoes the covenant with Noah and all humanity (Gen 9:16), a Priestly text widely dated to the exilic or post-exilic period.
- **The "city of chaos"** is never named. Proposals include Babylon (sacked by Sennacherib in 689 BCE and punished by Xerxes after a revolt c. 482 BCE), Samaria, Jerusalem, Tyre, and an unnamed symbol of the hostile world. The lack of a name is itself typical of later apocalyptic writing.

### Range of proposals

- **Late 6th century:** Some scholars (for example W. R. Millar) relate it to the fall of Babylon in 539 BCE.
- **5th–4th century (Persian period):** The majority view (for example Blenkinsopp, Wildberger, with variations).
- **Hellenistic or Maccabean:** Earlier critics such as Bernhard Duhm proposed dates as late as the 2nd century BCE. The presence of these chapters in the Great Isaiah Scroll (c. 150–100 BCE) makes a very late date difficult.

### Conservative view

Conservative scholars (for example Oswalt) regard chapters 24–27 as Isaiah's own climax to the oracles against the nations, showing God's universal sovereignty.

## Points of Agreement

- Not applicable. The chapter makes no claims about specific events.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. The question is whether the 8th-century prophet could have written it. Its genre and vocabulary point strongly to a later author.

## Assessment

Chapter 24 is best read as a composition of the Second Temple period, written to frame the preceding oracles against particular nations as a single, worldwide judgment. Calling these chapters an "apocalypse" is a modern convention: they lack visions mediated by angels and other features of full apocalypses like Daniel. But they show an early stage of the development toward apocalyptic thought.
