## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The fourth Servant Song (52:13–53:12) describes a figure who is despised, wounded "for our transgressions," killed and buried, and then vindicated. It contains no datable events. The historical question is whom its 6th-century author had in mind. Scholars have proposed several answers, and none can be proven.

## What the Text Claims

- The servant, once disfigured, will be exalted and startle kings (52:13–15).
- He was despised and rejected, "a man of sorrows," and bore the sins of others (53:3–6).
- He was led like a lamb to slaughter, "cut off from the land of the living," and given a grave with the wicked and the rich (53:7–9).
- After his suffering he "shall see offspring" and be satisfied (53:10–12).

## External Evidence

### Text-critical evidence

The **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a)** and the fragmentary **1QIsa^b** and **4QIsa^d** read in 53:11 "he shall see **light**," a reading shared by the Septuagint but missing in the Masoretic Text. Most modern translations adopt it. Otherwise the Qumran texts show that the passage was transmitted substantially as we have it by the 2nd century BCE, well before Christianity.

### Proposed historical referents

| Proposal | Basis | Main difficulty |
|----------|-------|-----------------|
| **Israel** (the exiled nation) | Elsewhere in Isaiah 40–55 the servant is Israel; the dominant medieval Jewish reading (Rashi, Ibn Ezra) | The servant suffers *for* others who confess "we" went astray, which sits awkwardly if the speakers are the nations and the sufferer is Israel |
| **The prophet himself** (Deutero-Isaiah) | Continues the individual voice of 49:1–6 and 50:4–9; the song may be a disciples' tribute after his death (Whybray, Westermann, others) | No external evidence about the prophet's life or death |
| **Jehoiachin** | Exiled king of Judah, imprisoned in Babylon; Babylonian ration tablets (Weidner, 1939) list "Ya'ukin, king of the land of Yahudu" and his sons receiving oil; released in 562 BCE (2 Kings 25:27–30) | He was not killed, and nothing suggests vicarious suffering |
| **Zerubbabel** | Davidic governor of Yehud c. 520 BCE; proposed by Ernst Sellin, who later changed his view (he also suggested Moses) | Chronologically late for Isaiah 40–55, and there is no record of his death |
| **Moses or another past figure** | Typology of the suffering intercessor | Speculative |
| **A future, messianic figure** | Some ancient Jewish sources (Targum Jonathan) apply the passage to the Messiah, though the Targum transfers the suffering to others; Christian interpretation applies it to Jesus (Acts 8:32–35) | A theological reading, not a historical referent for the original author |

## Points of Agreement

- The imagery of exile, imprisonment and shame matches the experience of the Judean exiles, including their captive king.
- The Jehoiachin ration tablets are a real piece of evidence for a Judean royal figure who suffered in Babylon and was later "lifted up" (2 Kings 25:27). That is why some scholars see him as at least partly behind the imagery.

## Points of Conflict

None historically, since the text makes no checkable claims. The traditional Christian claim that the passage is a specific prediction of Jesus's passion is a matter of faith. Historically, the author wrote for an audience in the 6th century BCE.

## Assessment

The most widely held critical views are that the servant is either Israel (or a faithful core of Israel) or the prophet himself, and that the song was composed in the exilic or early post-exilic period. Duhm's idea that the four songs form a separate collection has been strongly challenged (Mettinger), though the passage is clearly distinctive. The history of interpretation of Isaiah 53 is itself an important chapter in Jewish–Christian relations.
