## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The closing chapter of Deutero-Isaiah invites the exiles to come and "buy without money" and transfers the covenant promises to David to the people as a whole (55:3). Its significance for history is what it implies about the end of the Davidic monarchy.

## What the Text Claims

- Everyone who thirsts may come and eat without cost (55:1–2).
- YHWH will make with the people "an everlasting covenant, my steadfast, sure love for David" (55:3).
- As David was a witness to the peoples, so the people will summon nations (55:4–5).
- The exiles will "go out in joy," with mountains singing and the desert flourishing (55:12–13).

## External Evidence

### The Davidic line after 586 BCE

- **Jehoiachin**, the last Davidic king to rule (briefly in 597 BCE), is attested in **Babylonian ration tablets** as "king of the land of Yahudu" living in Babylon. He was released from prison in 562 BCE (2 Kings 25:27–30).
- His grandson **Zerubbabel** became governor of Yehud c. 520 BCE. **Haggai 2:20–23** and **Zechariah 4; 6:9–15** raised royal hopes around him. He then disappears from the record.
- A bulla and seal from a Persian-period archive published by N. Avigad (1976) name "**Shelomith**, maidservant of Elnathan the governor." Some scholars, notably Eric Meyers, identify her with Zerubbabel's daughter Shelomith (1 Chronicles 3:19). The identification is possible but uncertain.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter's silence about a restored Davidic king matches the historical outcome: Yehud was governed by Persian appointees and high priests, not kings.

## Points of Conflict

None directly. The chapter's position differs from the royal hopes of Haggai and Zechariah, which shows that the post-exilic community held competing ideas about the future of the monarchy.

## Assessment

By "democratizing" the Davidic covenant, Isaiah 55 reflects a realistic acceptance that the monarchy would not return under Persian rule. That fits an exilic author c. 540 BCE and anticipates the political reality of the Persian period. The joyful exodus of 55:12 was, as noted for chapter 48, only partly realized.
