## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy); Low as prediction.** Chapter 60 imagines nations streaming to a glorified Jerusalem, bringing gold, incense and flocks for the Temple. The geography of its Arabian peoples is accurate for the 6th–5th centuries BCE. The reality of Persian-period Jerusalem was a small, poor Temple town.

## What the Text Claims

- "Arise, shine, for your light has come" (60:1). Nations and kings come to Zion's light (60:3).
- Camels of **Midian and Ephah** and caravans from **Sheba** bring gold and frankincense (60:6).
- Flocks of **Kedar** and rams of **Nebaioth** are offered on the altar, and YHWH will glorify "my glorious house" (60:7).
- Ships of **Tarshish** bring sons home with silver and gold (60:9).
- Foreigners will rebuild the walls, and the gates will stay open day and night (60:10–11).

## External Evidence

### Arabian peoples and the incense trade

- **Sheba** (Saba) in south Arabia controlled frankincense and myrrh exports. Sabaean inscriptions and the trade routes through Arabia are well documented from the early 1st millennium BCE.
- **Kedar** (Qedar) and **Nebaioth** (Nabayati) appear together in the annals of **Ashurbanipal** (7th century BCE) as north Arabian peoples. Whether Nebaioth are the later **Nabataeans** is debated, since the names are spelled differently.
- **Ephah** may correspond to the Ḫayappa, listed among Arabian groups in the inscriptions of Tiglath-pileser III and Sargon II.

### The Temple and the "treasures of the nations"

- **Haggai 2:6–9** (520 BCE) promises that "the treasures of all nations" will fill the new Temple and that its glory will exceed the former. Isaiah 60 shares this hope.
- **Ezra 3:12** and **Haggai 2:3** admit that older people wept, because the new Temple seemed like nothing compared to Solomon's.
- **Ezra 6:8–10; 7:15–22** report Persian royal funding for the Temple. Such support is plausible but these Aramaic documents are debated as to authenticity.

## Points of Agreement

- The incense and camel caravans of Arabia are historically accurate.
- The existence (or rebuilding) of a Temple at the centre of the community fits the post-515 BCE situation.

## Points of Conflict

| Isaiah 60 | Historical record |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Kings and nations bring their wealth to Jerusalem | Yehud paid tribute to Persia, not the other way round (Nehemiah 5:4; 9:36–37) |
| Foreigners rebuild the walls (60:10) | Nehemiah's builders faced foreign opposition (Nehemiah 4) |
| A splendid Temple | The Second Temple was modest until Herod's rebuilding (from c. 20 BCE) |

## Assessment

Chapter 60 is utopian prophecy shaped by Persian-period hopes. Its picture of Jerusalem as the wealthy centre of the world contrasts strongly with archaeological evidence for a small Persian-period town. Later readers saw it partially fulfilled in Herod's Temple, and Revelation 21 reuses its imagery.
