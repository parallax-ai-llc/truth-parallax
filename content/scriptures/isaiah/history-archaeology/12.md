## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry).** Chapter 12 is a short hymn of praise with no historical claims. Its significance is literary and redactional: it closes the first major section of the book (chapters 1–12) and is generally dated after the exile.

## What the Text Claims

- "In that day" the redeemed will thank God because his anger has turned away (12:1).
- "The LORD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation" (12:2).
- "With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation" (12:3).
- The nations should hear of God's deeds, because "great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel" (12:4–6).

## External Evidence

There is no archaeological evidence relevant to this chapter. The arguments concern its date and sources.

### Dependence on other biblical texts

- **12:2b** repeats almost word for word the **Song of the Sea** (Exod 15:2): "The LORD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation." It also appears in Psalm 118:14.
- **12:4** closely parallels Psalm 105:1 ("Give thanks to the LORD, call on his name, make known his deeds among the peoples"), which is also quoted in 1 Chronicles 16:8.

Psalm 105 and the Chronicler's version of it are generally dated to the Persian period. Isaiah 12 uses the language of temple liturgy, of the kind that circulated widely in the Second Temple period.

### Exodus typology

The allusion to the Song of the Sea links chapter 12 to the "second Exodus" in 11:15–16, where God dries up the sea and makes a highway for the returning remnant. The same theme is central to chapters 40–55.

## Points of Agreement

- Not applicable. The chapter makes no historical claims.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. The issue is authorship: almost all critical scholars regard the chapter as an editorial composition, not the work of the 8th-century prophet.

## Assessment

Chapter 12 is best understood as a liturgical conclusion added by later editors to round off the collection of chapters 1–12. Just as Exodus 15 celebrates deliverance from Egypt, this hymn celebrates the hoped-for deliverance and return of the scattered people. It shows that the book of Isaiah was shaped for use in worship by the post-exilic community. Conservative scholars read it as Isaiah's own anticipation of future praise, but the dependence on psalm language points to a later setting.
