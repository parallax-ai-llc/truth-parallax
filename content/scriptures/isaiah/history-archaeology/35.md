## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 35 is a poem of restoration with no historical claims. Its language and themes are so close to chapters 40–55 that most scholars regard it as exilic or post-exilic, either by the same circle as Deutero-Isaiah or by an editor linking the two halves of the book.

## What the Text Claims

- The wilderness will rejoice and blossom; it will receive "the glory of Lebanon, the splendour of Carmel and Sharon" (35:1–2).
- "Strengthen the weak hands … Say to those who are anxious: Be strong, fear not! Behold, your God will come" (35:3–4).
- The blind will see, the deaf hear, and the lame leap; water will break out in the desert (35:5–7).
- A highway called **the Way of Holiness** will lead the redeemed (35:8–9).
- "The ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to **Zion** with singing" (35:10).

## External Evidence

There is no archaeological evidence directly relevant to this chapter. The arguments concern its date and literary relationships.

### Parallels with Isaiah 40–55

- **35:10** is repeated almost word for word in **51:11**.
- **A highway in the desert** for God's people (35:8) corresponds to 40:3 ("prepare the way of the LORD … make straight in the desert a highway") and 43:19.
- **Water in the wilderness** (35:6–7) matches 41:18 and 43:20.
- **"Behold your God"** (35:4) corresponds to 40:9.
- **Strengthening weak hands** and "do not fear" are characteristic phrases of chapters 40–55.

### The return from exile

The "ransomed" returning to Zion presupposes a community in exile, most naturally the Judahite exiles in Babylonia. Persian policy under Cyrus allowed some exiles to return after 539 BCE (Ezra 1; the Cyrus Cylinder records a general policy of returning displaced peoples and gods, though it does not mention the Jews). The return was smaller and harder than the triumphant procession imagined here.

### Position in the book

Chapter 35 is followed by the prose narrative of chapters 36–39, which is taken from 2 Kings. Many scholars think chapters 34–35 originally led straight into chapter 40, and that chapters 36–39 were inserted between them.

## Points of Agreement

- Not applicable. The poem expresses hope rather than describing events.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. Read as an 8th-century prophecy, the chapter would presuppose an exile that had not yet happened in Judah. Read as an exilic text, it fits naturally.

## Assessment

Chapter 35 is one of the clearest bridges between First and Second Isaiah. Its vocabulary, imagery, and theology belong to the circle that produced chapters 40–55. Its placement before the Hezekiah narratives shows that the book was consciously arranged by editors, who wanted readers to move from judgment on Edom (ch. 34) to the hope of return (ch. 35) before the story of Jerusalem's deliverance.
