## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (liturgy/prophecy).** Chapter 33 mixes prayer, lament, and oracle. Some details (a broken treaty, foreign officials counting towers) fit the Assyrian crisis of 701 BCE, but most scholars think the chapter was composed later, possibly as a bridge between the two halves of the book.

## What the Text Claims

- Woe to the destroyer who has not been destroyed, the betrayer who has not been betrayed (33:1).
- A prayer: "O LORD, be gracious to us" (33:2).
- "The highways lie waste … **The treaty is broken**; its oaths are despised" (33:8).
- "Where is he who counted, where is he who weighed the tribute? Where is he who counted the towers?" You will no longer see "a people of an obscure speech" (33:18–19).
- Zion will be a quiet habitation, an immovable tent (33:20). The LORD is our king (33:22).

## External Evidence

### A broken treaty

2 Kings 18:14–16 records that Hezekiah submitted to Sennacherib at Lachish and paid tribute, yet the Assyrians still demanded Jerusalem's surrender (2 Kgs 18:17). Some scholars read 33:7–8 as a reflection of this sense of betrayal. Sennacherib's annals mention the tribute but present it as paid after the campaign.

### Assyrian officials

The "one who counted" and "one who weighed" (33:18) fit Assyrian administrators assessing tribute and fortifications. The "people of obscure speech" (33:19) echoes the foreign-language motif of 28:11 and suggests Assyrian occupiers.

### Place in the Great Isaiah Scroll

In the **Great Isaiah Scroll** (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>), there is a noticeable blank space after chapter 33 before chapter 34 begins in a new column. W. H. Brownlee and others have argued that the scroll was deliberately divided into two halves (chapters 1–33 and 34–66). This shows that ancient scribes did not treat chapters 39/40 as the main dividing line, though the physical division may reflect copying practice rather than ideas about authorship.

## Points of Agreement

- The motifs of tribute assessment, foreign speech, and broken agreements fit the Assyrian period.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. But the chapter's liturgical form and its use of themes from across the book (Zion's security, God as king) point to a later editor rather than the 8th-century prophet.

## Assessment

Opinion is divided. Some scholars (for example Wildberger) date chapter 33 to the post-exilic period, as a liturgy composed to sum up the hopes of chapters 28–32. Others see a mixture of older material, perhaps going back to 701 BCE, and later framing. A few earlier critics proposed a Maccabean date, now generally abandoned. What is clear is that the chapter serves as a hinge between the Assyrian oracles and the later material that follows.
