## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High (for the political setting).** The crisis behind this chapter — an attack on Judah by Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel, c. 734 BCE — is confirmed by Assyrian inscriptions, which name all three kings involved. The prophecy that the threat would soon pass was borne out by 732 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- In Ahaz's reign, **Rezin king of Aram** and **Pekah son of Remaliah, king of Israel**, attacked Jerusalem but could not take it (7:1; compare 2 Kgs 16:5).
- They planned to replace Ahaz with "the son of Tabeel" (7:6).
- Isaiah met Ahaz "at the end of the conduit of the upper pool" and told him not to fear (7:3–9).
- A young woman (*'almah*) will bear a son named **Immanuel**. Before he knows good from evil, the two kings' lands will be deserted (7:14–16).
- God will shave Judah with "a razor hired beyond the River — the king of Assyria" (7:17–25).

## External Evidence

### Tiglath-pileser III's inscriptions

- **Ahaz:** A summary inscription of Tiglath-pileser III lists **"Jehoahaz of Judah"** (*Iauḫazi Iaudaya*) among western kings who paid tribute, around 734 BCE. "Ahaz" is a shortened form of the same name. This fits 2 Kings 16:7–9, where Ahaz pays Tiglath-pileser to rescue him.
- **Rezin:** The Assyrian texts call the king of Damascus **Raḫianu**. They record his defeat, the siege of Damascus, and the devastation of its territory in 733–732 BCE. 2 Kings 16:9 adds that Rezin was killed.
- **Pekah:** Another summary inscription says that the people of Israel "overthrew **Paqaḫa** their king" and that Tiglath-pileser installed **Hoshea** (*A-ú-si-'*) in his place. 2 Kings 15:30 says Hoshea assassinated Pekah.

### The Upper Pool

The "conduit of the upper pool" is mentioned again in 36:2 as the place where the Assyrian Rabshakeh stood. It was probably part of the water system near the Gihon Spring, which was the focus of Jerusalem's defensive preparations. Its exact location is not certain.

### "Son of Tabeel"

The identity of this pretender is unknown. Proposals include an Aramean or Transjordanian noble. No external text names him.

## Points of Agreement

| Isaiah 7 | External record |
|----------|-----------------|
| Rezin and Pekah allied against Judah | Both named as anti-Assyrian rulers in Tiglath-pileser III's texts |
| Ahaz turns away from trusting God (and, in Kings, to Assyria) | "Jehoahaz of Judah" pays tribute to Assyria |
| Damascus and Israel will soon be laid waste | Damascus falls in 732; Israel loses Galilee and Gilead in 733–732 and Pekah is overthrown |

## Points of Conflict

- **7:8b** ("within sixty-five years Ephraim will be shattered") breaks the poetic parallelism and is usually seen as a later gloss. It may refer to Assyrian resettlements in Samaria under Esarhaddon or Ashurbanipal (Ezra 4:2, 10).
- **"The king of Assyria"** in 7:17 and 7:20 is widely regarded as an explanatory addition.

## Assessment

Isaiah 7 is one of the best-anchored passages in the prophetic books. All the actors are attested in Assyrian sources, and the sequence of events is coherent with 2 Kings 15–16.

The Immanuel sign was a sign for **Ahaz's own time**. The child's growth measures how soon the threat would end. Most historians identify him with a child born in the 730s, perhaps a son of Ahaz (such as Hezekiah) or of Isaiah. The Hebrew *'almah* means a young woman of marriageable age. The Septuagint rendered it *parthenos* ("virgin"), and Matthew 1:23 follows the Greek. That later messianic reading is a matter of interpretation, not of the 8th-century historical setting.
