## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy), with a credible historical backdrop.** Chapter 1 is a collection of oracles, not a narrative. Its picture of a ravaged countryside with Jerusalem alone surviving (1:7–9) fits the aftermath of Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE very well.

## What the Text Claims

- Isaiah son of Amoz prophesied in the reigns of **Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah** (1:1), roughly 740–700 BCE.
- The land is desolate, its cities burned, and "strangers devour your land" (1:7).
- "Daughter Zion" is left "like a booth in a vineyard … like a besieged city" (1:8). Only a small remnant has survived (1:9).
- Sacrifices and festivals are condemned while injustice continues (1:10–17). Zion will be purged and restored (1:21–31).

## External Evidence

### The kings of the superscription

All four kings are known from the Bible's own chronology, and two are confirmed by Assyrian texts: **Ahaz** ("Jehoahaz of Judah," in a tribute list of Tiglath-pileser III) and **Hezekiah** (in Sennacherib's annals and on a royal bulla from the Ophel published in 2015). Uzziah's reign is attested indirectly by a later Aramaic burial plaque ("Hither were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah"), which dates to the Second Temple period and records a reburial.

### Sennacherib's campaign (701 BCE)

Sennacherib's annals (Rassam Cylinder; Taylor and Oriental Institute Prisms) report that he captured **46 fortified cities** of Hezekiah, deported 200,150 people, and confined Hezekiah in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." They add that he gave parts of Hezekiah's territory to the Philistine kings of Ashdod, Ekron, and Gaza. That detail matches "strangers devour your land in your very presence" (1:7). The destruction of Lachish Level III and many other Judahite sites in the Shephelah confirms the scale of the devastation.

## Points of Agreement

- A Judah whose countryside was wasted while Jerusalem alone escaped fits 701 BCE exactly. No other event in Isaiah's lifetime matches as well.
- Criticism of lavish temple worship alongside social injustice suits a prosperous 8th-century Judah with growing social inequality, which archaeologists infer from settlement patterns and royal administrative remains.

## Points of Conflict

- None of substance. The chapter makes few verifiable claims.
- The superscription (1:1) is an editorial heading for the *whole* book. It is accurate for the 8th-century oracles but not for chapters 40–66, which were written much later (see the overview).

## Assessment

Most scholars regard chapter 1 as a deliberately arranged **introduction to the whole book**, assembled by editors from oracles of different dates. Verses 4–9 are widely accepted as genuine Isaianic words from just after 701 BCE. Their stance is striking: Jerusalem's survival is not presented as a triumph (as in chapters 36–37) but as a narrow escape from deserved judgment. That difference in tone is itself evidence that the book preserves more than one perspective on the same event.

Verses 27–31 (Zion redeemed by justice, the shame of sacred groves) are often attributed to later editors because they link to themes in chapters 56–66.
