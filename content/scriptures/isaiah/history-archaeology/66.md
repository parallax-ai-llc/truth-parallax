## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** The final chapter opens by questioning the need for a Temple ("What is the house that you would build for me?" 66:1) and closes with nations gathering to Jerusalem and the corpses of rebels left outside the city. It reflects debates around the rebuilding of the Temple (520–515 BCE) and the divided post-exilic community. Its closing themes also echo chapter 1, framing the whole book.

## What the Text Claims

- "Heaven is my throne and the earth is my footstool; what is the house that you would build for me?" (66:1).
- Sacrificing an ox is like killing a man; sacrificing a lamb is like breaking a dog's neck (66:3).
- Zion gives birth to a nation in one day (66:7–9).
- Those who eat pork, "the abomination and the mouse," will perish (66:17).
- Survivors will be sent to Tarshish, Put, Lud, Tubal, Javan and distant coastlands, and some of the nations will be taken as priests and Levites (66:19–21).
- The book ends with the worm and fire of the corpses of rebels (66:24).

## External Evidence

### The Temple debate

- **Haggai 1:2–11** (520 BCE) rebukes the people for saying "the time has not yet come" to rebuild YHWH's house. Ezra 4–6 tells of the rebuilding, completed in 515 BCE.
- Scholars disagree on whether 66:1 opposes rebuilding in principle, opposes the people building it, or simply denies that any Temple can contain God (compare 1 Kings 8:27). Either way, it belongs to a period when building a Temple was a live question.

### Dogs and pigs

- The comparison with "breaking a dog's neck" (66:3) is unusual. At **Ashkelon**, Lawrence Stager's team found more than 1,000 dog burials from the Persian period (5th century BCE), probably connected to a Phoenician healing cult. A connection to Isaiah 66:3 has been suggested but is speculative.
- Eating pork and mice as signs of apostasy (66:17) matches chapter 65.

### The nations list

The peoples in 66:19 match names in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10): Put (Libya), Lud (Lydia), Tubal (Anatolia) and Javan (Ionian Greeks). Where the Hebrew has "who draw the bow" (*moshke qeshet*), the Septuagint reads "Meshech," another Anatolian people. Lydia and the Greeks became better known in Judah after Cyrus's conquest of western Anatolia. The Masoretic Text reads "Pul" for "Put," a probable copying error corrected by the Septuagint.

### Gehenna

The final verse lies behind later Jewish and Christian ideas of eternal punishment. The rebels' corpses are outside Jerusalem, probably in the Valley of Hinnom, which later gave its name to **Gehenna** (compare Mark 9:48, which quotes 66:24).

## Points of Agreement

- The Temple debate in 66:1 fits the rebuilding of 520–515 BCE.
- The list of distant peoples matches the expanded horizons of the Persian empire.

## Points of Conflict

None historically testable. The idea of foreigners becoming priests and Levites (66:21) is never reported as realized in the Second Temple period and contrasts with the genealogical checks of Ezra 2:62.

## Assessment

Chapter 66 is a post-exilic composition, probably later than 515 BCE in its final form. Many scholars see it as one of the last parts of the book to be written. Its links to chapter 1 (corrupt sacrifices, rebels, the Holy One judging his people, gardens, fire) form an **inclusio** around the entire book, pointing to deliberate editing of Isaiah as a single literary work.

**Conservative view.** Oswalt and Motyer see this framing as evidence of single authorship. **Critical reply:** most scholars (e.g., Williamson, Sweeney, Childs) see it as evidence of a long editorial process that intentionally gave the book unity while combining materials written over two or more centuries, from the 8th century to the late 6th or 5th century BCE. Brevard Childs's "canonical" approach in particular takes the final, unified book seriously as a theological whole while accepting its multiple origins.
