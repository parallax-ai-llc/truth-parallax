## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** The chapter has the second Servant Song (49:1–6) and a lament and reply for Zion (49:14–26). Its historical interest lies in the identity of the servant, a geographical clue from the Dead Sea Scrolls ("Syene"), and its picture of a depopulated Jerusalem.

## What the Text Claims

- The servant was called from the womb and told, "You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3).
- Yet the servant's mission is to bring Jacob back to YHWH and be "a light to the nations" (49:5–6).
- Exiles will come from far away, "and these from the land of Sinim" (49:12).
- Zion complains she is forsaken; her ruined land will be too small for the returning population (49:14–21).

## External Evidence

### "Sinim" or "Syene"

The Masoretic Text reads *Sînîm*, once taken as China. The **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a)** reads *swnyym*, "Syenites," people of **Syene** (modern Aswan) at Egypt's southern border. Most modern translations follow this reading. Syene and the nearby island of **Elephantine** hosted a Judean military colony, whose 5th-century Aramaic papyri describe a temple to YHW and contacts with Jerusalem. The colony probably existed before 525 BCE, since the papyri say their temple stood when Cambyses invaded Egypt.

### Who is the servant?

- **Israel collectively:** the text names the servant "Israel" in 49:3, and the word is present in the Masoretic Text and 1QIsa^a. Only one medieval Hebrew manuscript omits it.
- **The prophet himself:** the servant has a mission *to* Israel (49:5–6), which suggests an individual, perhaps the anonymous prophet, speaking about his own call.
- **An ideal or faithful remnant of Israel** charged with restoring the rest is a common compromise view.

### Jerusalem in ruins

Archaeological surveys show that Jerusalem and its immediate surroundings were largely depopulated after 586 BCE, while the area of Benjamin to the north (Mizpah/Tell en-Nasbeh, Gibeon) stayed relatively intact. Zion's "desolate places" and "devastated land" (49:19) fit this picture.

## Points of Agreement

- A Judean diaspora stretching from Babylonia to southern Egypt is well documented.
- Zion as a ruined, depopulated city is accurate for the mid-6th century.

## Points of Conflict

None substantive. The promise that returnees will overcrowd the land (49:19–20) was not fulfilled in the Persian period. Yehud remained thinly populated for a long time.

## Assessment

The "Syene" reading is a fine example of the Dead Sea Scrolls clarifying the geography of a biblical text. The servant question is not one history can settle. On the evidence of the text, the servant is described both as Israel and as someone sent to Israel, and scholars disagree on how to resolve that tension.
