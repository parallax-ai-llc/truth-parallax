## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High (for the setting).** The chapter opens with a warning to Samaria that must predate its fall in 722–720 BCE, and continues with an attack on Jerusalem's leaders for making a "covenant with death," usually understood as the alliance with Egypt against Assyria in 705–701 BCE. Both settings are well documented.

## What the Text Claims

- Woe to "the proud crown of the drunkards of **Ephraim**," the city set on a hill above a fertile valley (Samaria). A mighty one from the Lord will trample it (28:1–4).
- Priests and prophets reel with wine; they mock Isaiah's teaching as babble: *tsav latsav, tsav latsav, qav laqav* (28:7–10).
- God will speak to them "by people of strange lips and another tongue" (28:11).
- Jerusalem's rulers have made "a **covenant with death**" and "an agreement with Sheol," trusting in lies (28:14–15).
- God lays in Zion a tested, precious **cornerstone** (28:16). The covenant with death will be annulled (28:18).
- God will act as on Mount **Perazim** and in the valley of **Gibeon** (28:21).

## External Evidence

### The fall of Samaria

Samaria sits on a hill above a fertile valley, matching the "crown" image. Assyrian sources record its capture: the Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 1) credits **Shalmaneser V** (727–722 BCE) with ravaging Samaria, while **Sargon II** claims to have conquered it and deported **27,290** people (27,280 in some texts). Excavations at Samaria by Harvard (1908–1910) and by a joint expedition under J. W. Crowfoot (1931–1935) uncovered the royal acropolis, the **Samaria ivories**, and the **Samaria ostraca** recording deliveries of wine and oil — appropriate for a "drunkard" elite.

### The "covenant with death"

After Sargon's death in 705 BCE, Hezekiah joined a revolt supported by Egypt (see chapters 30–31). Many scholars think "covenant with death" is Isaiah's mocking name for this treaty. Some suggest a pun: *Mot* (Death) was a Canaanite god, and Egyptian deities connected with the underworld may also be in view. The phrase may also allude to treaties sworn before gods.

### "Strange lips and another tongue"

This fits the Assyrians, whose Akkadian language would be foreign to Judahites, though the Rabshakeh of chapter 36 speaks Hebrew. Paul later quotes the verse about speaking in tongues (1 Cor 14:21).

### Perazim and Gibeon

These recall David's victories over the Philistines (2 Sam 5:20, 25; 1 Chr 14:16), showing Isaiah drew on Jerusalem's royal traditions.

## Points of Agreement

| Isaiah 28 | External record |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Samaria's fall predicted | Taken by Shalmaneser V / Sargon II, 722–720 BCE |
| Samaria's luxury and wine | Samaria ivories and ostraca |
| A treaty Jerusalem trusts in | Hezekiah's anti-Assyrian alliance with Egypt, 705–701 BCE |

## Points of Conflict

- None of substance. The oracle against Samaria (28:1–4) appears to have been reused later against Jerusalem (28:7–22), showing how Isaiah or his editors applied older words to new situations.

## Assessment

Chapter 28 opens the "woe" collection (chapters 28–33), much of which most scholars regard as authentic Isaianic material from 705–701 BCE. The political situation it presupposes — Judah betting on Egypt against Assyria — is exactly what Assyrian sources describe. The prophet's view that this alliance would fail was confirmed when Sennacherib defeated the Egyptian army at Eltekeh in 701 BCE.
