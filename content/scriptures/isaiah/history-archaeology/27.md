## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 27 closes the "Isaiah Apocalypse." Its opening verse contains one of the clearest literary links between the Hebrew Bible and the texts of Ugarit: a description of **Leviathan** that closely matches the Ugaritic description of **Lotan**, written more than five centuries earlier.

## What the Text Claims

- "On that day the LORD with his hard, great and strong sword will punish **Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent**, and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea" (27:1).
- A new song of the vineyard: God now guards his vineyard (27:2–6), reversing the judgment of 5:1–7.
- Jacob's guilt will be expiated when altar stones are crushed and Asherim removed (27:9).
- The fortified city is desolate (27:10).
- A great trumpet will gather the lost from **Assyria** and the exiled from **Egypt** to worship on the holy mountain at Jerusalem (27:12–13).

## External Evidence

### Lotan in the Baal Cycle

In the Ugaritic **Baal Cycle** (KTU 1.5 I 1–3), Mot reminds Baal of his victory: "When you struck **Lotan the fleeing serpent**, destroyed **the twisting serpent**, the tyrant with seven heads." Isaiah 27:1 uses the same two epithets, *bariaḥ* ("fleeing") and *'aqallaton* ("twisting"), for Leviathan. Lotan and Leviathan are the same name in different forms. The Ugaritic tablets date to the 14th–13th centuries BCE.

This is one of the closest verbal parallels between Ugaritic and biblical literature. It shows that the myth of the storm god defeating the sea dragon (the *Chaoskampf*) was known in Israel and reused in Hebrew poetry (compare Ps 74:13–14; Job 26:12–13; Isa 51:9).

### Asherim and altars

The removal of Asherah poles and destruction of altars (27:9) reflect a polemic against non-centralised worship, a theme associated with Deuteronomistic reforms (2 Kgs 18:4; 23:4–15). Archaeological finds of Asherah inscriptions (Kuntillet 'Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom) and dismantled shrines (Arad) show such worship was real.

### Return from Assyria and Egypt

The ingathering of exiles from Assyria and Egypt (27:12–13) presupposes a diaspora in both regions. Israelite deportees lived in Assyria after 732 and 722 BCE, and Judahite communities were established in Egypt after 587 BCE (Jer 43–44; the Elephantine papyri).

## Points of Agreement

- The Leviathan imagery has a direct, documented antecedent in Ugaritic myth.
- The two-region diaspora is historically real for the exilic and post-exilic periods.

## Points of Conflict

- None external. As with chapters 24–26, an 8th-century date is unlikely.

## Assessment

Chapter 27 brings together ancient myth and post-exilic hope. Leviathan, probably standing for hostile world powers, will be defeated as Baal once defeated Lotan. Israel's dispersion will end. The chapter's dependence on Ugaritic mythic language does not indicate an early date: such imagery survived in Israelite poetry for centuries. Its universal scope and diaspora perspective place it with the rest of the "Isaiah Apocalypse" in the Persian period.
