## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (poetry/prophecy).** Chapter 25 continues the "Isaiah Apocalypse." It contains no verifiable events. Its most important historical feature is its use of older Canaanite mythology: the image of God "swallowing up death" reverses the role of **Mot**, the god Death, known from the texts of Ugarit.

## What the Text Claims

- A song of thanks: God has made a city a heap of ruins, a fortified city a ruin (25:1–5).
- On "this mountain" the LORD will make for all peoples a feast of rich food and aged wine (25:6).
- He will destroy the veil spread over all nations and **swallow up death forever**; he will wipe away tears from all faces (25:7–8).
- Moab will be trampled down like straw in a dung-pit (25:10–12).

## External Evidence

### Mot, the swallower

In the **Baal Cycle** from Ugarit (14th–13th centuries BCE), Mot (Death) is a god with an enormous appetite. He swallows Baal, and his throat is described as stretching from earth to heaven. Hebrew poetry uses the same image of Sheol or death opening its mouth (Isa 5:14; Hab 2:5; Prov 1:12). Isaiah 25:8 reverses the myth: the LORD will swallow up Death. Scholars such as John Day and Mark S. Smith have shown how Israelite poetry adapted this Canaanite material.

### The divine banquet

In Ugaritic texts Baal holds a feast for the gods after his palace is built, and El gives a banquet in the *Marzeah* text. Feasts celebrating a god's kingship are a common Near Eastern motif. Isaiah 25:6 extends the banquet to all peoples, a theme later developed in Jewish and Christian expectations of the messianic banquet.

### The ruined city and Moab

As in chapter 24, the destroyed city (25:2) is unnamed. The sudden attack on **Moab** (25:10–12) may reflect a later scribe's specific hostility, as Moab became a symbol of Israel's enemies. It cannot be tied to a known event.

## Points of Agreement

- The mythological background is well documented in the Ugaritic texts.

## Points of Conflict

- None external.
- If the chapter were 8th-century, its universalism and its hope for the end of death would be unusual for that time. These ideas are better attested in later texts.

## Assessment

Chapter 25 is most plausibly dated, with the rest of chapters 24–27, to the Persian period. Its significance for history is in the history of ideas. It provides one of the earliest biblical expressions of hope that death itself will end (25:8), taken up later in Daniel 12:2 and quoted by Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:54. It also shows how deeply Israelite religious imagery drew on older Canaanite myth, recast to celebrate the LORD's supremacy.
