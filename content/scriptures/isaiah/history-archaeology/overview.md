## Overview

This page does not read Isaiah as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the events and setting Isaiah describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

Isaiah is mostly prophetic poetry, not narrative, so the question has to be put in two ways. First, *when* were the different parts written, and what world do they presuppose? Second, where the book does make concrete historical claims — kings, campaigns, sieges, envoys — how do they compare with the record?

The answer of mainstream critical scholarship is that the book is a **library spanning roughly four centuries**. Its core goes back to a real Jerusalem prophet of the late 8th century BCE, and its picture of the Assyrian crisis is among the best-corroborated material in the Hebrew Bible. Much of the rest was written by later anonymous prophets and editors during and after the Babylonian exile.

## The Question of Authorship

### First Isaiah (chapters 1–39)

The superscription (1:1) places Isaiah in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, roughly 740–700 BCE. The oracles that best fit this setting — the call vision (ch. 6), the Syro-Ephraimite crisis (ch. 7–8), the woes against Assyria and against reliance on Egypt (ch. 10, 28–31) — use names, places, and politics that match Assyrian records closely.

But chapters 1–39 are not all from one hand. Critical scholars widely identify later material:

- **Chapters 13–14 and 21:1–10** foresee Babylon's fall to the Medes, a 6th-century concern.
- **Chapters 24–27**, the so-called "Isaiah Apocalypse," are cosmic and proto-apocalyptic, with no 8th-century setting. Most date them to the Persian period or later.
- **Chapters 34–35** share their vocabulary and outlook with chapters 40–66, and chapter 34's hatred of Edom fits the period after 587 BCE.
- **Chapters 36–39** are prose narrative taken over, with small changes, from **2 Kings 18:13–20:19** (minus Hezekiah's tribute payment in 2 Kgs 18:14–16 and with the added "Psalm of Hezekiah," Isa 38:9–20).
- Shorter additions (for example 2:2–4, shared with Micah 4:1–3; 4:2–6; 11:10–16; 12; 19:16–25) reflect exile and diaspora.

### Deutero-Isaiah (chapters 40–55)

Chapters 40–55 never mention Isaiah, Assyria as a live threat, or Hezekiah. They address exiles in Babylon whose city lies ruined (44:26) and announce their release by **Cyrus**, named at 44:28 and 45:1. Cyrus conquered Media c. 550 BCE and Babylon in 539 BCE. Since the oracles know of his rise but not the details of Babylon's fall, most scholars date them to c. 550–539 BCE. This view goes back to J. C. Döderlein (1775) and J. G. Eichhorn (1780s) and was established by Bernhard Duhm's commentary (1892).

### Trito-Isaiah (chapters 56–66)

Chapters 56–66 presuppose Jews back in Judah, disputes over the rebuilt Temple, Sabbath-keeping, and foreigners in the community. Most scholars date them to the late 6th or 5th century BCE, the period of Haggai, Zechariah, and Ezra–Nehemiah. Duhm also proposed this division.

### The conservative case

Conservative scholars (for example O. T. Allis, E. J. Young, J. N. Oswalt, J. A. Motyer) defend single authorship. They point to vocabulary shared across the whole book, especially the title "the Holy One of Israel"; to the unanimous ancient attribution to Isaiah (Ben Sira 48:24–25; the New Testament; Josephus, who says Cyrus read his own name in Isaiah, *Antiquities* 11.5–6); and to the absence of any manuscript break between chapters 39 and 40. Critical scholars answer that shared vocabulary reflects a deliberate editorial tradition, that ancient attribution reflects the finished book, and that naming a Persian king 150 years in advance is a predictive claim, not a historical datum. Many recent scholars (for example H. G. M. Williamson, M. A. Sweeney) stress that the book was shaped as a unity by its editors while still affirming multiple authors.

## The Great Isaiah Scroll

The **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>)**, found in Qumran Cave 1 in 1947, contains all 66 chapters on 17 sheets of leather in 54 columns, about 7.3 metres long. Palaeography dates it c. 150–100 BCE, and AMS radiocarbon tests in the 1990s gave a broadly compatible range. It is about a thousand years older than the Masoretic codices.

The scroll agrees substantially with the later Masoretic Text, with many spelling differences and a few hundred meaningful variants. It shows that by the 2nd century BCE the book circulated as a single 66-chapter work. Chapter 40 follows chapter 39 without a special break, but there is a notable blank space after chapter 33, which some scholars (W. H. Brownlee) read as a division of the scroll into two halves. Some twenty other copies of Isaiah were found at Qumran. None of this decides authorship: it fixes the latest possible date for the collection, not the earliest.

## The Assyrian Record

The 8th-century core of Isaiah can be checked against unusually rich Assyrian sources.

### Tiglath-pileser III and the Syro-Ephraimite war

Tiglath-pileser III (745–727 BCE) expanded Assyria into the Levant. His inscriptions name **Rezin** (Raḫianu) of Damascus, **Pekah** (Paqaḫa) of Israel, whom "they overthrew" and replaced with **Hoshea**, and list **"Jehoahaz [Ahaz] of Judah"** among tributaries. This matches Isaiah 7–8 and 2 Kings 16: Damascus and Israel attacked Judah (c. 734 BCE), Ahaz turned to Assyria, and Damascus fell in 732 BCE. The annexation of Galilee and the coastal route in 733–732 BCE stands behind Isaiah 9:1.

### Sargon II and Ashdod

Isaiah 20:1 is the only place in the Bible that names **Sargon**. Before Paul-Émile Botta excavated Sargon's palace at Khorsabad (1843–1844), he was otherwise almost unknown. Sargon's inscriptions describe the revolt of Iamani of Ashdod and its suppression in 712/711 BCE, and fragments of a Sargon stele were found at Ashdod itself. The Tang-i Var inscription from Iran says the Kushite king "Shapataku" handed Iamani over, which has helped redate Egypt's 25th Dynasty.

### Merodach-baladan

**Marduk-apla-iddina II**, the Merodach-baladan of Isaiah 39, was a Chaldean leader who ruled Babylon in 722–710 BCE and again briefly in 703 BCE and repeatedly sought allies against Assyria. Assyrian texts confirm his career, though they call him a son of Yakin, not "son of Baladan."

### Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE

Sennacherib's third campaign is the best-documented event in Isaiah:

- **Annals.** The Rassam Cylinder (700 BCE) and later prisms (the Taylor Prism in the British Museum, the Oriental Institute Prism in Chicago, the Jerusalem Prism in the Israel Museum) record that he took 46 of Hezekiah's fortified cities, deported 200,150 people, shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage," and received heavy tribute. They do not claim Jerusalem was taken.
- **Lachish.** The reliefs from Sennacherib's Southwest Palace at Nineveh, excavated by A. H. Layard and now in the British Museum, depict the siege of Lachish (Isa 36:2; 37:8). David Ussishkin's excavations (1973–1994) uncovered the Assyrian siege ramp and the destruction of Level III.
- **Hezekiah's preparations.** The **Siloam Tunnel** and its Hebrew inscription (found 1880) fit 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32:30, and radiometric dating published in 2003 places the tunnel c. 700 BCE (an earlier start has been argued). The **Broad Wall** in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter, built over demolished houses, matches Isaiah 22:10. The *lmlk* ("belonging to the king") jar handles are widely tied to Hezekiah's war preparations.

### Bullae and the figure of Isaiah

In 2015 Eilat Mazar announced a clay seal impression from the Ophel excavations reading "Belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah." In 2018 she published a second bulla found nearby reading "Belonging to Yesha'yahu" followed by the damaged letters **nvy**. If the missing letter was an *aleph*, it would read "prophet." But the reading is uncertain, and scholars such as Christopher Rollston have noted that *nvy* could be a personal name or place name. Mazar herself presented the identification as a possibility. It should be treated as **debated, not proven**. Apart from this, Isaiah son of Amoz has no secure extra-biblical attestation.

## Reliability Assessment

For the **late 8th century**, Isaiah is a valuable and largely accurate witness. Its kings, enemies, place names, and political choices — Assyrian vassalage, Egyptian alliances, Philistine revolts — match Assyrian records and archaeology well. The prophet's own words are best preserved in the oracles of chapters 1–11 and 28–31.

The **narratives of chapters 36–39** combine solid history (the campaign, Lachish, the Rabshakeh, Hezekiah's survival) with legendary elements (the angel killing 185,000, the shadow moving backward) and chronological problems (Tirhakah's title, the compression of Sennacherib's death twenty years later).

The **later layers** are reliable witnesses to their own times, exile and restoration, rather than to the 8th century. Predictions in the book of specific events are best explained, as in other ancient literature, by later authors writing after or during those events.
