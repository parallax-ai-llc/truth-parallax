## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (visionary account), with an accurate date-frame.** The vision itself cannot be tested historically. Its setting, "the year King Uzziah died" (c. 740 BCE), is consistent with the biblical chronology, and its imagery draws on iconography known from Judahite seals.

## What the Text Claims

- In the year Uzziah died, Isaiah saw the LORD enthroned in the Temple, attended by six-winged **seraphim** (6:1–4).
- A seraph touches his lips with a coal from the altar (6:6–7).
- Isaiah is sent to make the people's hearts dull "until cities lie waste without inhabitant" (6:9–12). A "holy seed" will remain in the stump (6:13).

## External Evidence

### King Uzziah

Uzziah (also called Azariah) died c. 740 BCE. Proposed dates vary, roughly 742–734 BCE, depending on how co-regencies are reconstructed. No inscription from his own lifetime securely names him. (An "Azriyau" in the annals of Tiglath-pileser III was once identified with him, but most Assyriologists now reject this.) An **Aramaic burial plaque**, found in 1931 and published by E. L. Sukenik, reads "Hither were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah. Do not open." It dates to the late Second Temple period and records a reburial, not his original tomb.

### Seraphim and Judahite iconography

The Hebrew *saraph* also means a fiery serpent (Num 21:6; Isa 14:29; 30:6). Judahite and Israelite seals of the 8th century often show the **winged uraeus**, an Egyptian royal cobra adapted with two or four wings. Othmar Keel and others argue that Isaiah's six-winged seraphim draw on this local imagery of protective winged serpents guarding the divine throne. Two-winged and four-winged versions are attested on seals. Six wings are unique to Isaiah.

### The "Isaiah Memoir"

Since Karl Budde (1928), many scholars have treated 6:1–9:6 (or 8:18) as a first-person **memoir** (*Denkschrift*) written by Isaiah or his disciples. Isaiah 8:16 refers to binding up "the testimony" among his disciples, which suggests early written preservation. The command to harden the people's hearts (6:9–10) is often read as written *after* the prophet's message had been rejected, explaining that rejection as part of God's plan.

## Points of Agreement

- The date formula fits the known sequence of Judahite kings.
- The temple imagery (throne, smoke, altar) and the winged guardians reflect real Iron Age Judahite religious art.

## Points of Conflict

- Verses 12–13 speak of large-scale removal of the population, which may anticipate the deportations of 701 BCE or the exile of 587 BCE.
- The last clause of 6:13 ("the holy seed is its stump") is **missing from the Septuagint** and is widely considered a later addition reinterpreting judgment as hope.

## Assessment

Chapter 6 offers no event to verify, but its framing and imagery fit an 8th-century Jerusalem setting. The closing lines show the work of later editors who, knowing that the land had in fact been devastated, added a note of survival.
