## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** Chapter 62 promises Zion a new name, watchmen on its walls and an end to foreigners consuming its harvest. It makes no historical claims. Its concerns (unbuilt walls, harvests lost to outsiders) fit Persian-period Judah before or around Nehemiah's governorship (c. 445 BCE).

## What the Text Claims

- For Zion's sake the prophet will not keep silent until her vindication shines (62:1).
- Zion will no longer be called "Forsaken" but "My Delight Is in Her" (*Hephzibah*) and "Married" (*Beulah*) (62:4).
- Watchmen are set on Jerusalem's walls and must give YHWH no rest (62:6–7).
- Grain and wine will no longer be given to enemies and foreigners (62:8–9).
- "Build up the highway, clear it of stones" (62:10).

## External Evidence

- **Jerusalem's walls:** Nehemiah 1:3 (c. 445 BCE) reports the walls still broken down and the gates burned. Excavations in the City of David have uncovered fortification remains that some excavators (Eilat Mazar) attributed to Nehemiah, though others date them differently. Most historians accept a mid-5th-century rebuilding, though Israel Finkelstein argues that the detailed wall account in Nehemiah 3 better fits the larger Hasmonean city.
- **Harvests to foreigners:** Nehemiah 5:4 refers to borrowing money "for the king's tax," and Nehemiah 9:36–37 complains that the land's produce goes to foreign kings. Persian tribute and taxes are well documented across the empire, including in the Persepolis administrative archives.
- **Hephzibah** is also the name of King Manasseh's mother (2 Kings 21:1), showing it was a real Judean name.

## Points of Agreement

The chapter's concerns match the situation in Yehud documented in Nehemiah: an unwalled or poorly defended city and heavy taxation.

## Points of Conflict

None.

## Assessment

Chapter 62 is a prayer-poem from post-exilic Jerusalem. The watchmen "on your walls" may be poetic or may anticipate the walls Nehemiah later built. Either way, the setting is the Persian period, not the 8th century.
