## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** Chapter 56 opens the third part of the book, usually called **Trito-Isaiah** (chapters 56–66). It addresses a community already back in Judah, debating who may belong to it and worship at the Temple. That places it in the late 6th or 5th century BCE, after the return and probably around or after the rebuilding of the Temple (520–515 BCE).

## What the Text Claims

- Keep justice and the Sabbath, for salvation is near (56:1–2).
- **Eunuchs** who keep the Sabbath will receive "a monument and a name better than sons and daughters" in YHWH's house (56:3–5).
- **Foreigners** who join themselves to YHWH will be brought to his holy mountain, and their sacrifices accepted, "for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (56:6–7).
- Israel's watchmen are blind and its shepherds greedy (56:9–12).

## External Evidence

### Date and setting

- Bernhard Duhm (1892) first argued that 56–66 came from a different author than 40–55. Most scholars now see these chapters as the work of several hands in Judah, not Babylon.
- The concerns are those of Persian-period Yehud: the Temple ("my house," 56:5, 7), the Sabbath, and community membership. Babylon and Cyrus are no longer mentioned.
- **Haggai** and **Zechariah 1–8** date the rebuilding of the Temple to 520–515 BCE, in the reign of Darius I (Ezra 6:15).

### Foreigners and eunuchs in the Persian period

- **Deuteronomy 23:1–8** bars men with crushed genitals from the assembly, and Ammonites and Moabites permanently. Isaiah 56 openly contradicts at least the first rule.
- **Ezra 9–10** and **Nehemiah 13:1–3, 23–30** (mid-5th century BCE) describe the expulsion of foreign wives and of foreigners from the assembly, citing Deuteronomy 23. Isaiah 56 represents the opposite, inclusive position in the same debate.
- Eunuchs served at the Persian and earlier Mesopotamian courts. Some Judean exiles may have served in such roles (compare 2 Kings 20:18 and Daniel 1:3). Some Greek manuscripts of Nehemiah 1:11 call Nehemiah a eunuch (*eunouchos*) instead of a cupbearer (*oinochoos*), probably a scribal error.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter's focus on Temple access and the Sabbath fits the Persian-period community as described in Ezra-Nehemiah and Haggai.
- A Temple community including foreigners and proselytes is historically plausible for Persian Yehud.

## Points of Conflict

There is no conflict with external evidence. There is an internal biblical tension: Isaiah 56 and Ezra-Nehemiah reflect opposed parties in a real dispute over who counts as Israel.

## Assessment

Chapter 56 is best understood as a voice from the early post-exilic community, probably late 6th or early 5th century BCE. Its inclusive vision stands against the exclusivism of Ezra and Nehemiah. Paul Hanson (*The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, 1975) saw Trito-Isaiah as the voice of a "visionary" group opposed to the priestly establishment; later scholars (e.g., Schramm) have questioned that reconstruction.

**Conservative view.** Oswalt and Motyer read 56–66 as Isaiah of Jerusalem's own vision of the future community. Critics reply that the chapters assume the Temple and community as present realities, not distant hopes.
