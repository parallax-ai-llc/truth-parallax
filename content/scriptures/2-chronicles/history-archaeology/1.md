## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The chapter is a reworking of 1 Kings 3:4–15 and 10:26–29. The note on trade in horses and chariots between Egypt, Kue (Cilicia) and the Aramean and Neo-Hittite kings uses real geography of the Iron Age, but nothing in the chapter can be tied to a 10th-century king by outside evidence.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon leads "all Israel" to the high place at **Gibeon**, where the Mosaic Tent of Meeting and the bronze altar of Bezalel stand, while the Ark is already in Jerusalem (1:3–6).
- God appears by night and grants Solomon wisdom, wealth and honour (1:7–12).
- Solomon has 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen, and makes silver and gold as common as stones in Jerusalem (1:14–15).
- His merchants import horses from "Egypt and Kue" and export chariots and horses to "the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Aram" (1:16–17).

## External Evidence

### Gibeon (el-Jib)

James B. Pritchard's excavations at el-Jib (1956–1962) identified the site through jar handles inscribed *gb'n* (Gibeon). He found a large rock-cut pool and a stepped water tunnel, and many Iron II wine installations. No sanctuary or high place has been found, and the well-attested remains belong mostly to the 8th–6th centuries BCE.

### Kue and the horse trade

**Kue** (Assyrian *Qawe*/*Que*) is the plain of Cilicia in south-east Anatolia. It appears in Assyrian inscriptions from the 9th century BCE, including Shalmaneser III's. The "kings of the Hittites" are the Neo-Hittite rulers of northern Syria (Carchemish, Hamath and others), and "Aram" the Aramean states. This is a credible map of the Iron Age Levant. Assyrian records show that Kue and the regions of Anatolia were known for horses.

Many scholars, following Hayim Tadmor, suggest that "Egypt" (*Miṣrayim*) here may originally have been **Muṣri**, a land in Anatolia, since Egypt was not a significant horse exporter. This is a plausible but unproven emendation.

### Solomon himself

No inscription of the 10th century BCE names Solomon. His existence is not directly attested outside the Bible.

## Points of Agreement

- The trade geography (Cilicia, Neo-Hittite and Aramean kings) fits the Iron Age, broadly the 9th–8th centuries BCE.
- Gibeon was a significant town in Benjamin, and local high places were normal before Josiah's centralisation, as Kings itself admits (1 Kgs 3:2–4).

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 1 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| The Mosaic Tabernacle stands at Gibeon (1:3–5) | Not in 1 Kings 3. The Chronicler harmonises Solomon's worship at a high place with the Law (compare 1 Chr 16:39; 21:29). There is no archaeological trace of such a shrine |
| Bezalel's bronze altar is still in use (1:5) | A link to the Priestly Tabernacle account (Exod 31; 38), which most scholars date to the exilic or Persian period |
| Silver and gold "as common as stones" (1:15) | Tenth-century Jerusalem was small and poor in luxury finds |
| 1,400 chariots, 12,000 horsemen (1:14) | Copied from 1 Kgs 10:26. Ahab's 2,000 chariots at Qarqar (853 BCE) show that large chariot forces were possible a century later for the richer northern kingdom, not for 10th-century Judah |

## Assessment

The Chronicler moves the horse-trade notice from the end of the Solomon story (1 Kgs 10) to its beginning, so that wealth follows directly from God's promise. He also removes the awkwardness of Solomon sacrificing at a high place by placing the Mosaic Tabernacle there. Both moves are theological and literary.

The geography of Kue and the Neo-Hittite kings may reflect a genuine memory of Iron Age trade networks, but it more likely belongs to the 9th–8th centuries than to Solomon's time. As history of the 10th century, the chapter adds nothing independent of Kings.
