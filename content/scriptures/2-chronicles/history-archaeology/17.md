## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Jehoshaphat was a real king of Judah in the mid-9th century, a contemporary and ally of the Omride kings of Israel. But nothing in this chapter is drawn from Kings, and its picture of officials teaching "the book of the law of the LORD" throughout Judah, an army of over a million, and tribute from Philistines and Arabs is not supported by evidence. It reflects Persian-period ideals.

## What the Text Claims

- Jehoshaphat garrisons Judah's fortified cities and the towns of Ephraim that Asa had taken (17:1–2).
- He removes high places and Asherah poles (17:6).
- In his third year he sends officials, Levites and priests to teach "the book of the law of the LORD" in all the cities of Judah (17:7–9).
- **Philistines** bring him tribute and silver, and **Arabs** bring 7,700 rams and 7,700 goats (17:11).
- He builds fortresses and store cities (17:12–13).
- His commanders lead **1,160,000** soldiers (17:14–19).

## External Evidence

### Jehoshaphat in his world

No inscription names Jehoshaphat. His reign is placed in the 9th century by the synchronisms of Kings with Ahab and his sons, who are externally attested (the Kurkh Monolith and the Mesha Stele). Kings presents Jehoshaphat as a junior partner of Israel (1 Kgs 22:4), which fits the archaeological picture of a much stronger northern kingdom in this period.

### Literacy and a law book

Writing in 9th-century Judah is sparsely attested. Inscriptions become far more common in the 8th–7th centuries BCE. Most scholars date the formation of a "book of the law" (Deuteronomy in some form) to the late 7th century at the earliest. The image of Levites travelling with a law book to teach the people resembles Ezra's reading of the law (Neh 8:7–8), where Levites help the people understand.

### Philistines and Arabs

Philistine cities were independent city-states in the 9th century. **Gath** (Tell es-Safi) was the most powerful until its destruction by Hazael of Damascus, c. 830 BCE (compare 2 Kgs 12:17), shown by Aren Maeir's excavations. Tribute from Philistia to Judah in this period is not attested and seems unlikely. Arabs appear in Assyrian texts from 853 BCE (a "Gindibu the Arab" at Qarqar), so Arab herders are a plausible feature of the southern landscape.

## Points of Agreement

- Jehoshaphat is placed in the right century, as a contemporary of the Omrides.
- Building of fortresses and store cities fits Iron II Judah generally.
- Arab pastoralists to the south and east are historically plausible.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 17 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| Removes high places (17:6) | 1 Kgs 22:43 and 2 Chr 20:33 say they were not removed |
| Teaching mission with a law book (17:7–9) | Anachronistic; modelled on Ezra's era |
| Philistine tribute (17:11) | Not attested; Gath was the dominant power until c. 830 BCE |
| 1,160,000 soldiers (17:14–18) | Impossible for a kingdom of tens of thousands of people |

## Assessment

The chapter is the Chronicler's portrait of a model king: he teaches the law, fortifies the land, and receives tribute from neighbours as reward. It contradicts both Kings and the Chronicler's own later statement about the high places. The figure of Jehoshaphat may be real, but the details of this chapter are idealised and should not be used as evidence for 9th-century Judah. The name Jehoshaphat ("Yahweh has judged") may also have inspired the Chronicler's emphasis on legal reform here and in chapter 19.
