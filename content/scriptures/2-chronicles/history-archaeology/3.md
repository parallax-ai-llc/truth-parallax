## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** No remains of Solomon's Temple have been found, and the Temple Mount cannot be excavated. The tripartite plan described here, however, closely matches excavated Iron Age temples in Syria, so the general description is plausible for a royal temple of the early first millennium. The gold totals and the identification of the site with Mount Moriah are the Chronicler's additions.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon builds on **Mount Moriah**, at the threshing floor of Ornan where David saw the angel (3:1).
- The house is 60 by 20 cubits "by the old standard" (3:3), with a vestibule 120 cubits high (3:4).
- The Most Holy Place is overlaid with **600 talents of fine gold** (3:8).
- Two cherubim fill the inner room, a veil of blue, purple and crimson separates it (3:10–14).
- Two bronze pillars, **Jachin and Boaz**, stand before the house (3:15–17).

## External Evidence

### Comparable temples

- **'Ain Dara** (northern Syria, Iron Age, possibly 10th–8th centuries BCE): a long-room temple with a porch with two columns, a main hall and a raised inner sanctuary, decorated with lions and sphinxes. John Monson and others have called it the closest known parallel to the biblical Temple.
- **Tell Tayinat** (ancient Kunulua, south-east Turkey): a small temple of the 9th–8th centuries BCE with a porch with two columns flanking the entrance and a tripartite plan.
- **Motza** (Tel Moẓa), a few kilometres west of Jerusalem: an Iron IIA temple excavated from 2012, showing that Judah had a monumental temple outside Jerusalem in the early monarchy.

### Jerusalem

The Temple Mount has never been excavated. Material from the Temple Mount Sifting Project comes from disturbed fill and does not reveal the building. Claims about objects from the Temple (for example the ivory pomegranate inscription) have been judged forgeries or doubtful.

### The "old" cubit

The note that the cubits are "by the old standard" (3:3) shows that the author knew a later, different cubit. Ezekiel 40:5 also mentions a longer "royal" cubit. The gloss is a sign of a writer looking back across a long period.

## Points of Agreement

- The tripartite long-room plan with a porch with two pillars fits Iron Age Levantine temple design.
- Cherubim (winged sphinxes) as guardians of a throne are well attested in Phoenician and Syrian art, for example on the Ahiram sarcophagus throne and in the Samaria and Nimrud ivories.
- Free-standing pillars at a temple entrance are known from Tayinat and from many temple models.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 3 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| Site is "Mount Moriah" (3:1) | The only biblical link between the Temple and Abraham's Moriah (Gen 22:2); a theological identification |
| Vestibule 120 cubits high (3:4) | About 55 metres, far higher than the house (30 cubits in 1 Kgs 6:2); usually seen as a textual error or exaggeration |
| 600 talents of gold for one room (3:8) | About 20 tonnes; not plausible for Iron Age Judah |
| Veil before the inner room (3:14) | Kings has wooden doors (1 Kgs 6:31–32); the veil matches the Tabernacle (Exod 26:31) |

## Assessment

The Chronicler abridges 1 Kings 6–7 and reshapes the Temple to echo the Tabernacle (the veil) and Abraham's sacrifice (Moriah). The basic plan he inherited from Kings is credible, because archaeology shows similar buildings across the Iron Age Levant. Whether Solomon in particular built a temple of this kind in the 10th century cannot be tested. Many scholars accept a royal temple in Jerusalem early in the monarchy, but its size and decoration in Kings may reflect the later, expanded building known to 7th-century writers.
