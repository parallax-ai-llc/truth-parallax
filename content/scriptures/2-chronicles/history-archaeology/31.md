## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The chapter describes cult administration: the removal of shrines, the collection of tithes, and their storage and distribution to priests and Levites. Archaeology shows that Hezekiah's Judah had an active royal storage system, which gives the picture a plausible background. But the organisation of priests and Levites described here closely matches Second Temple practice.

## What the Text Claims

- After the Passover, Israelites break pillars, cut down Asherim, and destroy high places throughout Judah, Benjamin, Ephraim and Manasseh (31:1).
- Hezekiah appoints the divisions of priests and Levites (31:2).
- The king gives from his own property for the regular offerings (31:3).
- The people bring firstfruits and tithes in heaps from the third to the seventh month (31:4–10).
- Chambers are prepared in the Temple; Levites named Conaniah and Shimei oversee the stores, and Kore the gatekeeper manages the freewill offerings (31:11–15).
- Distribution is made by genealogical enrolment, including priests in the outlying towns (31:16–19).

## External Evidence

### Royal storage in Hezekiah's time

- **The *lmlk* jars.** Hundreds of storage jar handles stamped *lmlk* ("belonging to the king"), with one of four place names (Hebron, Ziph, Socoh and *mmšt*), have been found across Judah. They are securely dated to the late 8th century, in Hezekiah's reign, especially in destruction layers of 701 BCE at Lachish. They show a centralised system for collecting and distributing produce, perhaps linked to preparations for Sennacherib's invasion.
- **Storehouses.** Pillared buildings at Beersheba, Lachish and other sites are widely interpreted as storehouses, though some see them as stables. 2 Chronicles 32:28 also credits Hezekiah with storehouses.

### Tithes

Tithes to temples and kings are known across the ancient Near East, for example in Mesopotamian temple archives and in Ugaritic texts. There is no direct evidence for Temple tithes in Jerusalem in the 8th century. The system in this chapter, with tithes given to Levites who then give a tithe to the priests, follows Numbers 18 and Nehemiah 10–13.

## Points of Agreement

- Hezekiah's state was capable of large-scale collection and storage of produce, as the *lmlk* jars show.
- Kings also reports his removal of high places and Asherim (2 Kgs 18:4).

## Points of Conflict

| Element | Comment |
|---------|---------|
| Shrines destroyed in Ephraim and Manasseh | These were Assyrian provinces after 720 BCE; Judahite action there is unlikely |
| Priestly and Levitical divisions | The 24 courses (1 Chr 24) are a Second Temple institution |
| Tithe procedures and Temple chambers | Closely parallel Nehemiah 10:35–39 and 13:4–13, from the 5th century |
| Genealogical enrolment of priests (31:16–19) | Reflects post-exilic concern with priestly lineage (Ezra 2:61–63) |

## Assessment

The administrative detail can give an impression of archival accuracy, but the institutions match the Persian-period Temple of Nehemiah. The Chronicler presents Hezekiah as founding, or restoring, the arrangements his own community knew. This gave them royal and ancient authority.

The *lmlk* jars show that the idea of Hezekiah as a strong organiser of goods is not baseless. Scholars such as Oded Lipschits and Andrew Vaughn have studied the jars as evidence of a highly organised economy in his reign. But they point to a royal economy preparing for war, not to the Temple tithe system described here.
