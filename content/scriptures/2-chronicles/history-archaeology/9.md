## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The visit of the Queen of Sheba and the catalogue of Solomon's wealth are copied from 1 Kings 10 with small changes. The Sabaean kingdom of south Arabia was real, but there is no evidence of a Sabaean queen or of long-distance caravan trade with Jerusalem in the 10th century. The wealth figures are legendary in scale.

## What the Text Claims

- The **Queen of Sheba** visits Solomon with camels bearing spices, gold and precious stones, and tests his wisdom (9:1–12).
- Solomon receives **666 talents of gold** a year (9:13), makes gold shields and an ivory throne (9:15–19).
- His ships go to **Tarshish** with Huram's servants and return every three years with gold, silver, ivory, apes and peacocks (9:21).
- He has **4,000 stalls** for horses and chariots and rules from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt (9:25–26).
- Solomon reigns forty years and dies (9:30–31).

## External Evidence

### Saba

Sabaean inscriptions in Old South Arabian script appear from about the 8th century BCE, perhaps slightly earlier. Assyrian kings record gifts from Sabaean rulers: **Itamra** under Sargon II (715 BCE) and **Karib-il** under Sennacherib. Tiglath-pileser III's annals name Arabian queens (Zabibe and Samsi), but these ruled north Arabian tribes, not Saba. No Sabaean queen is known from any source.

### Camels and the incense trade

Studies of animal bones from the copper sites in the Arabah (Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef, 2013) show domestic camels arriving in the southern Levant in the last third of the 10th century BCE. Overland incense trade from south Arabia to the Mediterranean is clearly documented only from the 8th century, when Assyria controlled its northern end.

### Ships of Tarshish

1 Kings 10:22 speaks of a "Tarshish fleet," a term for large seagoing ships. The Chronicler reads it as ships that **went to Tarshish** (9:21; compare 20:36), yet sails them from Ezion-geber on the Red Sea. Tarshish is usually located in the western Mediterranean (Tartessos in Spain, or Sardinia). The reading shows a misunderstanding of the older phrase.

### Stables and ivory

Structures with pillars at **Megiddo** were once identified as Solomon's stables (Chicago excavations, 1920s–30s) and later redated by Yadin to the time of Ahab. Whether they were stables or storehouses is still debated. Ivory furniture is well attested in 9th–8th-century Israel, in the **Samaria ivories**, and Amos 6:4 condemns "beds of ivory."

## Points of Agreement

- Saba was a real kingdom with a stake in luxury trade, and Judah lay on routes linking Arabia with the Mediterranean.
- Gold shields, ivory furniture and exotic animals are typical royal display items, also listed in Assyrian tribute records.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 9 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| A queen of Sheba visits in the 10th century | Sabaean state and its trade attested later; no Sabaean queen known |
| 666 talents of gold a year (9:13) | About 20 tonnes; legendary in scale |
| Ships sail from the Red Sea to Tarshish (9:21) | Misreading of "Tarshish ships" |
| 4,000 stalls (9:25) | 40,000 in 1 Kgs 4:26 (Hebrew 5:6); the Chronicler or his text reduces it |
| Rule to the Euphrates (9:26) | No archaeological or external support |

## Assessment

The Chronicler stops his Solomon story here, without the foreign wives and apostasy of 1 Kings 11. He leaves an idealised builder-king whose wealth is the reward for wisdom and devotion. The story of Sheba probably took shape when Arabian trade with Judah was at its height, in the 8th–7th centuries BCE, and was projected back onto the founder of the Temple.
