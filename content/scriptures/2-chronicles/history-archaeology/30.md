## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Hezekiah's great Passover, with invitations sent to the remnant of the northern tribes, appears only in Chronicles. Kings says that no Passover like Josiah's had been held since the days of the judges, which leaves no room for this one. The account is best read as the Chronicler's vision of a reunited Israel, though it may reflect a real memory of northern refugees in Jerusalem.

## What the Text Claims

- Hezekiah and the assembly decide to keep Passover in the **second month**, because not enough priests were sanctified in time (30:2–3).
- Couriers carry letters "from Beersheba to Dan," calling Israel to return to Yahweh; many in Ephraim and Manasseh mock them, but some from Asher, Manasseh and Zebulun come (30:5–11).
- A great crowd gathers and removes the altars in Jerusalem (30:13–14).
- Many northerners eat the Passover without being purified, and Hezekiah prays for their pardon (30:17–20).
- The feast is extended for a further seven days; "since the time of Solomon … there had been nothing like this in Jerusalem" (30:23–26).

## External Evidence

### The fall of Samaria and northern refugees

Samaria fell to Assyria in 722–720 BCE. Shalmaneser V and Sargon II both claimed its capture; Sargon claims to have deported 27,290 people. After this, part of the northern population remained under Assyrian governors, which matches the "remnant" addressed in 30:6.

### Jerusalem's growth

Excavations by Nahman Avigad in the Jewish Quarter uncovered the **Broad Wall**, a fortification about 7 metres thick from the late 8th century. It enclosed the Western Hill, showing that Jerusalem grew several times over in a short period. Magen Broshi first argued that this expansion came partly from refugees from the fallen northern kingdom. Israel Finkelstein has developed the idea, suggesting that many Israelites settled in Judah and brought northern traditions with them. Others, such as Nadav Na'aman, attribute more of the growth to Judahites displaced by Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BCE.

### Passover

No archaeological evidence bears directly on Passover celebrations in the monarchic period.

## Points of Agreement

- After 722 BCE there was a northern remnant under Assyrian rule, and Judah had reason to reach out to it.
- Jerusalem expanded dramatically in Hezekiah's time, possibly with northern immigrants.
- Hezekiah is remembered in Kings as a reformer who centralised worship.

## Points of Conflict

| Issue | Comment |
|-------|---------|
| A great Passover under Hezekiah | 2 Kgs 23:22 says no such Passover had been kept since the judges until Josiah's |
| Invitation to northerners "from Beersheba to Dan" | Assyrian provinces covered the north; a Judahite king calling them to Jerusalem would risk Assyrian anger, and no source mentions it |
| Second-month Passover | Applies the rule of Num 9:6–13 for the unclean or travelling, a priestly law usually dated late |
| "Nothing like this since Solomon" | Mirrors Chronicles' portrayal of Hezekiah as a second Solomon |

## Assessment

Most critical scholars regard the chapter as a Chronicler composition. Its message is that the north, having suffered judgment, can still return to Jerusalem's Temple, and that sincere seeking matters more than ritual purity (30:18–20). This fits the Persian period, when the relationship between Jerusalem and the people of the former northern kingdom, including Samaria, was a live issue.

Some scholars, including Williamson, think a historical Passover-type festival under Hezekiah cannot be ruled out. The archaeological evidence for northern immigrants to Jerusalem gives the story some plausibility in setting. But the account as told conflicts with Kings and has no external confirmation.
