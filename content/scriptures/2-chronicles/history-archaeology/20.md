## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The great victory over Moab, Ammon and the Meunites is told only in Chronicles and has no external support. Its form is a liturgical "holy war" in which Levites sing and the enemy destroys itself. The closing notice about the ships at Ezion-geber contradicts the parallel in Kings.

## What the Text Claims

- A great coalition of Moabites, Ammonites and Meunites comes from beyond the Dead Sea and reaches Hazazon-tamar, "that is, En-gedi" (20:1–2).
- Jehoshaphat proclaims a fast, prays in the Temple, and the Levite Jahaziel prophesies victory (20:3–17).
- The enemy comes up by the ascent of Ziz; Judah goes out to the wilderness of Tekoa with singers at the front (20:16, 20–21).
- The invaders destroy each other; Judah spends three days gathering spoil and names the place the Valley of Beracah ("blessing") (20:22–26).
- Jehoshaphat joins Ahaziah of Israel to build ships at Ezion-geber to go to Tarshish; the prophet Eliezer condemns the alliance and the ships are wrecked (20:35–37).

## External Evidence

### Moab and the 9th century

The **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE) records that Moab threw off Israelite rule after the reign of Omri's son. A Moabite war in Jehoshaphat's time is therefore plausible in general terms. 2 Kings 3 describes a joint Israel–Judah–Edom campaign against Mesha. But the Mesha Stele mentions only Israel, not Judah, and nothing in it corresponds to an invasion of Judah via En-gedi.

### En-gedi

Excavations at Tel Goren at En-gedi by Benjamin Mazar and later Ephraim Stern found that the Iron Age settlement was founded only in the late 7th century BCE. There is no evidence of a 9th-century town. The gloss "Hazazon-tamar, that is En-gedi" may reflect a later writer's geography.

### The Meunites

The Meunites appear elsewhere only in Chronicles (1 Chr 4:41; 2 Chr 26:7), Ezra–Nehemiah, and plausibly in an 8th-century text of Tiglath-pileser III that names a group near the Egyptian border. If that identification is right, the group belongs to a later horizon than Jehoshaphat.

### Ezion-geber

Nelson Glueck identified Ezion-geber with Tell el-Kheleifeh on the Gulf of Aqaba. Gary Pratico's re-evaluation of Glueck's finds dated the site's occupation mainly to the 8th–6th centuries BCE. No 9th-century port has been found.

## Points of Agreement

- Moab was hostile to the Israel–Judah alliance in the mid-9th century (Mesha Stele; 2 Kgs 3).
- Jehoshaphat was allied with the Omrides and had dealings with Ahaziah of Israel (1 Kgs 22:49).
- The topography of the Judean wilderness between En-gedi and Tekoa is described accurately. The "ascent of Ziz" is often placed at a pass above En-gedi.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 20 | Other evidence |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Moab, Ammon and Meunites invade Judah | No external record; Mesha Stele is silent on Judah |
| En-gedi as a 9th-century landmark | Settlement founded in the late 7th century |
| Ships built jointly with Ahaziah, wrecked as punishment | 1 Kgs 22:48–49: Jehoshaphat's ships wrecked, and he **refused** Ahaziah's offer of partnership |
| Ships go "to Tarshish" from the Red Sea | Kings says "ships of Tarshish" (a type of ship) going to Ophir; Chronicles seems to read it as a destination |

## Assessment

Peter Welten's study of the Chronicler's war reports argued that accounts like this reflect the conditions of the Persian or early Hellenistic period, when groups from east and south of the Dead Sea pressed on Judah. Most critical commentators accept that the story has been shaped into a sermon about trusting God rather than chariots. Japhet allows that a local tradition of a skirmish in the wilderness may lie behind it.

Conservative commentators such as Raymond Dillard defend a historical core, noting the precise geography. Precise geography, however, shows familiarity with the land in the author's own day, not 9th-century sourcing. The change to the Ezion-geber story shows the Chronicler reworking Kings to fit his doctrine that alliances with the wicked bring punishment.
