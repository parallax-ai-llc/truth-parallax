## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Josiah's reign falls in a well-understood period, the collapse of Assyrian power in the 620s–610s BCE, and a religious reform in his time is widely accepted. Personal names from the story appear on contemporary seal impressions. But Chronicles changes the order of events found in Kings, beginning the purge years before the law book is discovered, and the extent of the reform, especially in the north, is strongly debated.

## What the Text Claims

- Josiah becomes king at 8; in his 8th year he begins to seek God, and in his 12th year he purges Judah and Jerusalem of high places, Asherim and images (34:1–5).
- He extends the purge to the cities of Manasseh, Ephraim and Simeon, "as far as Naphtali" (34:6–7).
- In his 18th year he repairs the Temple; the priest Hilkiah finds "the book of the law of the LORD given through Moses," and the scribe Shaphan reads it to the king (34:8–18).
- Josiah tears his clothes; the prophetess Huldah predicts disaster, but not in his lifetime (34:19–28).
- The king reads the book to the people and makes a covenant (34:29–33).

## External Evidence

### The international setting

Assyrian power in the west faded after about 630 BCE. Nabopolassar made Babylon independent in 626 BCE, and the Babylonian Chronicles record the fall of Assyrian cities, including Nineveh in 612 BCE. This vacuum allowed a Judahite king to assert religious and political independence and perhaps to reach into former Assyrian provinces.

### Names from the court

Excavations by Yigal Shiloh in the City of David uncovered a hoard of over 50 clay bullae in a building destroyed in 586 BCE. One reads "Belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan." A Gemariah son of Shaphan the scribe appears in Jeremiah 36:10, and a scribe Shaphan appears in this chapter. Another bulla in the same group reads "Azaryahu son of Hilkiah"; 1 Chronicles 6:13 names an Azariah son of Hilkiah in the high-priestly line. These names were fairly common, so the identifications are possible rather than proven, but they show that the onomastics of the account fit late 7th-century Jerusalem.

### Evidence for the reform

Archaeological traces are limited and disputed:

- Judean pillar figurines, often associated with goddess worship, remained common until 586 BCE. This suggests either that the reform had limited effect or that the figurines were not its target.
- At Bethel, which 2 Kgs 23:15 says Josiah desecrated, the archaeological record for the late 7th century is thin.
- The **Mesad Hashavyahu** fortress on the coast yielded a Hebrew ostracon of Josiah's time. Some scholars cite it for Judahite expansion westward; others think the site was an Egyptian-controlled outpost with Greek mercenaries.

### The book of the law

Since W. M. L. de Wette in 1805, most scholars have identified the book with an early form of **Deuteronomy**. Its demands, including one sanctuary, covenant renewal and a ban on other gods, match Josiah's reform in Kings. Many critical scholars think the discovery story legitimised a new law code written in or shortly before Josiah's reign.

## Points of Agreement

- Josiah reigned c. 640–609 BCE and carried out a religious reform (2 Kgs 22–23).
- Shaphan, Hilkiah, Huldah and the discovery of the law book are shared with Kings.
- The decline of Assyria made Judahite independence and northern ambitions possible.

## Points of Conflict

| Issue | 2 Kings 22–23 | 2 Chronicles 34 |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|
| When the reform starts | After the book is found, in the 18th year | In the 12th year, six years before the book is found |
| Josiah's piety | Begins with the book | Begins in his youth (8th year) |
| Northern action | Focused on Bethel and Samaria's high places | Extended over a wide area "as far as Naphtali" |
| Huldah's prophecy | Josiah will be buried in peace | Same, but Chronicles later shows him dying in battle (ch. 35) |

## Assessment

Chronicles' reordering reflects its theology. A righteous king should begin seeking God early and reform on his own initiative, rather than being prompted by a discovered book. The reform's extension "as far as Naphtali" reflects the Chronicler's vision of "all Israel."

Nadav Na'aman and others argue that Josiah's kingdom grew only modestly, perhaps to include Bethel and parts of the Benjamin plateau, and that claims of rule over Galilee are not supported by archaeology. Finkelstein and Silberman see Josiah's reign as the setting in which much of the Deuteronomistic History took shape. Conservative scholars accept a larger kingdom and a Mosaic origin for the law book, but the archaeological evidence for a wide northern campaign is weak.
