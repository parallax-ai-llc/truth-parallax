## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The list of fifteen fortified towns (11:5–12) is the most discussed passage unique to Chronicles. It looks like a genuine administrative document because its towns are real and form a coherent defensive ring around the Judean heartland. But many scholars think it describes a later reality, under Hezekiah or Josiah, rather than Rehoboam's reign. The account of priests and Levites fleeing to Judah reflects the Chronicler's theology.

## What the Text Claims

- God, through the prophet Shemaiah, forbids Rehoboam to fight Israel (11:1–4).
- Rehoboam fortifies **Bethlehem, Etam, Tekoa, Beth-zur, Soco, Adullam, Gath, Mareshah, Ziph, Adoraim, Lachish, Azekah, Zorah, Aijalon and Hebron**, stocking them with supplies and weapons (11:5–12).
- Priests and Levites from the north, rejected by Jeroboam, move to Judah, strengthening Rehoboam for three years (11:13–17).
- Rehoboam has 18 wives and 60 concubines; Maacah, "daughter of Absalom," is his favourite and her son Abijah his heir (11:18–23).

## External Evidence

### The towns

All fifteen places are identifiable or roughly located, and lie in Judah's hill country and Shephelah. **Lachish** (Tell ed-Duweir), **Azekah** (Tel Azekah), **Mareshah** (Tel Maresha), **Beth-zur** (Khirbet et-Tabeiqa) and **Soco** have been excavated or surveyed. The list contains no fortress on the **northern** border with Israel, which is surprising if the main threat came from Jeroboam. The ring faces south and west, toward Egypt and Philistia.

### Archaeology of the sites

- **Lachish:** Level V (Iron IIA) is modest; the great fortified city of Level IV–III belongs mostly to the 9th–8th centuries, ending with Sennacherib's siege in 701 BCE.
- **Mareshah, Azekah and others** show strong Iron II remains, mostly of the 8th century, with less clear 10th-century fortification.

### Proposed later dates

- **Volkmar Fritz** (1981) argued that the list reflects Josiah's reign, after the Assyrian collapse.
- **Nadav Na'aman** (1986) linked it to **Hezekiah**, noting overlap with the distribution of *lmlk* ("belonging to the king") storage jar handles, which were part of Hezekiah's preparations against Assyria. Three of the four place names stamped on *lmlk* handles, **Hebron**, **Ziph** and **Socoh**, appear in the list (the fourth, *mmšt*, is unidentified).
- **Israel Finkelstein** (2011) has gone further and proposed a Hasmonean background, a view that few have followed.

Others, including Kitchen and some conservative commentators, accept a 10th-century date and connect the list with defence against Sheshonq I.

## Points of Agreement

- The towns are real, and the list reads like an archival record rather than a free invention.
- A system of fortified royal towns with supply depots fits what archaeology shows for Judah in the 8th century.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 11 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| Fortresses built by Rehoboam (11:5–12) | Archaeological profile and *lmlk* parallels suggest the late 8th or 7th century |
| No northern line of forts | Odd for Rehoboam, natural under Hezekiah facing Assyria from the south-west |
| Priests and Levites flee south (11:13–17) | Reflects the Chronicler's view that the legitimate cult was only in Jerusalem |
| Maacah "daughter of Absalom" (11:20) | 2 Chr 13:2 calls Abijah's mother "Micaiah daughter of Uriel"; the genealogy is confused |

## Assessment

The fortress list is the best candidate in this part of the book for genuine archival material. The Chronicler probably took it from a Judean administrative document. His attribution to Rehoboam may simply be his choice of setting: in his theology a faithful king builds, and a building list needed an early, obedient king. The archaeological profile of the sites and the *lmlk* evidence make a date under Hezekiah the most widely held option, with Josiah as an alternative.
