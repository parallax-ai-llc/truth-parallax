## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The dedication festival and God's reply are taken from 1 Kings 8:62–9:9, with the Chronicler adding fire from heaven and a new promise of healing for a repentant people (7:13–15). The sacrifice numbers are impossible, and God's reply is a theological text written with knowledge of the Temple's destruction.

## What the Text Claims

- Fire falls from heaven to consume the offerings, and God's glory fills the Temple (7:1–3).
- Solomon offers **22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep** (7:5).
- A festival lasts seven days, with an assembly from **Lebo-hamath to the Wadi of Egypt**, followed by a further seven days (7:8–10).
- God promises to hear and heal the land if his people humble themselves and pray (7:13–14), and warns that the Temple will become a byword if they turn away (7:19–22).

## External Evidence

### Sacrifice on this scale

There is no archaeological or textual evidence for animal sacrifice in Iron Age Judah anywhere near these numbers. For comparison, Assyrian records of Ashurnasirpal II's banquet at Nimrud (the **Banquet Stele**, c. 879 BCE) list about 69,574 guests fed over ten days, with numbers of cattle and sheep in the thousands. That was the feast of an imperial capital. A Judean dedication slaughtering 142,000 animals in a week, on one altar, is not credible.

### Borders

"Lebo-hamath to the Wadi of Egypt" is an idealised border formula (compare Num 34:8; Josh 13:5; Ezek 47–48). Lebo-hamath is probably Lebweh in the northern Beqaʿ valley of Lebanon. No archaeological evidence shows Jerusalem controlled territory this far north in the 10th century.

### Date of the divine reply

God's warning (7:19–22) presupposes the Temple's ruin and the exile, with passers-by asking why God did this to the land and the house. This fits writing after 586 BCE, both in 1 Kings 9 and in its copy here.

## Points of Agreement

- Large public festivals with sacrifice and feasting were part of royal temple dedications across the ancient Near East.
- Celebrating the dedication at the autumn festival in the seventh month (7:8–10) fits the agricultural calendar of the Levant, where the great harvest festival came in autumn.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 7 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| Fire from heaven (7:1) | Absent from 1 Kgs 8:54; modelled on Lev 9:24 and David's altar (1 Chr 21:26) |
| 22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep (7:5) | Copied from 1 Kgs 8:63; impossible logistically |
| Festival dates adjusted (7:9–10) | The Chronicler adds an eighth-day assembly so the festival fits the Priestly calendar (Lev 23:36) |
| Healing promise (7:13–15) | New to Chronicles; sets out the retribution principle that structures the rest of the book |

## Assessment

The chapter is important for understanding the Chronicler, not for reconstructing Solomon. Verses 13–15 state the doctrine that will govern the rest of 2 Chronicles: humility and prayer bring healing, and disobedience brings disaster. The Chronicler later applies it to king after king, often adding punishments or rewards that Kings lacks. Historically, the scene reflects the liturgy and theology of the Second Temple, with its emphasis on the divine fire, Priestly festival law and repentance.
