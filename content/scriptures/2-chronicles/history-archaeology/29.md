## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Hezekiah is one of the best-attested kings of Judah, and some cult reform in his reign is possible. But this long account of the Temple's purification and rededication is unique to Chronicles. Its Levitical singers, priestly courses and Davidic instruments reflect the Second Temple, and it depends on the Chronicler's claim, contradicted by Kings, that Ahaz had shut the Temple.

## What the Text Claims

- In the first month of his first year, Hezekiah opens and repairs the Temple doors (29:3).
- He orders the Levites to sanctify themselves and remove the "filth" from the holy place; they finish in sixteen days (29:4–19).
- The king and officials offer sin offerings for the kingdom, the sanctuary and "all Israel" (29:20–24).
- Levites play the instruments of David and sing the words of David and Asaph, while the priests sound trumpets (29:25–30).
- Because the priests are too few, the Levites help flay the burnt offerings, "for the Levites were more upright in heart" (29:34).

## External Evidence

### Hezekiah himself

Hezekiah is securely attested. **Sennacherib's annals** name "Hezekiah the Judahite" (see chapter 32). A royal bulla reading "Belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah" was found in a controlled excavation on the Ophel by Eilat Mazar in 2009 and published in 2015. Similar bullae had earlier appeared on the antiquities market.

### Evidence for cult reform

2 Kings 18:4 says Hezekiah removed the high places, cut down the Asherah and broke the bronze serpent. Archaeologists have tested this claim at two sites:

- **Arad:** Yohanan Aharoni found a shrine within the Judahite fortress, with an altar and standing stone, and argued that it went out of use in Hezekiah's time. Later analysis by Ze'ev Herzog proposed a different stratigraphy, and the date of its decommissioning remains disputed.
- **Beersheba:** Stones from a large horned altar were found reused in a storehouse wall of the late 8th century. Aharoni linked this to Hezekiah's reform, but the interpretation is also debated.

These finds are consistent with some centralising reform in the late 8th century, but they do not prove it.

### The Temple

No remains of the First Temple have been excavated. The description of rites cannot be checked archaeologically.

## Points of Agreement

- Hezekiah was a real king, son of Ahaz, who reigned in the late 8th century.
- Kings also presents him as a reforming king who acted against non-Jerusalem shrines.
- Arad and Beersheba offer possible, though contested, archaeological traces of such reform.

## Points of Conflict

| Element | Comment |
|---------|---------|
| Temple shut by Ahaz and reopened by Hezekiah | Kings says Ahaz modified, not closed, the Temple (2 Kgs 16:10–18) |
| Elaborate Levitical choirs with the "instruments of David" | Reflect Second Temple liturgy, described in 1 Chr 15–16 and 25 |
| Focus on Temple purification | Kings stresses removing the high places and the bronze serpent, which Chronicles barely mentions here |
| Levites more upright than priests (29:34) | Echoes priest–Levite tensions of the post-exilic period |

## Assessment

Chronicles devotes four chapters (29–32) to Hezekiah, against three in Kings, but changes the focus. In Kings his great moment is the Assyrian crisis. In Chronicles it is the restoration of worship, modelled on David and Solomon. Most critical scholars, including Japhet and Klein, see chapter 29 as the Chronicler's own composition, built from Second Temple practice.

Conservative scholars accept the account as historical and point to the archaeological signs of reform. Those signs, however, concern regional shrines rather than the Jerusalem Temple ritual that dominates this chapter.
