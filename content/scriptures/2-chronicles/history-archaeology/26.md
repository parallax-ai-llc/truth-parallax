## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Uzziah (Azariah) is a historical king whose long reign coincided with Judah's 8th-century prosperity, which archaeology broadly confirms. His building and military successes are told only in Chronicles and are written in the Chronicler's standard style. The famous "Uzziah plaque" is genuine but dates from the Second Temple period, and the identification of Uzziah with "Azriyau" in Assyrian annals has largely been abandoned.

## What the Text Claims

- Uzziah becomes king at 16, reigns 52 years, and rebuilds Eloth (Elath) (26:1–3).
- He prospers while he seeks God in the days of Zechariah (26:5).
- He breaks down the walls of Gath, Jabneh and Ashdod and builds cities among the Philistines; he defeats Arabs of Gur-baal and Meunites; Ammonites pay tribute (26:6–8).
- He builds towers in Jerusalem and the wilderness, digs cisterns, and loves farming (26:9–10).
- He has an army of 307,500 and makes "machines, invented by skilful men," to shoot arrows and stones from the walls (26:11–15).
- He enters the Temple to burn incense, is opposed by the priests, and is struck with a skin disease (often translated "leprosy") for the rest of his life; Jotham governs in his place (26:16–21).
- He is buried "in the burial field of the kings" because he was a leper (26:23).

## External Evidence

### The Uzziah plaque

A stone plaque with an Aramaic inscription, now in the Israel Museum, reads roughly: "Here were brought the bones of Uzziah, king of Judah. Do not open." E. L. Sukenik published it in 1931 after it was found in the collection of the Russian convent on the Mount of Olives. Its original location is unknown. The script dates it to about the 1st century BCE or 1st century CE.

The plaque shows that, some 700 years after his death, people in Jerusalem believed they had Uzziah's remains and moved them. That fits the note that he was buried apart from the other kings. It is evidence of a later tradition, not of the 8th century itself.

### "Azriyau" in Tiglath-pileser III's annals

Tiglath-pileser III's annals mention an Azriyau who led a revolt involving districts of Hamath around 738 BCE. Earlier scholars, following a join of fragments that seemed to mention "Yaudi," identified him with Azariah of Judah. In 1974 Nadav Na'aman showed that the key fragment belongs to an inscription of Sennacherib instead. Most scholars now regard Azriyau as a north Syrian ruler with no proven link to Judah. Some still defend the identification, but it is no longer the consensus.

### Seals and the earthquake

Two unprovenanced seals read "Abiyau, servant of Uzziyau" and "Shebanyau, servant of Uzziyau." They are often cited for the king's name, but without a known findspot they carry limited weight. The great earthquake "in the days of Uzziah" (Amos 1:1; Zech 14:5) is widely linked to destruction at Hazor and other sites around 760 BCE.

### Judah's 8th-century growth

Survey and excavation show that Judah expanded markedly in the 8th century. Farms, forts and cisterns in the Negev and Judean wilderness fit the picture of 26:10 in general terms.

## Points of Agreement

- Uzziah/Azariah had a long and prosperous reign in the mid-8th century (2 Kgs 15:1–7).
- He suffered a skin disease, and his son Jotham acted as regent (2 Kgs 15:5).
- Elath was in Judahite hands at some point in the 8th century (2 Kgs 14:22).
- The plaque confirms that he was buried in a special location, at least in later tradition.

## Points of Conflict

| Element | Comment |
|---------|---------|
| Conquest of Gath, Jabneh, Ashdod | Not in Kings; Gath had already been devastated by Hazael (see chapter 24) and Amos 6:2 treats it as ruined |
| Army of 307,500 | Impossible for Judah's population |
| "Machines" shooting arrows and stones | Torsion artillery was invented in the 4th century BCE. Yigael Yadin suggested defensive screens on the walls, like those on the Lachish reliefs; otherwise an anachronism |
| Disease caused by burning incense | Kings gives no reason; the cause reflects Chronicles' concern for priestly privilege |

## Assessment

The chapter blends a reliable core from Kings with the Chronicler's typical "good king" material: building, armies, and victories. Peter Welten argued that such notices are literary topoi rather than archival records. Others, including Japhet, allow that some notices may reflect real information. The Chronicler's main purpose is clear in 26:16–21. A king who intrudes on the priests' role is punished, a lesson that mattered in a Temple-centred community. The Uzziah plaque is a fine illustration of how that community honoured, and moved, its royal past.
