## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The pressure of Hazael of Aram on Judah in the late 9th century is supported by the destruction of Gath, and Joash's temple repairs and assassination come from Kings. But Chronicles reorganises the reign into a pious first half and an apostate second half, and changes the Aramean episode from a payment of tribute into a divine punishment.

## What the Text Claims

- Joash reigns forty years and does right "all the days of Jehoiada the priest" (24:1–2).
- He collects money in a chest for temple repairs, as "the tax of Moses" (24:4–14).
- Jehoiada dies aged 130 and is buried among the kings (24:15–16).
- The princes turn to Asherim and idols; Zechariah son of Jehoiada prophesies against them and is stoned in the Temple court at the king's command (24:17–22).
- A small Aramean army defeats Judah's large army; Joash is wounded and killed in his bed by two servants with Ammonite and Moabite mothers (24:23–26).

## External Evidence

### Hazael and the destruction of Gath

2 Kings 12:17 says Hazael captured Gath before turning toward Jerusalem. Excavations at **Tell es-Safi** (Philistine Gath), directed by Aren Maeir, uncovered a massive late-9th-century destruction. A siege trench surrounds the city, apparently dug by the besieger. Maeir and most scholars attribute this to Hazael. This is one of the clearest archaeological confirmations of an event in the books of Kings, and shows Aramean armies operating in Judah's neighbourhood.

### The "Jehoash Inscription"

In 2003 an unprovenanced stone tablet appeared on the antiquities market describing temple repairs by King Jehoash in language close to 2 Kings 12. An Israel Antiquities Authority committee declared it a modern forgery. A long criminal trial ended in 2012 without a forgery conviction, but the court explicitly did not authenticate the object. Most epigraphers regard it as a forgery, and it cannot be used as evidence.

### Temple finance

No archaeological evidence survives from the First Temple. In general terms, collecting silver by weight for public works fits the economy of the Iron Age Levant, where hoards of cut silver (hacksilber) were common before coinage.

## Points of Agreement

- Joash repaired the Temple with funds collected from the people (2 Kgs 12:4–16).
- Aram under Hazael threatened Jerusalem (2 Kgs 12:17–18), consistent with the destruction of Gath.
- Joash was assassinated by his own servants (2 Kgs 12:20–21). Chronicles even gives the same names in slightly different form.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 12 | 2 Chronicles 24 |
|------------|-----------------|
| Hazael is bought off with Temple and palace treasures | A small Aramean force defeats Judah, kills princes, takes spoil |
| No apostasy of Joash mentioned | Joash turns to idols after Jehoiada's death and has Zechariah killed |
| Assassination with no stated motive | Assassination is revenge for Zechariah's blood (24:25) |
| Joash buried with his fathers | Buried in the city of David but "not in the tombs of the kings" |

Jehoiada's age of 130 is also far beyond normal lifespans and reflects the honour Chronicles gives to the priest.

## Assessment

The archaeology of Gath provides a solid backdrop. Aramean armies did reach the Shephelah in the late 9th century, and Judah was in danger. Beyond that, Chronicles imposes a theological pattern. A defeat needs a cause, so the Chronicler supplies apostasy and the murder of a prophet. The murder of Zechariah is not in Kings and cannot be checked, though it became a well-known tradition (Matt 23:35; Luke 11:51).

Critical scholars such as Japhet treat the second half of the reign as the Chronicler's construction. Conservative commentators accept the additions as drawn from a lost source, which the Chronicler refers to as the "Commentary on the Book of the Kings" (24:27). No such source survives.
