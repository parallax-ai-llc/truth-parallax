## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter copies 1 Kings 12:1–19 almost verbatim. Two separate Hebrew kingdoms, Israel in the north and Judah in the south, certainly existed by the 9th century BCE. Whether they had previously been one state ruled from Jerusalem is one of the most disputed questions in the archaeology of Israel. The negotiation scene itself cannot be tested.

## What the Text Claims

- Rehoboam goes to **Shechem**, where "all Israel" has gathered to make him king (10:1).
- **Jeroboam** returns from Egypt, where he had fled from Solomon (10:2).
- The people ask for relief from Solomon's heavy yoke; Rehoboam rejects the elders' advice and threatens worse, "scorpions" instead of whips (10:3–14).
- Israel rebels; Hadoram, the officer over forced labour, is stoned; Rehoboam flees to Jerusalem (10:16–18).

## External Evidence

### Shechem (Tell Balata)

Shechem was a major Middle and Late Bronze Age city, mentioned in the Amarna letters, where its ruler Labayu troubled neighbouring kings. Excavations (Ernst Sellin; the Drew–McCormick expedition under G. Ernest Wright, 1956–1973) show Iron Age occupation, with substantial Iron II remains. Its role as a traditional assembly place for the northern tribes is plausible.

### Two kingdoms

- The **Tel Dan inscription** (9th century BCE) mentions a "king of Israel" and the "House of David" as distinct.
- The **Kurkh Monolith** (853 BCE) names "Ahab the Israelite" as a major power; Judah is not mentioned.
- The **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE) names Omri and his son as kings of Israel.

These show two kingdoms by the mid-9th century, with Israel the larger and richer.

### The united monarchy debate

Finkelstein and others argue that the northern highlands were never governed from a small 10th-century Jerusalem, and that the story of a united kingdom split by Rehoboam's folly is a Judean claim to the north, written after Israel fell in 720 BCE. Mazar, Dever and the excavators of Khirbet Qeiyafa argue for a modest early Judean state that may have exercised some influence over the north. Neither side has decisive evidence.

## Points of Agreement

- Shechem as a northern political centre fits its long history and its later use as Jeroboam's first capital (1 Kgs 12:25).
- Forced labour (*mas*) is a well-known institution in the region, attested at Alalakh and Ugarit and in Amarna letter 365 from Megiddo.
- The Egyptian refuge of Jeroboam (10:2) fits the policy of Sheshonq I, who soon campaigned in Israel and Judah.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 10 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| "All Israel" had been one kingdom under Solomon | Contested; archaeology does not show a single state ruling the northern highlands from Jerusalem |
| The cause is royal arrogance and taxation | A Judean interpretation; northern economic and political strength may be the real reason for independence |
| Ahijah's prophecy assumed (10:15) | The Chronicler cites a prophecy he did not narrate (it is in 1 Kgs 11:29–39), showing he expects readers to know Kings |

## Assessment

The Chronicler takes this story over unchanged, even though it does not suit his idealised Solomon. Rehoboam's words imply Solomon's yoke really was heavy. He also refers to Ahijah's prophecy, which he never told, a clear sign that his audience knew Kings. Historically, the chapter preserves the memory of Shechem as the northern assembly place and of tension over forced labour. Whether it describes the breakup of a real united kingdom, or the later Judean explanation of why Israel was separate, remains open.
