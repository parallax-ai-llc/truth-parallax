## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (speech).** The prayer is a theological composition, not a record of words spoken in the 10th century. It is copied almost entirely from 1 Kings 8:12–50, a text widely regarded as Deuteronomistic and shaped by the experience of exile. Its historical value lies in what it reveals about the times of its authors.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon, standing on a bronze platform, blesses the assembly and recalls God's choice of David and Jerusalem (6:3–13).
- He asks God to hear prayers made toward the Temple in cases of oath-taking, defeat, drought, famine, foreign worshippers, war, and captivity in a foreign land (6:22–39).
- He admits that heaven cannot contain God, still less a house (6:18).
- The prayer ends with words from Psalm 132:8–10, asking God to "arise" to his resting place and to remember David (6:41–42).

## External Evidence

### Royal dedicatory texts

Kings of the ancient Near East regularly marked temple building with inscriptions and prayers. Examples are the **Gudea cylinders** (c. 2100 BCE), Neo-Assyrian building inscriptions, and Nabonidus's prayers on cylinders from Ur and Sippar. These texts usually ask the god to bless the king and his dynasty in return for the new house. Solomon's prayer shares this concern for the dynasty (6:16–17).

The Phoenician **Yehawmilk stele** from Byblos (5th–4th century BCE) shows a king who has built an altar and asks the "Lady of Byblos" for life and favour. This illustrates the genre, though it is much shorter.

### Evidence of date

- Petitions about defeat by enemies and **captivity in a foreign land**, with prayer "toward this land" from exile (6:36–39), fit the 6th-century situation of Judeans deported to Babylon.
- The theology of the Temple as the place where God's **name** dwells, while God himself is in heaven (6:18–21), is characteristic of Deuteronomy and its school, usually dated from the late 7th century onward.

## Points of Agreement

- A king dedicating a temple with sacrifice and prayer is a standard ancient Near Eastern practice.
- The concern with dynastic succession in a temple prayer fits royal ideology across the region.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 6 | Assessment |
|----------------|------------|
| Words presented as Solomon's own | Composition in Deuteronomistic style, at the earliest late monarchic, revised in the exile |
| Bronze platform (6:13) | Added by the Chronicler; not in Kings |
| Ends with Psalm 132 (6:41–42) | Replaces the Exodus reference of 1 Kgs 8:51–53, shifting the focus from Moses to David |
| Expectation of exile and return (6:36–39) | Reads as written after the events of 597–586 BCE |

## Assessment

The Chronicler makes small but telling changes to his source. He adds the platform, and he replaces the Exodus ending with a psalm about David and the Ark, which fits his focus on the Davidic covenant and the Temple. Historically, the prayer is a document of the exilic and Persian periods. It explains disaster as the result of sin and offers repentance and prayer toward Jerusalem as the remedy. It cannot be used as evidence for Solomon's reign.
