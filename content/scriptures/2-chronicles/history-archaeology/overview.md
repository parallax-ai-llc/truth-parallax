## Overview

This page does not read 2 Chronicles as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the events 2 Chronicles describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

2 Chronicles retells the history of Judah from Solomon's accession (c. 970 BCE on the conventional chronology) to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BCE, and closes with the edict of Cyrus in 538 BCE. Together with 1 Chronicles it forms one work, written in Judea in the **late Persian or early Hellenistic period, most likely c. 400–250 BCE**. The author, usually called "the Chronicler," worked mainly from a version of Samuel–Kings, which he copied, abridged, and expanded.

That makes the book a secondary source. Where it follows Kings, its value is roughly that of Kings. Where it adds material, each addition has to be judged on its own: a few additions look like genuine older information, many reflect the Chronicler's theology and the institutions of his own day.

## Sources and Composition

The Chronicler cites more than a dozen sources, such as "the Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah," "the Midrash of the Book of Kings," and the "words" or "visions" of prophets such as Nathan, Iddo, Shemaiah and Isaiah. Older scholarship sometimes took these as lost archives. Most scholars now think they are largely references to the canonical Kings, or to prophetic stories within it, given new titles. Whether the Chronicler also had independent pre-exilic sources is the central historical question about the book.

The minimalist answer, associated with W. M. L. de Wette (1806) and Julius Wellhausen (1878), is that he had almost none. A more moderate view, represented by Sara Japhet and H. G. M. Williamson, allows a small number of independent notices, especially about fortifications, building works and military incidents, while treating most additions as the Chronicler's own composition. A. Graeme Auld has argued, in the minority, that Kings and Chronicles both drew on a shorter common source.

## Retribution Theology Reshaping History

The book's guiding principle is **immediate retribution**: a king who seeks God prospers, and a king who sins is punished in his own lifetime. Where Kings did not supply a matching reward or punishment, the Chronicler often provides one.

- **Asa** (2 Chr 16) falls ill in his feet after relying on Aram and on physicians; Kings mentions only the illness.
- **Uzziah** (2 Chr 26) is struck with skin disease for burning incense in the Temple; Kings gives the disease without a cause.
- **Manasseh** (2 Chr 33), the worst king in Kings, nevertheless reigns for 55 years. Chronicles explains this by an Assyrian captivity in Babylon, repentance, and restoration.
- **Josiah** (2 Chr 35) dies at Megiddo because he ignored God's word spoken through Pharaoh Neco.

These patterns show that much of the unique material is theological explanation, not archival recovery.

## Main Historical Problems

### Solomon and the Temple (chapters 1–9)

Chronicles follows 1 Kings 3–11 but removes Solomon's faults, his foreign wives and his apostasy. Mainstream archaeology finds little support for a wealthy Solomonic empire. Tenth-century Jerusalem appears to have been modest. The date of the monumental gates at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer is disputed between the conventional chronology (Yadin, A. Mazar) and Finkelstein's Low Chronology, which moves them into the 9th century. No trace of Solomon's Temple has been excavated, though its tripartite plan has good parallels at 'Ain Dara and Tell Tayinat in Syria.

### Sheshonq I (chapter 12)

The invasion by "Shishak" is the earliest event in the book confirmed by an outside source. Sheshonq I's relief at Karnak lists conquered places in the Negev and the northern kingdom. Chronicles adds Libyan and other troop contingents that fit the 22nd Dynasty well, but the list itself does not mention Jerusalem.

### Fortresses, battles and numbers (chapters 11–20)

The list of Rehoboam's fortresses (11:5–12) may preserve a genuine administrative document, but many scholars date it to the time of Hezekiah or Josiah. The battles of Abijah, Asa (against Zerah the Cushite) and Jehoshaphat (chapter 20) have no external confirmation, and their army figures (up to a million men) are impossible for Iron Age Judah.

### Hezekiah and Sennacherib (chapters 29–32)

This is the best-attested part of the book. Sennacherib's annals (the Taylor and Chicago prisms), the Lachish reliefs, the Siloam tunnel and inscription (compare 2 Chr 32:30), the *lmlk* jar handles and Hezekiah's bullae all fit an era of fortification and Assyrian invasion in 701 BCE. Chronicles shortens the story, omitting Hezekiah's heavy tribute (2 Kgs 18:14–16) that the Assyrian annals also record. Hezekiah's great Passover (chapter 30) is unique to Chronicles and looks modelled on Josiah's.

### Manasseh's captivity (chapter 33)

Assyrian texts name **Manasseh of Judah** as a vassal of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, so he really was under Assyrian control. No Assyrian source mentions his arrest, and the destination Babylon would be odd for an Assyrian king. Some scholars link it to the Babylonian revolt of Shamash-shum-ukin (652–648 BCE). Most consider the episode a theological construction.

### Josiah and the last kings (chapters 34–36)

Josiah's death at Megiddo in 609 BCE fits the movement of Neco II's army to help Assyria, known from the Babylonian Chronicles. Chronicles places Josiah's reform earlier than Kings (from his 8th and 12th years). Its claim that Jehoiakim was taken in chains to Babylon (36:6) is not supported by the Babylonian Chronicle or by Kings. The closing edict of Cyrus (36:22–23, repeating Ezra 1:1–3) fits Persian policy as shown in the Cyrus Cylinder, though the wording is a Jewish composition.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Ancient reception:** The Septuagint called the book *Paraleipomena*, "things left out," treating it as a supplement to Kings. Jewish tradition attributed it to Ezra.
- **Critical scholarship** (de Wette, Wellhausen, Japhet, Williamson, Klein) treats Chronicles as a theological rewriting of Kings for the Persian-period Jerusalem community, focused on the Temple, the Levites and the Davidic line. Unique material is presumed late unless there is a specific reason to think otherwise.
- **Conservative scholarship** (for example R. B. Dillard and K. A. Kitchen) gives the Chronicler's cited sources more weight, explains large numbers as textual corruption or as a different meaning of *'elef* ("thousand" or "unit"), and harmonises chronological discrepancies with Kings.
- **Archaeological work** has shifted some judgments both ways. Discoveries at Lachish, the Siloam tunnel and the Assyrian annals support the late 8th-century setting, while Persian-period Jerusalem's small size helps explain the Chronicler's idealised picture of a vast Davidic and Solomonic kingdom.

## Reliability Assessment

2 Chronicles is **a valuable witness to Judean thought in the Persian period and a secondary witness to the monarchy**. Where it agrees with Kings, it adds little independent evidence. Where it differs, the default should be caution. Its figures for armies and sacrifices are not historical, and many reward-and-punishment episodes are theological explanations.

Even so, a small set of notices unique to Chronicles, such as Hezekiah's water works, building and fortification lists, and some details of Sheshonq's army, may go back to older sources. These are best evaluated case by case against archaeology.
