## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** This is an inventory list adapted from 1 Kings 7:23–50. Large-scale bronze casting is technically possible for the Iron Age, and copper mining in the Arabah in the 10th century BCE is now well documented. But none of the objects survive, the quantities are idealised, and the Chronicler's changes bring the furnishings into line with later ritual practice.

## What the Text Claims

- A **bronze altar** 20 cubits square and 10 high (4:1).
- The cast **"sea"**, 10 cubits across, resting on twelve bronze oxen and holding 3,000 baths (4:2–5).
- Ten basins for washing the burnt offerings, while the sea is for the priests (4:6).
- Ten golden lampstands and ten tables (4:7–8).
- Huram casts the bronze in clay ground in the Jordan plain between Succoth and Zeredah (4:17).
- The bronze is so plentiful it is not weighed (4:18).

## External Evidence

### Copper production

Excavations by Thomas Levy, Erez Ben-Yosef and colleagues at **Khirbet en-Nahas** (Faynan, Jordan) and at **Timna** (Israel) show intensive copper smelting in the 11th–9th centuries BCE, with peaks in the 10th century. Who controlled this industry is debated. Most researchers link it to local, probably Edomite, nomadic groups rather than to a Jerusalem state. Still, the finds show that large quantities of copper were available in the southern Levant at the right period.

### Jordan valley metalworking

Succoth is usually identified with Tell Deir 'Alla, and the region of the Jordan valley around it has produced evidence of Iron Age metalworking, including iron smelting at nearby Tell Hammeh. Clay deposits suitable for moulds are found in the valley, which suits the casting note in 4:17. Zeredah (Zarethan in 1 Kgs 7:46) is not securely identified.

### Parallel objects

Large bronze or stone basins and wheeled stands (1 Kgs 7:27–39, omitted by the Chronicler apart from 4:14) have parallels in miniature: Late Bronze and Iron Age wheeled bronze stands from Cyprus. Monumental bronze basins are known from Assyrian reliefs of the sack of Muṣaṣir (714 BCE), which show large cauldrons at a temple entrance.

## Points of Agreement

- The kinds of objects (a large basin, smaller basins, lampstands, tables, shovels and bowls) fit Near Eastern temple equipment.
- Casting in clay moulds in the Jordan valley is technically sound and geographically specific, which suggests the tradition goes back to someone who knew the region.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 4 | 1 Kings 7 / assessment |
|----------------|------------------------|
| Bronze altar described at the start (4:1) | Absent from the inventory in 1 Kgs 7, though implied in 1 Kgs 8:64; may be added from the Second Temple altar |
| Sea holds 3,000 baths (4:5) | 2,000 baths in 1 Kgs 7:26; about 44,000 litres even on the lower figure, more than the stated dimensions hold |
| Ten lampstands and ten tables (4:7–8) | Kings has ten lampstands and one table; the Chronicler expands |
| Washing roles defined (4:6) | A ritual explanation absent from Kings, reflecting priestly practice |

## Assessment

The chapter follows the Kings inventory with additions that make the Temple fit later ritual law. It cannot be checked against surviving objects, since the bronze was broken up and taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 25:13–17). The most interesting historical point is indirect. The 10th-century copper boom in the Arabah, revealed by recent fieldwork, makes an early royal bronze programme less far-fetched than older critics assumed, although it does not show that Jerusalem controlled the mines.
