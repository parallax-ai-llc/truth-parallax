## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The basic picture of a Judean king buying Lebanese timber and skilled labour from Tyre is plausible for the Iron Age, and a Tyrian king named Hiram is known from Phoenician king lists preserved by Josephus. But the chapter is a free rewriting of 1 Kings 5, and several of its additions, such as Joppa as the timber port and the census of 153,600 foreign labourers, look like the Chronicler's own setting.

## What the Text Claims

- Solomon conscripts 70,000 porters, 80,000 stonecutters and 3,600 overseers (2:2, 18).
- He writes to **Huram** (Hiram) king of Tyre asking for a craftsman and for cedar, cypress and algum timber, offering wheat, barley, wine and oil (2:3–10).
- Huram praises the God of Israel and sends **Huram-abi**, son of a Danite mother and Tyrian father (2:11–14).
- The timber is floated by sea to **Joppa** and carried up to Jerusalem (2:16).
- The labourers are all **resident aliens**, 153,600 counted in a census (2:17–18).

## External Evidence

### Hiram of Tyre

Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.106–127; *Antiquities* 8.144–149) quotes the Hellenistic historians **Menander of Ephesus** and **Dius**, who drew on Tyrian records. They list a king **Hirom son of Abibaal**, who reigned 34 years and exchanged riddles with Solomon. The Tyrian king list is also used to date the founding of Carthage. Most historians accept that a 10th-century Hiram existed, though the Solomon link in these sources may reflect Josephus's or Menander's knowledge of the Bible.

### Phoenician timber trade

Lebanese cedar was exported throughout antiquity. Egyptian texts (the *Report of Wenamun*, c. 1100 BCE) and Assyrian inscriptions describe kings obtaining it from the Phoenician coast. Wenamun pays for timber with goods, showing the kind of exchange 2 Chronicles 2 describes.

### Joppa

Joppa (Jaffa) was a Canaanite and Egyptian port in the Late Bronze Age. In the Persian period the **Eshmunazar II sarcophagus inscription** from Sidon records that the "lord of kings" (the Persian king) granted Dor and Joppa to the Sidonians. Ezra 3:7 has the same route for the Second Temple: cedar shipped to Joppa. Kings does not name the port (1 Kgs 5:9).

## Points of Agreement

- A Tyrian king named Hiram in the 10th century is independently attested, if indirectly.
- Payment in agricultural produce (wheat and oil) matches the economics of the two regions: Phoenicia had timber and limited farmland, and the highlands had grain and oil.
- Phoenician craftsmen and architectural models influenced Levantine temples and palaces.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Chronicles 2 | 1 Kings 5 / assessment |
|----------------|------------------------|
| Labour force is entirely foreign (2:17–18) | 1 Kgs 5:13–14 has 30,000 Israelites on corvée; the Chronicler removes the oppression later blamed for the schism |
| Huram-abi's mother is from Dan (2:14) | From Naphtali in 1 Kgs 7:14; possibly modelled on Oholiab of Dan, the Tabernacle craftsman (Exod 31:6) |
| Timber landed at Joppa (2:16) | Not in Kings; matches the Persian-period route of Ezra 3:7 |
| Huram blesses the God of Israel (2:12) | A literary confession typical of the Chronicler, not a diplomatic document |

## Assessment

The chapter's core is borrowed from 1 Kings 5. The Chronicler adds a long, theologically worded exchange of letters and reshapes the details to echo the Tabernacle story and the Second Temple project. The link between Tyre and Jerusalem is historically credible in general terms, and Hiram himself is likely a real 10th-century king. The specific numbers and the Joppa route are best read as reflecting the author's own Persian-period world.
