## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Manasseh is confirmed by Assyrian records as a loyal vassal of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. But his captivity in Babylon, repentance and restoration (33:11–17) appear only in Chronicles. Kings, which blames Manasseh for the fall of Judah, knows nothing of them. The episode is one of the most debated passages in Chronicles. A historical summons to an Assyrian court is possible, but the story as told serves the Chronicler's theology of repentance.

## What the Text Claims

- Manasseh reigns 55 years and rebuilds the high places, sets up altars to Baal and the host of heaven, burns his sons, and practises divination (33:1–9).
- The commanders of the king of Assyria take Manasseh "with hooks," bind him with bronze chains, and bring him to **Babylon** (33:11).
- In distress he humbles himself and prays; God restores him to Jerusalem (33:12–13).
- He builds an outer wall for the City of David, places commanders in fortified cities, and removes foreign gods from the Temple (33:14–16).
- His prayer is recorded in "the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel" and "the records of the seers" (33:18–19).
- His son Amon reigns two years and is killed by his servants; "the people of the land" kill the conspirators and make Josiah king (33:21–25).

## External Evidence

### Manasseh in Assyrian records

- **Esarhaddon (Nineveh Prism A, c. 673 BCE):** Among 22 kings of the west who supplied building materials for his palace is "Manasseh (Me-na-si-i), king of Judah."
- **Ashurbanipal (Prism C):** Manasseh is listed among the kings who sent troops and gifts to support the Assyrian campaign against Egypt in 667 BCE.

These texts confirm Manasseh as a long-reigning, cooperative vassal. They do not mention any arrest or deportation.

### Possible contexts for a summons

Vassal kings were sometimes summoned to the Assyrian court, and some were taken in chains. Ashurbanipal's records describe the Egyptian prince Necho I being taken to Nineveh in fetters and then sent back to rule. Scholars have proposed two settings for Manasseh's captivity:

- The **revolt of Shamash-shum-ukin**, Ashurbanipal's brother and king of Babylon, in 652–648 BCE. Western vassals may have been suspected of supporting it, and Ashurbanipal was in Babylon after its fall. This would explain why Manasseh was taken to Babylon rather than Nineveh.
- A summons to Esarhaddon, who rebuilt Babylon and sometimes held court there.

Neither is documented for Manasseh.

### Jerusalem's walls

Late Iron Age walls on the eastern slope of the City of David and the expansion of Jerusalem in the 7th century fit a general picture of building under Manasseh, though no wall can be assigned to him with certainty.

### Prayer of Manasseh

The **Prayer of Manasseh**, a short penitential psalm found in some Greek and Latin Bibles and the Apocrypha, claims to be the prayer mentioned in 33:18. It is generally dated to the Hellenistic period and was written to fill the gap.

## Points of Agreement

- Manasseh reigned a long time as an Assyrian vassal.
- His reign was a period of relative stability and recovery after 701 BCE.
- Amon's short reign and assassination, and Josiah's installation by "the people of the land," also appear in 2 Kgs 21:19–26.

## Points of Conflict

| Element | Comment |
|---------|---------|
| Captivity in Babylon | Absent from Kings and Assyrian records |
| Repentance and reform | Absent from Kings, which says Manasseh's sins were so great that Judah's fall could not be averted (2 Kgs 23:26; 24:3–4) |
| Removal of foreign gods | Contradicts 2 Kgs 23:12, where Josiah removes Manasseh's altars |
| Long reign as reward | Chronicles needs repentance to explain why the worst king reigned the longest |

## Assessment

The debate falls into three main positions. Some scholars, including Williamson and many conservative commentators, accept a historical core: Manasseh was summoned or arrested, probably during the Shamash-shum-ukin crisis, and returned as a loyal vassal. Others, following Japhet and William Schniedewind, regard the episode as a theological construction. Under the Chronicler's doctrine of retribution, a 55-year reign required a righteous phase. A third group allows a real detention but considers the repentance and reform to be the Chronicler's interpretation.

The story also mirrors the experience of exile and return. A king of Judah carried to Babylon for sin, who repents and is restored, is a model for the Chronicler's own community. That symbolic fit makes many scholars cautious about treating it as a report.
