## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter preserves a plausible memory of conflict between Jerusalem and neighbouring elites, and its Aramaic correspondence has a credible Persian administrative flavour. But it is arranged thematically, not chronologically: letters from the reigns of Xerxes and Artaxerxes I (after 486 BCE) about the city walls are used to explain a delay in Temple building under Cyrus and Darius (538–520 BCE). The authenticity of the letters is debated.

## What the Text Claims

- "Adversaries of Judah and Benjamin," descendants of people settled by **Esarhaddon**, offer to help build and are refused (4:1–3).
- The "people of the land" frustrate the work through the reign of Cyrus until Darius (4:4–5).
- An accusation is written under **Ahasuerus** (Xerxes) (4:6) and another under **Artaxerxes** by Bishlam, Mithredath, and Tabeel (4:7).
- **Rehum the commander and Shimshai the scribe** write to Artaxerxes that the Jews are rebuilding a rebellious city's walls (4:8–16). The text switches to Aramaic at 4:8.
- Artaxerxes orders a search of the records, finds that Jerusalem has a rebellious past, and halts the work (4:17–23).
- The Temple work stops until the second year of Darius (4:24).

## External Evidence

### Imperial geography and terms

- **"Beyond the River"** (*ʿAbar-Naharā*) is the genuine Persian name for the province west of the Euphrates; it appears in Babylonian documents as *Ebir-nāri*.
- **"The great and noble Osnappar"** (4:10) is generally identified with the Assyrian king **Ashurbanipal**. Assyrian deportation and resettlement of foreign groups in Samaria is well attested in Sargon II's inscriptions, and Esarhaddon (4:2) also conducted deportations.
- The titles *beʿel-ṭeʿem* ("commander" or "chancellor") and *saprā* ("scribe") correspond to real offices in Achaemenid administration.

### Comparative Aramaic correspondence

Real Persian-period letters survive from **Elephantine** (Egypt) and in the **Arsames letters** (leather documents of a satrap of Egypt, published by G. R. Driver). They show addresses, greetings, and file notes similar to those in Ezra. Conservative and moderate scholars, such as Williamson, take this as evidence that Ezra's letters rest on real archive material.

**Lester Grabbe** is more sceptical, noting that the letters are suspiciously favourable to the Jews or theologically convenient. **Dirk Schwiderski**, in a study of Northwest Semitic letter formulae, argued that some of Ezra's greeting and introductory forms resemble those of Hellenistic-period letters more than those of Achaemenid examples. If so, the documents were at least reworked, perhaps composed, well after the Persian period.

### Historical context for a halt under Artaxerxes I

Artaxerxes I faced the revolt of **Inaros** in Egypt (c. 460–454 BCE), and the satrap **Megabyzus** later rebelled. Kenneth Hoglund argued that the empire was then sensitive to fortifications in the Levant. A royal suspicion of a walled Jerusalem fits this context.

## Points of Agreement

- Tension between the returnee community and the Samarian and local elites is confirmed indirectly by Nehemiah and, later, by the Elephantine papyri, which show Samaria and Judah as rival provinces.
- Assyrian resettlement of foreigners in Samaria is historical.
- The chancellery terminology is authentic to the Persian period.

## Points of Conflict

| Ezra 4 | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| Opposition under Cyrus explains the Temple delay | Haggai blames Judean apathy, not outside opposition |
| Letters about the city walls under Xerxes and Artaxerxes | These belong to 486–424 BCE, decades after the Temple was finished in 515 BCE |
| Adversaries descended from Esarhaddon's settlers | The northern population was largely Israelite in origin, as archaeology and later Samaritan identity show; the label serves to delegitimize them |
| Letter formulae | Possibly Hellenistic in form (Schwiderski) |

## Assessment

Ezra 4 is the clearest example of the compiler's thematic arrangement. Verses 6–23 are out of chronological order; most commentators, conservative and critical alike, accept this. The letters may draw on genuine archival documents, as Williamson argues, or be literary reconstructions in Persian style, as Grabbe and Schwiderski suggest. Either way, the chapter's purpose is to define the returnees as the true Israel and the northerners as foreigners, an ideological claim rather than a neutral record.
