## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The list of men with foreign wives has the appearance of an archival document, and the winter assembly in the rain is a vivid, realistic detail. Whether a mass divorce actually took place, and how effective it was, cannot be verified. A passing reference to Jehohanan son of Eliashib is central to the scholarly debate over whether Ezra came before or after Nehemiah.

## What the Text Claims

- Shecaniah proposes a covenant to send away foreign wives and their children (10:1–4).
- Ezra withdraws to the chamber of **Jehohanan son of Eliashib** and fasts (10:6).
- A proclamation summons all returnees to Jerusalem within three days, on pain of confiscation and exclusion (10:7–8).
- On the 20th day of the ninth month (Kislev, December) the people sit in the Temple square, "trembling because of this matter and because of the heavy rain" (10:9).
- Four men oppose the plan (10:15). A commission investigates over three months (10:16–17).
- A list of about 110 offenders is given, beginning with priests of the high priestly family of Jeshua (10:18–44).

## External Evidence

### Johanan the high priest

Nehemiah 12:10–11, 22 gives a high priestly line: Jeshua, Joiakim, **Eliashib**, Joiada, **Johanan** (or Jonathan), and Jaddua. The **Elephantine papyri** include the petition of 407 BCE (**TAD A4.7**, with its draft A4.8) addressed to **Bagavahya (Bagohi), governor of Judah**. It mentions an earlier letter sent to "Jehohanan the high priest and his colleagues the priests in Jerusalem," which had gone unanswered. The petition also mentions **Delaiah and Shelemiah, sons of Sanballat governor of Samaria**.

If Ezra's Jehohanan son of Eliashib is this high priest, active around 410 BCE, then Ezra's mission fits better in 398 BCE (Artaxerxes II) than 458 BCE. Defenders of 458 reply that Johanan and Eliashib were common names, that "son" can mean "grandson," and that Ezra could have used the chamber of a younger man who was not yet high priest. Frank Moore Cross proposed repeated names (papponymy) in the high priestly line, which would weaken the link; that reconstruction is itself debated.

### Winter rain

Kislev falls in the rainy season in Jerusalem, and heavy December rain is typical. The detail is circumstantially accurate.

### Effectiveness

Nehemiah 13:23–28, set in 432 BCE or later, describes the same problem continuing, including a grandson of the high priest Eliashib married to the daughter of Sanballat. If Ezra preceded Nehemiah, his reform did not last. If he followed Nehemiah, the order of the narrative has been rearranged.

## Points of Agreement

- Leading priestly families were involved in marriages with neighbouring elites, as Nehemiah 13:28 independently confirms.
- Names in the list are typical of the Persian period.
- The rain in Kislev is a realistic seasonal detail.
- Confiscation and exclusion as sanctions are consistent with 7:26.

## Points of Conflict

| Ezra 10 | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| Reform appears complete and successful | Nehemiah 13 shows intermarriage continuing, even in the high priestly family |
| Jehohanan son of Eliashib has a Temple chamber | Possibly the high priest of c. 410 BCE (Elephantine), implying a 398 BCE date for Ezra |
| About 110 offenders out of a population of tens of thousands | Very small number, or only elite families recorded |
| Book ends abruptly | 1 Esdras continues directly with Neh 8, suggesting Neh 8 originally followed Ezra 10 |

## Assessment

The chapter preserves a credible memory of an intermarriage dispute in Yehud and a list that may come from an archive. Its value for chronology is debated: the Jehohanan reference is the best single argument for dating Ezra to 398 BCE, but it is not decisive. Mainstream scholarship remains divided, with Williamson favouring 458 and others, including many continental scholars, favouring 398 or treating the Ezra story as largely literary.
