## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Uncertain.** Artaxerxes' letter (7:12–26) is written in plausible Persian-period Aramaic with Persian loanwords, but its sweeping grant of authority to a Judean priest is hard to credit as it stands. The date (458 or 398 BCE) is disputed, and Ezra himself is unattested outside the biblical tradition. The chapter matters greatly for debates about how the Torah became authoritative.

## What the Text Claims

- Ezra son of Seraiah, of Aaronic descent back to Aaron, is "a scribe skilled in the law of Moses" (7:1–6).
- He goes up from Babylon in the **seventh year of Artaxerxes** with priests, Levites, and temple servants, arriving in the fifth month (7:7–9).
- The Aramaic rescript names Ezra "priest, scribe of the law of the God of heaven" (7:12), allows any Israelite to go with him, sends silver and gold for sacrifices, exempts temple staff from taxes, and orders treasurers of Beyond the River to provide up to 100 talents of silver (7:13–24).
- Ezra is to appoint magistrates and judges for all "who know the laws of your God," and to punish disobedience with death, banishment, confiscation, or imprisonment (7:25–26).
- Ezra blesses God in the first person (7:27–28).

## External Evidence

### Which Artaxerxes?

- **Artaxerxes I** (465–424 BCE): seventh year = **458 BCE**. Ezra precedes Nehemiah (445 BCE).
- **Artaxerxes II** (404–359 BCE): seventh year = **398 BCE**. Ezra follows Nehemiah.

Arguments for 398 rest mostly on Ezra 10:6 and the Elephantine papyri (see chapter 10). A few scholars, following John Bright, emended the text to the 37th year (428 BCE). Williamson and Blenkinsopp, among others, favour 458; the question is not settled.

### Language and titles

The rescript uses Persian loanwords such as *dāt* ("law"), *ʾadrazdā* ("diligently"), and *ganzê* ("treasuries"). H. H. Schaeder argued that "scribe of the law of the God of heaven" was a Persian official title, something like a commissioner for Jewish affairs. This remains a hypothesis; no Persian source confirms such an office.

### The imperial authorization hypothesis

**Peter Frei** proposed that Persian authorities formally adopted local norms as imperial law for each community. Examples cited include:

- The **Demotic Chronicle** report that Darius I ordered Egyptian laws collected and written down.
- The trilingual **Letoon inscription** from Xanthos (Lycia, 337 BCE), in which a satrap ratifies a local cult regulation.
- The Elephantine **Passover Letter** (TAD A4.1, 419 BCE), conveying instructions about the festival to the Jewish garrison under Darius II.

On this view, Ezra 7 reflects the Torah becoming imperially sanctioned law for Yehud. Critics in James Watts's volume *Persia and Torah* (2001) argued that the examples are few and local, that the Letoon is a cult rule rather than a law code, and that no systematic policy can be proven. Many now think the Persian role was at most indirect.

## Points of Agreement

- Persian kings did delegate religious and legal matters to local elites and occasionally sponsored temple cults.
- The Aramaic is consistent with Imperial Aramaic of the Achaemenid period.
- Tax exemptions for temple personnel have parallels, such as the disputed Gadatas letter.

## Points of Conflict

| Ezra 7 | Problem |
|--------|---------|
| Up to 100 talents of silver from provincial treasuries | A huge sum for a small temple community |
| Authority over all Jews in Beyond the River, including capital punishment | Unparalleled jurisdiction for a priest without governor's rank; Nehemiah never mentions it |
| Persian king writes in Judean theological terms | Suggests composition or heavy editing by a Judean writer |
| Ezra's genealogy (7:1–5) | Skips generations and makes Ezra a son of Seraiah, the high priest executed in 587 BCE (2 Kgs 25:18–21) |
| No extra-biblical mention of Ezra | Absent from Ben Sira's praise of heroes (Sir 49) |

## Assessment

Williamson argues for an authentic core to the rescript, reworked by a Jewish scribe. Grabbe and Pakkala are more sceptical, seeing it as a literary creation intended to give the Torah and the returnee leadership royal legitimacy. Whatever its origin, the chapter shows that by the time Ezra-Nehemiah was compiled, the "law of Moses" was imagined as a written book, the community's constitution under Persian sovereignty. That is valuable evidence for the history of the Torah's authority, even if the episode itself cannot be verified.
