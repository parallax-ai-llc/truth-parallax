## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (unverifiable climax with plausible background).** The sudden fall of a powerful minister at a word from the queen is plausible in the context of Achaemenid court politics. The scene itself, with Haman falling on the queen's couch and being hanged on his own stake, is crafted for maximum dramatic irony and has no external confirmation.

## What the Text Claims

- At the second banquet Esther reveals that she and her people have been "sold to be destroyed" and names Haman (7:1–6).
- The king goes out to the garden in anger; Haman throws himself on Esther's **couch** to plead (7:7–8).
- The king takes this as an assault on the queen; Haman's face is covered (7:8).
- The eunuch **Harbona** points out the 50-cubit stake, and Haman is hanged (impaled) on it; the king's anger subsides (7:9–10).

## External Evidence

### The power of royal women

Greek sources describe Persian queens and queen mothers wielding real influence over the fate of courtiers. **Herodotus** recounts Amestris' revenge on the wife of Masistes (9.108–113). **Ctesias** (preserved in Photius' summary of his *Persica*) tells of Parysatis, mother of Artaxerxes II, securing the deaths of those she blamed for her son Cyrus' death, and of Amestris obtaining the execution of the Egyptian rebel Inaros. Plutarch's *Life of Artaxerxes* draws on the same traditions. The idea that a queen could bring down a powerful official is therefore historically realistic, even if Esther and Haman themselves are unattested.

### Couches and banquets

Persians ate and drank reclining on couches, as the Greek sources and later Achaemenid-style iconography show. Haman falling on the couch where Esther was reclining (7:8) fits this setting.

### Restrictions near royal women

Plutarch (*Artaxerxes* 27) reports that Persian custom strictly forbade men from approaching or even passing near the king's concubines. Haman's breach of the queen's space would, in Persian eyes, be a grave offense, though the king's charge of assault is clearly ironic.

### Covering the face

The covering of Haman's face (7:8) is not otherwise attested as a Persian judicial custom. Later Greek and Roman sources mention covering the heads of the condemned, but there is no direct Persian parallel.

### Execution

As noted on the chapter 5 page, impalement was a standard Persian punishment for rebels and traitors (Behistun inscription; Herodotus 3.159).

## Points of Agreement

- Royal women at the Achaemenid court could influence the punishment of high officials.
- Reclining banquets and segregation of royal women fit Persian custom.
- Impalement was a Persian execution method.

## Points of Conflict

| Esther 7 | Historical consideration |
|----------|--------------------------|
| A grand vizier "Haman" executed under Xerxes | No such minister or execution is recorded |
| The vizier hanged on the stake he built for his enemy | A classic poetic-justice motif (cf. Ps 7:15–16), not a historical datum |

## Assessment

Chapter 7 is the dramatic climax of the story. Its background, a queen's influence, court etiquette around royal women, and Persian forms of execution, is realistic and paralleled in Greek accounts of Achaemenid court intrigue. The precise symmetry of Haman's fate, however, belongs to narrative art. As with the rest of the book, the author gives plausible color to an unverifiable plot.
