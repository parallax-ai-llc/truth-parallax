## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (realistic customs, literary plot).** Recording and rewarding the king's "benefactors" was a genuine Persian institution, and wearing the king's robe was a recognized sign of royal favor. The chapter's chain of coincidences and its comic reversal are the work of a storyteller.

## What the Text Claims

- The king cannot sleep and has the **book of memorable deeds, the chronicles**, read to him (6:1).
- He learns that Mordecai's service in exposing the plot of Bigthan and Teresh was **never rewarded** (6:2–3).
- Haman, arriving to request Mordecai's execution, is asked how to honor "the man whom the king delights to honor" and proposes a **royal robe**, a **horse the king has ridden** with a **royal crown** on its head, and a public procession (6:4–9).
- Haman must lead Mordecai through the city square in this way (6:10–11). His wife and advisers predict his downfall (6:13).

## External Evidence

### Royal records and benefactors

- **Herodotus 8.85** reports that Persian "benefactors of the king" were called *orosangai* and that the names of those who served the king were recorded. He mentions two Samian captains rewarded with land for their deeds at Salamis.
- **Herodotus 8.90** says that during the battle of Salamis Xerxes watched from the shore, and whenever he saw one of his men perform a notable deed, his secretaries wrote down the captain's name, father, and city.
- **Ezra 4:15 and 6:1–2** refer to Persian archives searched for earlier decrees, and **Ctesias** (cited by Diodorus 2.32.4) claimed to have used the "royal parchments" in which the Persians recorded their history.

The idea that the king kept a written record of services owed is therefore well grounded.

### Rewards and the royal robe

Persian kings rewarded service with land, robes, horses, golden jewelry, and other gifts, as Xenophon (*Anabasis* 1.2.27) and Herodotus (3.84, 8.85) report. Wearing the king's own clothing was a mark of exceptional favor. In **Herodotus 7.15–17**, Xerxes has his uncle Artabanus put on the royal robes and sleep in the royal bed in order to test a dream. The Greek reports of the robe Xerxes gave to Artaynte (9.109–111) show that such a gift could carry royal status.

### The crowned horse

The detail of a royal crown placed on the horse's head (6:8) is not attested elsewhere, though Assyrian and Achaemenid reliefs show horses with elaborate top-knots and headgear. Commentators are divided over whether the crown is on the horse or on the rider.

## Points of Agreement

- The Persian court recorded services to the king and rewarded them.
- Royal robes and horses were real symbols of the king's favor.
- Public honors for royal benefactors fit Persian ideology.

## Points of Conflict

| Esther 6 | Historical consideration |
|----------|--------------------------|
| A major service left unrewarded for five years | Contrary to the Persian ideal of prompt reward, though not impossible |
| Haman arrives just as the king reads the record | A dramatic coincidence typical of court tales |

## Assessment

Chapter 6 rests on a solid Persian background: the institution of "benefactors," royal record-keeping, and gifts of robes and horses. The chapter's plot, the sleepless night, the timely reading, and Haman's misunderstanding, is a masterpiece of comic irony and reversal. It belongs to the genre of the court tale (compare Joseph, Daniel, and Ahiqar), not to historical record.
