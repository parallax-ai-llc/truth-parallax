## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low for the events; high as evidence for a Jewish festival.** The empire-wide fighting and the killing of 75,000 people are unattested anywhere and are best understood as part of a festival legend. The chapter is, however, genuine evidence that a Diaspora festival called Purim existed and was being given a written foundation, and its explanation of the name from *pur* ("lot") is linguistically sound.

## What the Text Claims

- On 13 Adar the Jews strike down their enemies; in Susa the citadel they kill 500 men and the **ten sons of Haman**, whose names are listed (9:1–10).
- At Esther's request, the Jews of Susa are allowed a **second day** of fighting (14 Adar), killing 300 more; Haman's sons' bodies are hung (9:11–15).
- In the provinces the Jews kill **75,000** of their enemies but "laid no hand on the plunder" (9:16).
- Rural Jews celebrate on 14 Adar, those in Susa on 15 Adar (9:17–19).
- **Mordecai** writes letters establishing the annual festival, and **Esther** writes a second letter confirming it (9:20–32). It is called **Purim** "after the word *pur*" (9:24–26).

## External Evidence

### No record of the violence

Fighting on this scale across the Persian empire would surely have left some trace in Babylonian, Elamite, Egyptian, or Greek sources. None exists. The Elephantine papyri, from a Jewish community in Persian Egypt later in the 5th century BCE, show no awareness of such events. The Septuagint gives the number killed as **15,000** rather than 75,000 (9:16), which already shows the figure's instability in transmission.

### The names of Haman's sons

Haman's ten sons (9:7–9) have names that many scholars regard as Persian or Persian-like (e.g., Parshandatha, Aridatha, Vaizatha). Some can be plausibly analyzed as Old Iranian, others are uncertain. Like the other names in the book, they show the author's familiarity with the Persian milieu, not the historicity of the individuals. In Hebrew manuscripts the names are traditionally written in a special columnar layout, a scribal custom tied to their liturgical reading.

### Purim and *pūru*

The word **pur** is the Akkadian *pūru*, "lot," and the book itself explains it for Hebrew readers. The inscribed clay die of **Iahali**, a 9th-century Assyrian official, calls itself a *pūru* and was used in a lot-drawing ritual. This confirms the meaning, though not the story.

### The earliest outside evidence for the festival

- **2 Maccabees 15:36** (late 2nd or 1st century BCE) mentions 13 Adar as "the day before Mordecai's day." This is the earliest datable reference to the festival outside Esther.
- The festival is absent from the **Dead Sea Scrolls** calendars, and Esther is the only book of the Hebrew Bible not found at Qumran. This may reflect the Qumran community's calendar or its disapproval of a festival not grounded in the Torah.
- The **Greek colophon** to Esther (Addition F) describes the book as "the preceding letter about Purim" brought to Egypt in the 4th year of Ptolemy and Cleopatra, most likely 78/77 BCE.

### Possible origins of the festival

Scholars have proposed that Purim began as a Diaspora Jewish adaptation of a non-Jewish festival, perhaps a Persian New Year or a Babylonian festival, and in the late 19th century some (e.g., Peter Jensen) linked Mordecai and Esther with the Babylonian deities Marduk and Ishtar and Haman with an Elamite god. These ideas remain hypotheses. What the chapter shows clearly is a **festival etiology**: a story told to explain why Jews celebrate on 14 and 15 Adar, why the festival includes feasting and gifts (9:22), and why it has two dates.

### Literary layers

Verses 20–32, with the letters of Mordecai and Esther, read like an appendix designed to give the festival authority, and many scholars think they were added to an earlier form of the story. The repetition and shift of style support this.

## Points of Agreement

- *Pur* means "lot" in Akkadian, and its explanation in the text is correct.
- A Jewish festival in Adar connected with Mordecai is attested by the 2nd–1st century BCE.

## Points of Conflict

| Esther 9 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| Jews kill 75,000 enemies across the empire | No external evidence; LXX gives 15,000 |
| 800 killed in Susa over two days | No record |
| Festival founded by royal-court letters of Mordecai and Esther | Earliest outside reference is 2 Macc 15:36, centuries later |

## Assessment

Chapter 9 is a festival legend with a historically important core: it documents the emergence of Purim in the Jewish Diaspora and links it to a story of deliverance. The massacres it describes are not historical and reflect a literary pattern of total reversal. Their violence has troubled readers ancient and modern. The chapter's real historical value lies in what it reveals about the life and celebrations of Jewish communities in the late Persian and Hellenistic periods.
