## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The three-verse epilogue claims that Mordecai's greatness was recorded in "the book of the chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia." No such record of a Jewish second-in-command under Xerxes survives, and the Greek sources on Xerxes' reign do not know of him.

## What the Text Claims

- Xerxes imposes **tribute on the land and the coastlands** of the sea (10:1).
- His deeds and the greatness of Mordecai are written in "the **Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Media and Persia**" (10:2).
- Mordecai is **second only to the king**, great among the Jews, seeking the welfare of his people (10:3).

## External Evidence

### Tribute

Achaemenid kings collected tribute from all their lands. **Herodotus 3.89–97** lists the tribute of the satrapies under Darius, and the reliefs of the **Apadana at Persepolis** show delegations from across the empire bringing gifts. "Coastlands" (*iyyim*) fits the Persian control of Mediterranean coasts and islands, though after Xerxes' defeat in Greece (480–479 BCE) the Persians lost control of most Aegean islands. The verse is therefore broadly accurate in general terms.

### Royal chronicles

Persian archives certainly existed. **Ezra 6:1–2** describes a search of the archives at Ecbatana that produced Cyrus' decree; the Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets are administrative archives. **Ctesias** claimed to have read the Persians' "royal parchments" (Diodorus 2.32.4). No narrative chronicle of the Achaemenid kings has survived, however, so the reference in 10:2 cannot be checked. It imitates the formula of Kings ("are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel/Judah?"), which suggests a literary convention.

### "Media and Persia"

Elsewhere Esther usually writes "Persia and Media" (1:3, 14, 18), reflecting the Achaemenid order. Here the order is "Media and Persia," as in Daniel (e.g., 5:28; 6:8). The variation may reflect a different hand or simply stylistic convention.

### A Jewish second-in-command

Greek historians record many of Xerxes' high officials and relatives, including Mardonius, Artabanus, Masistes, and Hydarnes. None corresponds to Mordecai. The idea of a Jew rising to second place at a foreign court is a recurring theme of Jewish court tales (Joseph in Gen 41:40–43; Daniel in Dan 2:48; 6:3), which points to a literary pattern.

### The Greek additions

In the Septuagint the book continues with **Addition F**, which interprets Mordecai's dream (Addition A) and explicitly credits God with the deliverance, followed by the **colophon** describing the Greek translation's arrival in Egypt. These additions show how the book was reshaped for Hellenistic Jewish readers.

## Points of Agreement

- Persian kings imposed tribute across their lands and coastlands.
- Persian royal archives existed.

## Points of Conflict

| Esther 10 | Historical record |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Mordecai second to Xerxes | Not known to Greek historians, who name many of Xerxes' top officials |
| His deeds recorded in Persian royal chronicles | No such chronicle survives; the formula imitates Kings |

## Assessment

The epilogue gives the story a formal close, modeled on the regnal summaries of Kings and on the court-tale ideal of the Jewish courtier who rises to high office. Its general statements about Persian tribute and archives are reasonable. Its specific claim about Mordecai, like the rest of the plot, has no external support.
