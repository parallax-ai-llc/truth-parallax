## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (unverifiable narrative with realistic touches).** The chapter's motifs, the king's open-ended promise and the banquet as a setting for petitions, match stories told about Xerxes by Herodotus. The events themselves are private and unattested, and the 50-cubit "tree" is a storyteller's exaggeration.

## What the Text Claims

- On the third day Esther stands in the inner court; the king holds out his golden scepter (5:1–2).
- He promises her anything "even to half of my kingdom" (5:3, 6).
- She invites the king and Haman to a banquet, then to a second one the next day (5:4–8).
- Enraged again by Mordecai, Haman, on the advice of his wife **Zeresh** and his friends, has a "tree" (*'ets*) **fifty cubits** high prepared for Mordecai (5:9–14).

## External Evidence

### The king's open promise

Herodotus tells a striking parallel about Xerxes himself. At the royal birthday feast, at which, according to Herodotus, the Persian king was obliged to grant any request, Amestris asked for the wife of Masistes, and Xerxes, bound by custom, had to agree (9.110–111). Earlier, Xerxes had promised his mistress Artaynte anything she wished and was forced to give her a robe woven by Amestris (9.109). These stories show that Greek writers knew a tradition of Persian kings making rash promises at banquets. Esther 5 uses the same motif. The parallel shows the motif was current; it does not confirm the Esther episode.

### Persian banqueting

Royal and noble banquets were central to Persian court life and political communication. Herodotus (1.133) notes that Persians deliberated important matters while drinking. The Persepolis Fortification Tablets record large provisions for the "king's table," and Greek sources such as Heraclides of Cyme (quoted by Athenaeus 4.145–146) describe the scale of the royal table.

### The "tree" and impalement

The Hebrew *'ets* (tree, wooden stake) in Esther is usually translated "gallows," but the Persian method of public execution that it most probably reflects is **impalement** or exposure on a stake. Darius' **Behistun inscription** repeatedly records that rebels were impaled (e.g., §§32–33, 43), and Herodotus (3.159) says Darius impaled about 3,000 leading Babylonians after the city's revolt. A stake of **fifty cubits** (about 22–23 m) is, however, far beyond any practical need and is generally taken as exaggeration for comic and dramatic effect.

### Names

Zeresh and many other names in Esther (Haman, the eunuchs, Haman's sons in 9:7–9) have been analyzed as Persian or Elamite. Some of these analyses are secure, others speculative. The presence of plausible Iranian names supports the author's familiarity with the Persian world.

## Points of Agreement

- The royal promise of "up to half the kingdom" fits a motif told of Xerxes in Herodotus.
- Banquets as settings for petitions and decisions fit Persian court culture.
- Impalement on a stake was a Persian punishment.

## Points of Conflict

| Esther 5 | Historical consideration |
|----------|--------------------------|
| A 50-cubit stake erected overnight | Implausible scale; literary exaggeration |
| The queen's banquets for the king and his vizier | Unattested; the episode is shaped for dramatic irony |

## Assessment

Chapter 5 shows how the author adapted court motifs familiar from wider Persian lore. The king's rash promise and the banquet setting are told of Xerxes in Herodotus, which suggests that such stories circulated widely in the eastern Mediterranean and Near East. Esther 5 is better understood as a skillful use of this lore than as a record of an actual audience.
