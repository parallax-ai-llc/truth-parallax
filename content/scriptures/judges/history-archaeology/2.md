## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (theological framework).** This chapter is the editorial program of the book, not a historical report. It does, however, accurately reflect the religious environment of Iron Age Canaan, where Baal and Astarte were widely worshipped.

## What the Text Claims

- The angel of the LORD goes up from Gilgal to Bochim and rebukes Israel for making covenants with the inhabitants of the land (2:1–5).
- Joshua dies at 110 and is buried at Timnath-heres (2:6–10), repeating Joshua 24:28–31.
- A new generation "did not know the LORD" and served "the Baals and the Ashtaroth" (2:11–13).
- A cycle is announced: apostasy, oppression, crying out, a judge raised up, deliverance, and relapse after the judge's death (2:14–19).
- The nations are left in the land to test Israel (2:20–23).

## External Evidence

### Baal and Astarte

The **Ugaritic texts** from Ras Shamra (14th–13th centuries BCE) describe Baal, the storm god, and Astarte (*ʿAṯtartu*) as leading deities of the Canaanite world. Astarte also appears in Egyptian New Kingdom texts and later in Phoenician inscriptions. The plural forms "Baals" and "Ashtaroth" reflect the many local manifestations of these gods. Baal names (e.g. Jerubbaal, Eshbaal) also occur among Israelites in the early period.

### Israelite religion in Iron I and II

Archaeology shows that the line between "Israelite" and "Canaanite" religion was blurred for centuries. Examples include the Iron I **Bull Site** in northern Samaria, with its bronze bull figurine, and the 8th-century inscriptions at **Kuntillet ʿAjrud** and **Khirbet el-Qom** that bless by "Yahweh and his Asherah." Judges presents such practices as apostasy, but they were probably a normal part of early Israelite religion.

### Timnath-heres

Joshua 24:30 names the burial place Timnath-*serah*; here it is Timnath-*heres* ("portion of the sun"). The site is often identified with Khirbet Tibnah in the western hills of Ephraim. The variant may be a deliberate scribal change to avoid a solar name, or the reverse.

## Points of Agreement

- Baal and Astarte were real and widely venerated in the region.
- Israelites used Baal names and engaged in practices later condemned, consistent with the chapter's charge of persistent syncretism.
- The continued presence of non-Israelite populations in the land matches the archaeological picture.

## Points of Conflict

- The cycle of apostasy and repentance is a theological pattern, not an observed historical rhythm.
- The idea of a pure Yahwism followed by "falling away" reverses the likely historical development. Most historians think Israelite monotheism emerged gradually, becoming dominant only in the late monarchy and exile.
- The doublet of Joshua's death shows editorial seams between the books of Joshua and Judges.

## Assessment

Judges 2 is the clearest statement of the **Deuteronomistic theology** that shapes the whole book. Its language closely resembles Deuteronomy and 2 Kings 17, which explains the fall of the northern kingdom in similar terms. For the historian, its value lies in what it reveals about the editors' late monarchic or exilic outlook, and in its accurate naming of the Canaanite gods Israel was tempted to serve.
