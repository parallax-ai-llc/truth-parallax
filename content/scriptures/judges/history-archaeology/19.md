## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** This story is a literary and political narrative, closely modelled on Genesis 19, that casts Gibeah, Saul's hometown, in the darkest light. Its geography is realistic, but it contains no verifiable event, and its purpose appears to be polemical.

## What the Text Claims

- A Levite from Ephraim retrieves his concubine from her father's house in Bethlehem (19:1–9).
- Refusing to stay in **Jebus** ("that is, Jerusalem"), a city of foreigners, they lodge at **Gibeah** of Benjamin (19:10–15).
- An old man takes them in; the men of the city surround the house and demand the Levite; the concubine is abused all night and dies (19:16–28).
- The Levite cuts her body into twelve pieces and sends them throughout Israel (19:29–30).

## External Evidence

### Gibeah

Gibeah is traditionally identified with **Tell el-Ful**, north of Jerusalem. W. F. Albright excavated there in 1922–1923 and 1933, and Paul Lapp in 1964. Albright described an Iron I settlement destroyed by fire and a later fortress he attributed to Saul. Later reassessments (e.g. by Patrick Arnold and by Israel Finkelstein) have questioned both the identification and the stratigraphy. Some scholars place biblical Gibeah at **Jabaʿ** instead. The Iron I remains at Tell el-Ful are modest in any case.

### Jebus and Jerusalem

The Bible equates Jebus with Jerusalem (19:10; 1 Chr 11:4). But no extra-biblical source calls Jerusalem "Jebus." The city is *Urusalim* in the 14th-century **Amarna letters** and *Rušalimum* in the earlier Execration Texts. J. Maxwell Miller argued that "Jebus" was originally a separate settlement north of Jerusalem and that the identification is a later scribal assumption.

### Dismemberment as a summons

A letter from the **Mari** archives (18th century BCE) reports a proposal to cut off a prisoner's head and parade it among villages so that the men would assemble for military service. Saul summons Israel by cutting up oxen and sending the pieces (1 Sam 11:7). The Levite's act belongs to this symbolic world of mobilization by threat.

## Points of Agreement

- The route from Bethlehem past Jerusalem to Gibeah and the hill country of Ephraim is geographically accurate, a journey of a day.
- Jerusalem was a non-Israelite (Jebusite) town before David, according to the biblical tradition, and its Late Bronze rulers in the Amarna letters were Canaanite.
- Customs of hospitality, and the vulnerability of travellers at night, fit ancient Near Eastern norms.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 19 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Story closely parallels Sodom (Gen 19) | Literary dependence (in one direction or the other), not independent history |
| Jebus = Jerusalem | The name "Jebus" is not attested outside the Bible |
| Gibeah identified with Tell el-Ful | Identification and stratigraphy debated |
| Dismemberment into twelve pieces | Assumes a twelve-tribe Israel that is not attested in Iron I |

## Assessment

Most scholars read chapters 19–21 as a late composition aimed at discrediting the house of Saul. Gibeah was Saul's capital, Benjamin his tribe, and Jabesh-gilead his ally (1 Sam 11; 31). By contrast, Bethlehem, David's town, is the place of hospitality. The story's heavy use of the Sodom narrative and its framing by the "no king in Israel" refrain point to a writer arguing for the monarchy, and specifically for the Davidic line. It tells us more about political debates in Judah than about events in the Iron I period.
