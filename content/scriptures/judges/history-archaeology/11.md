## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Jephthah may be a genuine Gileadite strongman, and his story reflects the social world of Transjordanian clans. However, his diplomatic speech contains an obvious error about Ammon's god and depends on literary traditions from Numbers. The 300-year figure is an editorial calculation.

## What the Text Claims

- Jephthah, son of a prostitute, is driven out by his half-brothers and leads a band of "worthless men" in the land of Tob (11:1–3).
- Gilead's elders recall him to fight Ammon and make him their head at Mizpah (11:4–11).
- Jephthah argues with the Ammonite king that Israel took the land from **Sihon** the Amorite, not from Ammon; he asks, "Will you not possess what **Chemosh** your god gives you?" (11:12–28).
- Israel has held Heshbon and its towns for **300 years** (11:26).
- He vows to sacrifice whatever first comes out of his house; his daughter comes out, and he fulfils the vow (11:29–40).

## External Evidence

### Chemosh

**Chemosh** was the national god of **Moab**, as the **Mesha Stele** shows. Ammon's god was **Milcom** (1 Kgs 11:5, 33). The speech's arguments also concern Moabite territory (the Arnon, Sihon of Heshbon). Most scholars conclude that the speech was composed from Numbers 20–22 and Deuteronomy 2 and was originally about Moab, or that the author confused the two peoples.

### Heshbon

Heshbon is identified with **Tell Hesban**, excavated by Andrews University teams from 1968. The excavators found no significant Late Bronze occupation and only modest Iron I remains. That makes Sihon's "city of Heshbon" difficult to place in the Late Bronze or early Iron Age. Some conservative scholars propose that biblical Heshbon was at a different site, such as Tell Jalul. The question remains open.

### Child sacrifice

Sacrifice of a child in a moment of crisis is attested in the region. 2 Kings 3:27 reports that the king of Moab sacrificed his son on the city wall, and the **tophet** precincts at Carthage and other Phoenician colonies contain urns of infant remains that most scholars interpret as sacrificial. Greek tradition offers a close literary parallel in Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia.

### Land of Tob

Tob is mentioned again in 2 Samuel 10:6 as an Aramean region north of Gilead. Bands of displaced men led by a chieftain recall the *ʿapiru* of the Amarna letters, outlaws and mercenaries on the margins of society.

## Points of Agreement

- Outlaw bands and chieftains on the fringes of settled society are well attested in the Late Bronze and Iron Ages.
- Elders negotiating with a strongman reflects a kin-based polity without a king.
- Mizpah of Gilead and the land of Tob fit northern Transjordanian geography.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 11 | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Chemosh as Ammon's god | Chemosh was Moab's god; Ammon's was Milcom |
| 300 years since Israel took Heshbon | An editorial figure; conflicts with the shorter span of Iron I |
| Sihon's Heshbon | Tell Hesban shows no Late Bronze city |
| Ammonite kingdom with a king | Ammonite statehood clearly attested only from the 9th century |

## Assessment

The Jephthah tradition probably preserves a local Gileadite hero story, including the tragic vow and the annual mourning ritual for his daughter (11:39–40). That ritual may be an aetiology for a women's festival. The long speech is a later insertion that rehearses the conquest traditions of Numbers and Deuteronomy, and its confusion of Ammon with Moab exposes its secondary character. The 300 years is consistent with the editors' long chronology, but not with archaeology.
