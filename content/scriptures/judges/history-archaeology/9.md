## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate.** The Abimelech story is the most "political" episode in Judges and has the strongest archaeological anchor. Shechem's fortress-temple, its destruction in the 12th century, and the city's long tradition as a regional power all fit the narrative well. The identification of the excavated temple with the "house of El-berith" remains an inference, and the dating of the destruction is debated.

## What the Text Claims

- Abimelech, Gideon's son by a Shechemite concubine, persuades the "lords of Shechem" to make him king, funded with 70 silver pieces from the **house of Baal-berith**; he kills 70 brothers on one stone (9:1–6).
- **Jotham**, the surviving brother, tells the fable of the trees from **Mount Gerizim** (9:7–21).
- After three years, Shechem revolts under Gaal son of Ebed; Abimelech destroys the city and sows it with salt (9:22–45).
- The people of the **Tower of Shechem** take refuge in the stronghold of the **house of El-berith**; Abimelech burns it with about 1,000 inside (9:46–49).
- At **Thebez**, a woman drops a millstone on Abimelech's head (9:50–57).

## External Evidence

### Shechem in the Amarna letters

Shechem (Tell Balata, near Nablus) was a major city-state in the Late Bronze Age. In the **Amarna letters** (14th century BCE), its ruler **Labʾayu** expanded his power across the highlands, and his sons continued his policies after his death (EA 252–254, 289). This shows that a Shechem-based ruler building a small highland kingdom with local support had real precedent.

### The fortress-temple at Tell Balata

Ernst Sellin excavated at Shechem in 1913–1914 and 1926–1927. The **Drew-McCormick expedition** led by G. Ernest Wright (1956–1973) uncovered a massive **fortress-temple** (*migdal*) on the acropolis, about 26 by 21 metres with walls over 5 metres thick, originally built in the Middle Bronze Age and reused in the Late Bronze Age. Wright linked it to the "tower of Shechem" and the "house of El-berith."

The excavators reported a destruction of the city in the **12th century BCE** (Stratum XI), followed by a gap in occupation. Wright and later Lawrence Stager associated this with Abimelech. Stager (2003) argued in detail that the fortress-temple matches the biblical description.

### Debate over the stratigraphy

Some scholars, including Israel Finkelstein, have questioned the dating and interpretation of the Shechem strata, since the excavations predated modern methods and were published slowly. The building's use in Iron I and the precise date of its destruction are not beyond dispute.

### Jotham's fable

Plant fables are well known in the ancient Near East, for example in Mesopotamian disputation poems. Jotham's fable fits this genre and also resembles the parable of the thistle and the cedar in 2 Kings 14:9.

## Points of Agreement

- Shechem was a powerful, independent city with a long tradition of local kingship.
- A massive fortified temple stood on its acropolis.
- The city suffered destruction around the 12th century BCE.
- The divine name El-berith ("El of the covenant") fits Canaanite religious terminology.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 9 | Issue |
|----------|-------|
| Baal-berith (9:4) vs. El-berith (9:46) | Editorial alternation, perhaps polemical |
| Abimelech "king over Israel" (9:22) | His rule was over Shechem and its surroundings |
| City sown with salt (9:45) | A literary curse motif also known from ANE treaties and inscriptions |
| Date of Shechem's destruction | Linked to Abimelech by inference; stratigraphy debated |

## Assessment

Chapter 9 stands out from the cycle of judges. It has no oppressor, no deliverer, and a non-Israelite city at its centre. It reads like a local chronicle of a failed attempt at kingship in a Canaanite–Israelite city. The archaeological match with Tell Balata is one of the better correlations in the book, although it is not proof. The episode also serves the editors' argument against illegitimate kingship, which may explain why it was preserved.
