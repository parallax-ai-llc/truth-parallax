## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** Seasonal raids by eastern pastoralists on highland farmers are entirely plausible, and Gideon's clan name is anchored in later administrative records. However, the massive camel-borne invasion reflects a later period, and the call narrative and miracles belong to religious story rather than history.

## What the Text Claims

- For seven years, Midianites, Amalekites, and "people of the East" invade at harvest time with tents and **camels without number**, destroying crops as far as Gaza (6:1–6).
- Israelites hide in caves and strongholds (6:2); Gideon threshes wheat in a winepress at **Ophrah of the Abiezrites** (6:11).
- An angel commissions Gideon; he tears down his father's Baal altar and Asherah pole and is renamed **Jerubbaal** (6:11–32).
- Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali muster; the fleece signs confirm the call (6:33–40).

## External Evidence

### Midian

Midian lay in northwest Arabia (the Hejaz). Excavations at **Qurayyah** and finds of "Midianite" (Qurayyah) painted pottery at **Timna** in the Arabah show an active Midianite culture in the 13th–12th centuries BCE. Their reach into the Jezreel Valley is not independently attested, but raiding by desert groups on settled zones is a recurring pattern in Near Eastern history.

### Camels

Domesticated camels used on a large scale are a problem for an Iron I setting. Studies of animal bones in the Aravah copper mines (Sapir-Hen and Ben-Yosef, 2013) indicate that domestic camels appear in the southern Levant in significant numbers only in the late 10th century BCE. Assyrian records of the 9th–8th centuries show Arab groups fighting on camels. The image of camel-riding raiders "like locusts" probably reflects the experience of later centuries.

### Abiezer and Ophrah

The **Samaria ostraca** (early 8th century BCE) record deliveries of wine and oil from clan districts of Manasseh, including **Abiezer**, the clan of Gideon. This confirms that Abiezer was a real kin group of the Manasseh hill country. Ophrah itself has not been securely identified.

### The name Jerubbaal

In 2021 an ink inscription on a jar from **Khirbet er-Raʿi** near Lachish, dated to about 1100 BCE, was published as reading "Jerubbaal" (*yrbʿl*). It does not refer to the biblical Gideon, but it shows that the name was in use in the Iron I period.

## Points of Agreement

- Highland farmers vulnerable at harvest; hiding of grain in winepresses and caves fits Iron I villages.
- Gideon's clan, Abiezer, is a genuine Manassite kin group.
- Baal and Asherah worship among Israelites, and the Baal-bearing name Jerubbaal, match evidence for early Israelite religion.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 6 | Evidence |
|----------|----------|
| Camels "without number" | Large-scale camel use only from the late 10th century |
| Raids reach Gaza (6:4) | A sweeping range that looks like editorial generalization |
| Gideon's double name | Many scholars think two separate heroes (Gideon and Jerubbaal) were merged |
| A prophet's speech (6:7–10) | Deuteronomistic language, absent from a Qumran manuscript (4QJudg<sup>a</sup>) |

## Assessment

The core of the Gideon story probably preserves memory of a local Manassite leader who repelled raiders from the east. The equation of Gideon with Jerubbaal and the theological framing (the altar story explains the name; the prophet's speech repeats chapter 2) are secondary. The camels suggest that at least part of the story was told, or retold, after the 10th century. Notably, the Qumran fragment 4QJudg<sup>a</sup> lacks 6:7–10, which supports the view that this speech is a late insertion.
