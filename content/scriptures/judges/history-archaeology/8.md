## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The Transjordan campaign is set in real places, and the refusal of kingship and the ephod shrine reflect genuine features of pre-monarchic society. The large numbers and the pan-Israel kingship offer are editorial, and no extra-biblical evidence names any of the people involved.

## What the Text Claims

- Gideon calms Ephraim's anger (8:1–3).
- Pursuing the Midianite kings **Zebah and Zalmunna**, Gideon is refused supplies by **Succoth** and **Penuel**; he later punishes both, tearing down Penuel's tower (8:4–17).
- A young man of Succoth **writes down** the names of 77 officials and elders (8:14).
- Of 135,000 enemy, 120,000 have fallen (8:10).
- Israel offers Gideon hereditary rule; he refuses, but makes a golden **ephod** at Ophrah which becomes a snare (8:22–27).
- Gideon has 70 sons; after his death Israel worships **Baal-berith** (8:29–35).

## External Evidence

### Succoth and Penuel

Succoth is usually identified with **Tell Deir Alla** in the central Jordan Valley, excavated by H. J. Franken and later Dutch and Jordanian teams. The site had Late Bronze and Iron Age occupation and is famous for the **Balaam inscription**, an 8th-century plaster text. Penuel is often placed at Tulul edh-Dhahab on the Jabbok. Both identifications are widely accepted but not certain.

### Writing in Iron I

The young man's list implies basic literacy. Alphabetic writing in the period is attested by the **Izbet Sartah abecedary** (12th–11th century), the **Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon** (c. 1000 BCE), and inscribed arrowheads. Literacy was limited but not absent, so a written list is possible, though it may simply reflect later scribal assumptions.

### Ephods and local shrines

Ephods appear in early narratives as oracular cult objects (1 Sam 23:9; 30:7). Iron I villages and towns had local cult places, such as the Bull Site in northern Samaria. A family or clan shrine at Ophrah fits this picture.

## Points of Agreement

- Tension between Cisjordanian and Transjordanian towns, and local towers or fortified buildings, fit Iron I settlement patterns.
- Kin-based leadership and resistance to kingship (8:23) match a society that had not yet formed a state.
- Clan shrines with cult objects are archaeologically plausible.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 8 | Evidence |
|----------|----------|
| 135,000 Midianites, 120,000 slain | Far beyond the population of the region in Iron I |
| "Rule over us, you and your son" for all Israel | No unified Israel existed to offer a dynasty |
| 70 sons | A conventional number (cf. 9:2; 12:14; 2 Kgs 10:1) |
| Baal-berith ("Baal of the covenant") | Called El-berith in 9:46; the editors may have shifted El to Baal |

## Assessment

The Transjordan episode has the feel of an old local feud story: a clan leader avenging his brothers (8:18–19) and punishing towns that refused hospitality. The refusal of kingship is theologically charged, yet a reluctance toward centralized rule was real in early Israel. The theme also prepares for Abimelech's failed kingship in chapter 9. The ephod and Baal-berith reflect a later polemic against northern cult places.
