## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The episodes of this chapter are heroic folk tales with widely paralleled motifs. Their one historically significant feature is the picture of Judah submitting to Philistine overlordship, which agrees with the archaeological evidence for Philistine power in the 11th century BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Denied his wife, Samson ties torches to the tails of 300 foxes (or jackals) and burns the Philistines' grain, vineyards, and olive groves (15:1–5).
- The Philistines burn his wife and her father; Samson takes revenge and retreats to the rock of **Etam** (15:6–8).
- **3,000 men of Judah** hand Samson over, saying, "Do you not know that the Philistines are rulers over us?" (15:9–13).
- At **Lehi**, he kills 1,000 Philistines with a donkey's jawbone; God splits a hollow to give him water at **En-hakkore** (15:14–19).
- He judges Israel 20 years "in the days of the Philistines" (15:20).

## External Evidence

### Philistine power in the 11th century

The four Philistine cities that have been extensively excavated (Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron/Tel Miqne, and Gath/Tell es-Safi) were large, fortified urban centres in Iron I. Ekron grew to about 20 hectares, and Gath became the largest city in the region by the 10th–9th centuries. Philistine influence extended into the Shephelah (Timnah, Tel Batash). A Judahite population in the hills acknowledging Philistine authority is entirely plausible.

### The fox motif

The Roman poet **Ovid** (*Fasti* 4.681–712) describes a ritual at the festival of Ceres in which foxes with burning torches tied to them were released, and tells an aetiological tale of a fox that set grain fields on fire. Scholars have long compared this with Samson's foxes. The parallel suggests a widespread folk motif linked to the grain harvest.

### Place names

Lehi ("jawbone") and Ramath-lehi ("height of the jawbone") are unidentified. En-hakkore ("spring of the one who called") is explained by the story itself. These are classic **aetiologies**: stories that explain how a place got its name. The rock of Etam is also uncertain; a town called Etam near Bethlehem is mentioned in 2 Chronicles 11:6.

### The Judahite connection

This is the only Samson episode involving Judah. The figure of 3,000 men and their submissive speech may reflect a Judahite editor's interest, or a realistic memory that Judah was not part of an anti-Philistine coalition in this period.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistines dominated the Shephelah and exerted authority over highland Judah.
- The economy described (grain, vineyards, olives) matches Iron I agriculture in the region.
- Wheat harvest as a time of vulnerability fits the agricultural calendar (cf. the Gezer Calendar's list of seasonal tasks).

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 15 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| 300 foxes caught and paired | Folk exaggeration; jackals were common, but this is a tall tale |
| 1,000 killed with a jawbone | Heroic hyperbole |
| Place names explained by the story | Typical aetiological narrative |
| Samson "judged Israel" 20 years | Editorial framing of a lone border fighter |

## Assessment

Chapter 15 is a cluster of aetiological folk tales tied to places in the Shephelah and the hill country. Samson is not a leader of armies but a solitary avenger, which is precisely why the editors' note that he "judged Israel" sits so oddly. The historical kernel is the political situation the stories assume: Philistine supremacy, Judahite acquiescence, and no organized Israelite resistance before Saul.
