## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The Othniel episode is a skeletal model narrative with an unidentifiable foe. The Ehud story is vivid and locally rooted, but it presupposes a Moabite kingdom that is not otherwise attested this early. Shamgar's name has an authentic early ring.

## What the Text Claims

- Philistines, Canaanites, Sidonians, and Hivites are left to test Israel (3:1–6).
- Israel serves **Cushan-rishathaim king of Aram-naharaim** for eight years; Othniel the Kenizzite delivers them, and the land rests 40 years (3:7–11).
- **Eglon king of Moab**, with Ammon and Amalek, takes "the city of palms" and rules Israel 18 years (3:12–14).
- The left-handed Benjaminite **Ehud** assassinates Eglon in his "cool upper room" and leads Israel to kill 10,000 Moabites at the Jordan fords; the land rests 80 years (3:15–30).
- **Shamgar son of Anath** kills 600 Philistines with an oxgoad (3:31).

## External Evidence

### Cushan-rishathaim

No king of this name is known from Mesopotamian or Syrian records. "Aram-naharaim" (north Mesopotamia) is an unlikely base for a ruler oppressing southern Judah, where Othniel's clan lived. "Rishathaim" means "double wickedness" and is almost certainly a mocking epithet. Proposals include:

- **Abraham Malamat (1954)** linked him with a Syrian usurper named Irsu, mentioned in the Great Harris Papyrus as seizing power in Egypt around 1200 BCE. The link is speculative.
- **An Edom/Aram confusion:** in Hebrew script, *ʾrm* (Aram) and *ʾdm* (Edom) differ by one similar letter. "Cushan" appears in Habakkuk 3:7 alongside Midian, in the south. An Edomite or southern enemy would better fit a Kenizzite deliverer.

### Moab and Eglon

Egyptian sources of Ramesses II mention Moab as a region. But the first evidence of a Moabite kingdom with kings is the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE). Iron I settlement on the Moabite plateau was sparse and village-based. No King Eglon is attested.

### "The city of palms"

This usually means Jericho (Deut 34:3). Kathleen Kenyon's excavations at Tell es-Sultan found very little occupation in Iron I. A Moabite king holding Jericho is therefore difficult archaeologically.

### Shamgar son of Anath

Shamgar is probably a non-Semitic (perhaps Hurrian) name. "Son of Anath" refers to the warrior goddess Anat. Inscribed bronze arrowheads from El-Khadr near Bethlehem, dated to the 12th–11th centuries BCE, bear personal names including a "son of ʿAnat" formula. Many scholars think this was a military title or a family identity for warriors, so the name fits the period.

## Points of Agreement

- Shamgar's name and the "son of Anath" formula match early Iron Age onomastics.
- Left-handed Benjaminite warriors (also 20:16) may reflect a genuine regional military tradition.
- Conflict between highland Benjamin and Transjordanian groups over the Jordan fords is geographically realistic.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 3 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| A Mesopotamian king oppresses Israel | No such king is known; geography is implausible |
| Moab is a kingdom with a king in Iron I | Moabite kingship is first attested in the 9th century |
| Eglon holds Jericho | Jericho was virtually unoccupied in Iron I |
| 40 and 80 years of rest | Schematic numbers typical of the editorial chronology |

## Assessment

The Othniel paragraph reads as a model case created by the Deuteronomistic editor to show the cycle in its simplest form, perhaps giving Judah a judge of its own. The Ehud story is a classic folk tale of a trickster hero, rich in local detail and humour. Baruch Halpern has argued that its architectural details reflect real knowledge of palace design. Even so, its Moabite kingdom probably reflects later conditions. Shamgar is a fragment of tradition that the editors attached here, perhaps borrowed from the Song of Deborah (5:6).
