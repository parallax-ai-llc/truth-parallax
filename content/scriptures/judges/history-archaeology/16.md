## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Samson's last exploits are legendary. But two background details have archaeological interest. Philistine temples with central pillars have been excavated, and the "lords of the Philistines" fit what is known of the Philistine city-state system.

## What the Text Claims

- At **Gaza**, Samson carries off the city gate and its posts to a hill facing **Hebron** (16:1–3).
- **Delilah** of the Sorek Valley is offered 1,100 pieces of silver by each of the **lords of the Philistines** to discover the source of his strength (16:4–20).
- Shaved and blinded, Samson grinds grain in a Gaza prison (16:21–22).
- At a festival of **Dagon**, he pushes apart the two central pillars of the temple, killing himself and about 3,000 people on the roof (16:23–30).
- He is buried between Zorah and Eshtaol (16:31).

## External Evidence

### Philistine temples

At **Tell Qasile** in modern Tel Aviv, Amihai Mazar excavated a sequence of Philistine temples (Strata XII–X, 12th–11th centuries BCE). The Stratum X temple had **two wooden pillars on stone bases** about 2 metres apart that supported the roof. Many scholars point to this as a striking parallel to 16:29. A pillared temple at Ekron (Tel Miqne) also shows the type. This does not verify the story, but it shows that the narrator knew how such buildings were constructed.

### Dagon

Dagon (Dagan) was not originally a Philistine god. He was an ancient Syrian and Mesopotamian deity of grain or storms, worshipped at **Ebla**, **Mari**, and **Ugarit** (where he is Baal's father). The Bible associates him with Gaza (here) and Ashdod (1 Sam 5). The Philistines evidently adopted local Semitic gods after settling in Canaan, which fits the archaeological evidence for gradual cultural integration. The later idea that Dagon was a fish-god rests on a folk etymology (*dag*, "fish") and is not supported by ancient evidence.

### The "lords of the Philistines"

The Hebrew term *seranim*, used only for Philistine rulers, may be related to Greek *tyrannos* or a Luwian word. The five lords correspond to the Pentapolis of Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron, and Gath. Later, the 7th-century **Ekron royal inscription** names a king Achish (*Ikausu*) and his father Padi. By then Philistine rulers used the title "king."

### Gaza

Iron Age Gaza lies under the modern city and has seen only limited excavation. Carrying its gates some 60 km uphill to Hebron is a heroic exaggeration.

## Points of Agreement

- Philistine cities were ruled by a council of five lords.
- Dagon was worshipped in Philistine cities; a temple of Dagon at Ashdod is still mentioned in the 2nd century BCE (1 Macc 10:83–84).
- Temples supported by central pillars existed in Philistia.
- Grinding grain was a typical task imposed on prisoners and slaves in the ancient Near East.

## Points of Conflict

| Judges 16 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Gaza's gates carried to Hebron | About 60 km uphill; legendary strength |
| 3,000 people on the temple roof | No excavated Philistine temple could hold such numbers |
| 1,100 silver pieces from each lord | Literary sum; the same figure as Micah's silver in 17:2 |
| Hair as the source of strength | A folk motif with parallels in many cultures |

## Assessment

The Samson cycle ends with a heroic death story whose motifs (betrayal by a woman, hair as strength, a final act of vengeance) are found in folklore worldwide. The narrator's knowledge of Philistine institutions and temple architecture is real and suggests the stories were shaped by people who knew Philistine cities, probably in the Iron Age. The story is not history, but it accurately reflects the setting of conflict between Philistines and highlanders.
