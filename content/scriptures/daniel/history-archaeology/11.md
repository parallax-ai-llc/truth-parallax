## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High for 11:2–39, then wrong for 11:40–45.** The chapter is an extraordinarily accurate survey of Persian and Hellenistic history down to c. 167 BCE, written as prophecy. Its final prediction about the death of Antiochus IV did not come true. This pattern is the decisive evidence that the book was completed between 167 and 164 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

The angel reveals the future from Persia to "the time of the end." Most of the chapter describes the wars between the **"king of the south"** (the Ptolemies of Egypt) and the **"king of the north"** (the Seleucids of Syria), then the career of a "contemptible person" who profanes the Temple and finally dies "between the sea and the glorious holy mountain."

## External Evidence: Verse by Verse

The main sources are Polybius, Livy, Diodorus Siculus, Appian's *Syrian Wars*, Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel* (which preserves Porphyry's historical identifications), 1–2 Maccabees, and Hellenistic inscriptions.

### The failed prediction (11:40–45)

The chapter then predicts that "at the time of the end" the king of the south will attack, and the king of the north will sweep through, conquer Egypt, Libya, and Cush, and then hear alarming reports from the east and north. He will pitch his tents "between the sea and the glorious holy mountain" and "come to his end, with none to help him."

None of this happened. Antiochus IV made **no third invasion of Egypt**. In 165 BCE he went east to campaign in Persia and Elymais, and he died at **Tabae in Persia** in late 164 BCE (Polybius 31.9; 1 Macc 6:1–16; 2 Macc 9). A Babylonian king list confirms the news of his death in Kislev 148 of the Seleucid era (late 164 BCE).

## Points of Agreement

For 11:2–39 the agreement with external sources is remarkable, more precise than any other prophetic text in the Hebrew Bible. Even small details, such as the failed marriage alliances, the "exactor," the humiliation by Rome, and the two kings lying at one table, match the historians.

## Points of Conflict

- **11:2** counts only four Persian kings after Cyrus and passes straight from Xerxes to Alexander, skipping about 150 years.
- **11:40–45** fails on the king's last campaign and on the place of his death.

## Assessment

This is the standard ancient form of *vaticinium ex eventu*: a survey of past history written as future prophecy, ending with a real prediction. Babylonian parallels include the so-called Akkadian prophecies, such as the *Dynastic Prophecy*, which describes past reigns in prophetic form. Daniel's author knew 3rd- and 2nd-century history in detail, knew 6th- to 4th-century Persian history only vaguely, and did not know how Antiochus would die. That places the writing of the chapter around 166–165 BCE.

**Conservative response.** Defenders of a 6th-century date see the precision as genuine prophecy. Since Jerome, many have argued that 11:36–45 shifts from Antiochus to a future **Antichrist**, so that the "failed" verses remain unfulfilled. Critical scholars note that the text gives no sign of such a jump in time: the same "king of the north" continues from 11:21 through 11:45. Porphyry made this argument in the 3rd century, and Jerome preserved it in order to refute it.
