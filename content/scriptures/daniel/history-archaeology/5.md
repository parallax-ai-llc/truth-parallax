## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The chapter preserves one striking historical memory: Belshazzar existed and held power in Babylon at the end of the Neo-Babylonian empire. However, it is wrong about his father, his title, and how the city fell, and its "Darius the Mede" has no historical counterpart.

## What the Text Claims

- Belshazzar is **king** of Babylon (5:1) and the **son of Nebuchadnezzar** (5:2, 11, 13, 18, 22).
- During a banquet using the vessels taken from the Jerusalem Temple, a hand writes on the wall; Daniel interprets the words (5:5–28).
- Daniel is made "third ruler in the kingdom" (5:29).
- "That very night" Belshazzar is killed, and **Darius the Mede** receives the kingdom at the age of 62 (5:30–31).

## External Evidence

### Cuneiform sources on Belshazzar

- **Nabonidus Cylinders from Ur** (found 1854): Nabonidus prays for "Bēl-šarra-uṣur, my firstborn son." This established that Belshazzar was real and that he was Nabonidus's son.
- **Verse Account of Nabonidus:** Nabonidus "entrusted the camp to his eldest son" and "entrusted the kingship to him" before leaving for Tayma in Arabia.
- **Nabonidus Chronicle (ABC 7):** For several years it notes that "the king was in Tayma; the crown prince, his officers and his army were in Akkad," and that the New Year festival was not celebrated.
- **Economic and legal texts** record oaths sworn by the king and the crown prince together, but they are dated only by **Nabonidus's** regnal years. None calls Belshazzar *šarru* (king).

### The fall of Babylon (539 BCE)

- **Nabonidus Chronicle:** On 16 Tashritu (12 October 539 BCE), "Ugbaru, governor of Gutium, and the army of Cyrus entered Babylon without battle." Cyrus himself entered on 3 Arahsamnu. Nabonidus was captured. The Chronicle does not mention Belshazzar's death.
- **Cyrus Cylinder:** Cyrus claims Marduk let him enter Babylon "without fighting or battle" and that the population welcomed him.
- **Herodotus (1.191)** and **Xenophon (*Cyropaedia* 7.5)** tell of Babylon being taken during a festival, with the Persians diverting the Euphrates. Xenophon also has an unnamed "impious king" killed. These Greek accounts are later and partly legendary, but they share the festival motif with Daniel 5.

## Points of Agreement

- **Belshazzar's existence and prominence.** Before 1854 no source outside the Bible and texts dependent on it named Belshazzar, and critics doubted him. The cuneiform record proved the book right that he was the leading figure in Babylon in Nabonidus's absence.
- **"Third ruler in the kingdom"** (5:7, 16, 29) makes good sense if Nabonidus was first and Belshazzar second. Many scholars, critical and conservative alike, accept that this detail reflects a real memory of the co-regency.
- **A feast on the night of the fall** fits the festival tradition in Herodotus and Xenophon.

## Points of Conflict

| Daniel 5 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| Belshazzar is "king" | Never titled king. He was crown prince and regent, and documents are dated by Nabonidus |
| Son of Nebuchadnezzar (stated five times) | Son of Nabonidus, a usurper from outside Nebuchadnezzar's family |
| The kingdom passes to "Darius the Mede" | Babylon fell to the Persian Cyrus II through his general Ugbaru, and no Median king ruled |
| Belshazzar dies that night | Not recorded. His fate is unknown, though the Chronicle notes a death (probably Ugbaru's) weeks later |

Conservative scholars suggest that "father" means "royal predecessor," or that Nabonidus's mother or wife came from Nebuchadnezzar's family. These readings are possible in Aramaic idiom, but no evidence supports a family link. Also, the queen's speech in 5:11 ("your father the king, Nebuchadnezzar your father") reads naturally as a claim of literal descent.

## Assessment

Daniel 5 is a good example of how the book uses history. A Jewish author of the Hellenistic period kept accurate fragments, such as the name Belshazzar, his practical rule, and a festival on the night Babylon fell. These were set inside a theological story about God judging a blasphemous king. The literary aim, the fall of the empire that destroyed the Temple, took priority over accurate dynastic detail. For 2nd-century readers under Antiochus IV, who had also profaned the Temple, the message about the vessels was clear.
