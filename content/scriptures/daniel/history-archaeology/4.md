## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No Babylonian source mentions a seven-year madness of Nebuchadnezzar, whose reign is well documented. A Dead Sea Scroll shows that a similar story was told about his later successor **Nabonidus**, and the episode is best explained as a Nabonidus tradition transferred to the more famous king.

## What the Text Claims

- The chapter is framed as a **royal proclamation** by Nebuchadnezzar to all peoples (4:1–3, 34–37).
- The king dreams of a great tree that is cut down, leaving a stump bound with iron and bronze. Daniel interprets it as the king himself (4:4–27).
- Twelve months later, as Nebuchadnezzar boasts on his palace roof of "great Babylon, which I have built," he is driven from human society, eats grass like an ox, and lives in the open for **"seven times"** (4:28–33).
- His reason returns, he praises the Most High, and he is restored to his kingdom with greater majesty (4:34–36).

## External Evidence

### Nebuchadnezzar's own inscriptions

Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562 BCE) left many building inscriptions, such as the East India House inscription, now in the British Museum. They boast at length about his work on Babylon's walls, palaces, the Ishtar Gate, and the temples of Marduk and Nabu. Excavations by Robert Koldewey (1899–1917) confirmed this building program. The boast in 4:30 matches the king's own self-presentation very well.

### No gap in his reign

Economic and legal documents are dated by Nebuchadnezzar's regnal years across his 43-year reign. The Babylonian Chronicle breaks off after 594 BCE, so there is no continuous narrative for his later years, but no source suggests he was removed from power for seven years. Conservative scholars point to a fragmentary cuneiform text (BM 34113), published by A. K. Grayson in 1975, which some have read as describing erratic behavior by Nebuchadnezzar. The tablet is badly broken, and its reference to a mental illness is not generally accepted.

### The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242)

This Aramaic fragment from Qumran Cave 4, first published by J. T. Milik in 1956, is introduced as the prayer of **Nabonidus, king of Babylon**, when he was struck with a severe skin disease "by decree of God" in **Teman** (Tayma) for **seven years**. A Jewish exile, described as a diviner (*gzr*), forgave his sin and told him to honor the God of Israel. The parallels with Daniel 4 are striking: a Babylonian king, a seven-year affliction, a Jewish wise man, and a royal confession written in the first person.

### Nabonidus in Arabia

Nabonidus (556–539 BCE) really did spend about ten years at the oasis of **Tayma** in Arabia. His own **Harran stelae** say he wandered there for ten years. The **Nabonidus Chronicle** repeatedly records that "the king was in Tayma." Babylonian sources hostile to him, such as the *Verse Account of Nabonidus*, present his absence and his devotion to the moon god Sin as scandalous. Nabonidus's long, strange absence from his capital is a far better historical seed for the legend than anything in Nebuchadnezzar's reign.

### Abydenus

The Greek writer Abydenus, quoted by Eusebius (*Preparation for the Gospel* 9.41), says that Nebuchadnezzar went up onto his palace roof, was seized by a god, prophesied the coming of a Persian conqueror, and then vanished. This is a late legend, but it shows that stories of Nebuchadnezzar's strange end on the palace roof were in circulation.

## Points of Agreement

- **Nebuchadnezzar as a great builder** of Babylon (4:30) is fully confirmed by inscriptions and excavation.
- **A Babylonian king absent from his capital for years** is historical, but it was Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar.
- **First-person royal proclamations** are a real ancient genre.

## Points of Conflict

| Daniel 4 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| Nebuchadnezzar loses his reason and rule for "seven times" | No evidence. His reign is continuously dated |
| The king praises the God of Israel | His inscriptions are devoted to Marduk and Nabu |
| The story is about Nebuchadnezzar | The Qumran parallel tells it about Nabonidus, whose absence at Tayma is documented |

The Greek (Old Greek) version of chapter 4 differs substantially from the Aramaic, which shows that the story circulated in more than one form.

## Assessment

Most scholars conclude that Daniel 4 grew from a Jewish tradition about Nabonidus, preserved in a different form in 4Q242, and that it was later attached to Nebuchadnezzar, the king who destroyed Jerusalem and therefore the natural figure for a story of a proud ruler humbled. The building boast is authentic in tone, but the madness, the restoration, and the confession are legend. The tale's theme, that God gives kingdoms "to whom he will" (4:25), would have spoken directly to readers under Antiochus IV, whom Polybius said was nicknamed *Epimanes*, "the madman."
