## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy), with a chronological error.** The "seventy weeks" reinterpret Jeremiah's prophecy so that it ends in the Maccabean crisis. Its final "week" fits the events of c. 171–164 BCE well, but its overall span overestimates the length of the Persian period by several decades, a mistake shared by other ancient Jewish chronographers.

## Dating and Setting

The chapter is dated to "the first year of **Darius son of Ahasuerus**, by descent a Mede" (9:1). As the overview explains, this figure is unhistorical, and Ahasuerus (Xerxes I) was the son, not the father, of the Persian Darius I. The setting is literary. The content points to c. 167–164 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Daniel studies "the books" and finds Jeremiah's prophecy of **seventy years** of desolation for Jerusalem (9:2; cf. Jer 25:11–12; 29:10).
- He offers a long prayer of national confession (9:4–19).
- Gabriel explains that **seventy weeks** (of years, that is, 490 years) are decreed (9:24). From "the going forth of the word to restore and rebuild Jerusalem" to "an anointed one, a prince" will be **seven weeks**, then **sixty-two weeks**. After that "an anointed one shall be cut off," a prince's people will destroy the city and sanctuary, and for **half of the final week** sacrifice will cease and an "abomination that desolates" will stand (9:25–27).

## External Evidence and Historical Referents

### Reading Jeremiah as scripture

Daniel consults prophetic "books" as an authoritative collection, and he reinterprets a past prophecy for a new situation. This method is characteristic of Second Temple interpretation, seen also in the Qumran *pesharim*. Jeremiah's seventy years had already been read in different ways in 2 Chronicles 36:21 and Zechariah 1:12.

### The prayer

The prayer (9:4–19) is in fluent Hebrew and has a Deuteronomistic style. It closely resembles other Second Temple penitential prayers: Ezra 9, Nehemiah 1 and 9, and especially **Baruch 1:15–2:19**, which shares long stretches of wording with it. Many scholars think the author used an existing liturgical prayer.

### The final week (c. 171–164 BCE)

In the critical reading:

- **"An anointed one shall be cut off"** (9:26) is the high priest **Onias III**, deposed by Antiochus IV and murdered near Antioch around 171 BCE (2 Macc 4:33–35).
- **"He shall make a strong covenant with many for one week"** (9:27) refers to Antiochus's alliance with Hellenizing Jews (1 Macc 1:11–15).
- **"For half of the week he shall make sacrifice and offering cease"** matches the ban on Temple sacrifice from 167 to 164 BCE (1 Macc 1:45, 54).
- **The destruction of the city** recalls the attack on Jerusalem by Antiochus's official Apollonius in 167 BCE (1 Macc 1:29–32).

### The first seven weeks (49 years)

Counting from Jeremiah's oracles around 587 BCE, 49 years leads to about 538 BCE, the time of **Cyrus** (whom Isaiah 45:1 calls "anointed") and of the return under the high priest **Joshua** and governor **Zerubbabel**. Each is a candidate for the first "anointed one, a prince."

### The sixty-two weeks: a chronological gap

From about 538 to 171 BCE is about 367 years, but 62 weeks equal **434 years**. The excess of over sixty years is the chronology's main problem. Ancient Jewish writers did not know the length of the Persian period accurately. The rabbinic chronicle **Seder Olam Rabbah** compresses Persian rule after the Temple's rebuilding to 34 years. Josephus's figures also vary. The author of Daniel seems to have worked with a schematic, inaccurate chronology of the period between the exile and his own day.

## Traditional Readings

Christian interpretation has usually ended the seventy weeks with **Jesus**. One common count starts from Artaxerxes I's decree in his 20th year (445/444 BCE, Neh 2) and uses "prophetic years" of 360 days to reach about 32–33 CE (Sir Robert Anderson, 1881; Harold Hoehner, 1977). Another starts from Artaxerxes's 7th year (458/457 BCE, Ezra 7) and reaches about 27 CE. Josephus (*Antiquities* 10.276) also read Daniel as foretelling Roman destruction of Jerusalem.

Critical scholars respond that these counts need decrees that do not concern rebuilding the city, or 360-day years that Daniel never mentions, and that they separate 9:27 from the parallel descriptions of the "abomination" in 8:11–13, 11:31, and 12:11, which all refer to 167 BCE.

## Assessment

Daniel 9 is a reinterpretation of Jeremiah, written to assure readers that the long desolation was nearly over. It is accurate in its picture of the final crisis and the murder of Onias III, but wrong about the length of the Persian period. That kind of error is natural for a 2nd-century writer with incomplete records and hard to explain for a 6th-century prophet.
