## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No source records a golden statue on the plain of Dura or a forced act of worship under Nebuchadnezzar. The chapter's vocabulary includes Persian titles and Greek instrument names that point to a later author, and its theme of compulsory idolatry reflects the persecution of Antiochus IV more than Babylonian policy.

## What the Text Claims

- Nebuchadnezzar sets up a golden image **60 cubits high and 6 cubits wide** on "the plain of Dura" (3:1).
- He summons satraps, prefects, governors, and other officials to its dedication (3:2–3).
- At the sound of an orchestra, including horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, and bagpipe or "symphony," all must worship the image or be thrown into a **burning furnace** (3:4–6).
- Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse, are thrown in, and are unharmed. A fourth figure "like a son of the gods" appears with them (3:8–25).
- The king praises their God and forbids blasphemy against him on pain of death (3:28–30).

## External Evidence

### Nebuchadnezzar's religious policy

Nebuchadnezzar's many building inscriptions describe temple restorations for **Marduk, Nabu**, and other Babylonian gods. They give no hint of a state cult image that subjects had to worship, and Babylonian rule did not require conquered peoples to give up their own gods. The Babylonian Chronicle for his reign breaks off after 594 BCE, so there are gaps, but nothing in the surviving record resembles the event in Daniel 3.

### Dura

"Dura" reflects Akkadian *dūru*, "wall" or "fortified place," which appears in several Mesopotamian place names. No specific site has been securely identified, and no remains of a monumental statue are known.

### Burning as a punishment

Execution by fire is attested in Mesopotamia. The **Laws of Hammurabi** (c. 1750 BCE) prescribe burning for certain crimes (§§25, 110, 157). Within the Bible, **Jeremiah 29:22** says Nebuchadnezzar "roasted in the fire" two Judean prophets, Ahab and Zedekiah. This detail of the story is therefore plausible.

### Vocabulary

- **Persian titles.** Several officials in 3:2–3 bear Old Persian titles, such as *ʾăḥašdarpĕnayyāʾ* ("satraps," from Old Persian *khshathrapāvan*). Persian administrative terms in a Neo-Babylonian court scene are anachronistic, although they would be natural for a writer living after 539 BCE.
- **Greek instrument names.** Three instruments have Greek names: *qayterōs* (*kitharis*), *pĕsantērîn* (*psaltērion*), and *sûmpōnyâ* (*symphōnia*). The first two could have travelled east early. But *symphōnia* is not attested as the name of an instrument before the 2nd century BCE; Polybius (26.1) uses the word for music played at the revels of **Antiochus IV**. T. C. Mitchell and R. Joyce (1965), writing from a conservative perspective, argued that the names prove nothing about date. Most critical scholars find the cluster telling.

### Textual tradition

The Masoretic text gives no date. The Old Greek and Theodotion versions date the event to Nebuchadnezzar's **eighteenth year**, the year Jerusalem fell (587/586 BCE). The Greek versions also insert the *Prayer of Azariah* and the *Song of the Three Young Men* (68 additional verses, canonical in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles). This shows the story was still growing in the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE.

## Points of Agreement

- **Burning as a royal punishment** is attested in Mesopotamian law and in Jeremiah 29:22.
- **Large cult statues covered in gold** were part of Mesopotamian religion, although they stood in temples.
- **Lists of officials** at royal ceremonies are a known feature of ancient administrative and court literature.

## Points of Conflict

| Daniel 3 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| Compulsory worship of a state image | No such policy is known for Nebuchadnezzar or any Neo-Babylonian king |
| Officials with Persian titles | Persian administration began only after 539 BCE |
| Instruments with Greek names, including *symphōnia* | *Symphōnia* as an instrument is attested only from the 2nd century BCE |
| A 60 × 6 cubit statue (about 27 × 2.7 m) | No evidence. The 10:1 proportion would be odd for a human figure, though possible for a statue on a high base or an obelisk-like pillar |
| King decrees protection for the Jewish God (3:29) | No trace in Babylonian sources |

## Assessment

Daniel 3 does not include Daniel himself, which suggests it was once an independent tale about three Jewish courtiers. Its real subject, a king who demands that all peoples worship a single image on pain of death, closely matches the decree of **Antiochus IV** that "all should be one people" and abandon their customs (1 Macc 1:41–50), and his installation of the "abomination of desolation" in 167 BCE. Whether the story was composed then or adapted then from an older Diaspora legend, it is best read as a martyr story for readers facing forced idolatry, not as a record of an event in Babylon.
