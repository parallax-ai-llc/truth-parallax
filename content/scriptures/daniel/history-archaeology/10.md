## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (vision).** The chapter is the introduction to the long revelation of chapters 10–12. It makes almost no checkable historical claims. Its setting is a literary frame, and its developed ideas about angels point to the Hellenistic period.

## Dating and Setting

The vision is dated to "the **third year of Cyrus** king of Persia" (10:1), about 536 BCE, and set on the bank of the **Tigris** (10:4). This creates a small internal tension with 1:21, which says Daniel remained "until the first year of King Cyrus." The date also implies that Daniel did not join the return to Judah that Cyrus allowed (Ezra 1). Like the other dates in the book, this one serves the narrative: it places the final revelation at the start of the Persian era, from which chapter 11 will trace history down to Antiochus IV.

## What the Text Claims

- Daniel mourns and fasts for **three weeks** in the first month (10:2–4).
- A man "clothed in linen," with a belt of gold and a body like beryl, appears to him (10:5–9).
- The angel explains that the **"prince of the kingdom of Persia"** resisted him for 21 days until **Michael**, "one of the chief princes," came to help (10:13).
- He must return to fight the prince of Persia, and "the prince of Greece will come" (10:20). Michael is "your prince," Israel's patron (10:21).

## Historical Setting the Vision Presupposes

### Angels of the nations

The idea that each nation has a heavenly patron goes back to an older tradition in which the Most High divided the nations "according to the number of the sons of God." This reading appears in a Dead Sea Scrolls manuscript of Deuteronomy (4QDeut<sup>j</sup>) and in the Greek Septuagint of **Deuteronomy 32:8**, where the Masoretic text has "sons of Israel." Daniel turns these divine beings into rival "princes" whose battles in heaven mirror wars on earth.

### Named angels

Daniel is the first book of the Hebrew Bible to name angels (**Gabriel** in 8:16 and 9:21, and **Michael** here). The same development appears in the **Book of the Watchers** (1 Enoch 1–36, generally dated to the 3rd century BCE) and later in the Dead Sea Scrolls, such as the *War Scroll*. A hierarchy of named angels with assigned roles is characteristic of Jewish literature in the Hellenistic period, not of the 6th century BCE.

### Persia, then Greece

The "prince of Persia" giving way to the "prince of Greece" (10:20) follows the real sequence of empires that ruled Judea: Achaemenid Persia (539–332 BCE), then Alexander and his successors. This repeats the scheme of chapters 2, 7, and 8, and prepares for the detailed survey in chapter 11.

### Literary models

The description of the heavenly figure borrows from **Ezekiel** (the man clothed in linen in Ezek 9:2, and the throne vision in Ezek 1). Daniel's response, falling to the ground and being raised and strengthened, is a convention of vision reports. The fast during the first month, which includes Passover, reflects Jewish practice, but it has no independent historical attestation.

## Assessment

Chapter 10 belongs to the latest layer of the book. Its date under Cyrus is a frame, and nothing in it is confirmed or contradicted by external sources. Its theology, in which angelic princes fight for nations and Michael defends Israel, fits the apocalyptic thought of the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE and the conviction during the Maccabean crisis that the earthly struggle against the Seleucids was part of a larger heavenly war.
