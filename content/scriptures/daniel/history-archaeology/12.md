## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (prophecy).** The chapter predicts the end of history soon after the persecution of Antiochus IV. That end did not arrive. Its revised time figures and its new belief in resurrection are valuable evidence for Jewish thought in the 160s BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- At that time **Michael** will arise, and there will be a time of unprecedented distress, but the people "written in the book" will be delivered (12:1).
- "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." The wise (*maskilim*) will shine like stars (12:2–3).
- Daniel is told to **seal the book** "until the time of the end" (12:4, 9).
- The end will come after "a time, times, and half a time" (12:7), then after **1,290 days** (12:11), and those who wait for **1,335 days** are blessed (12:12).

## Dating and Historical Setting

### The calculations

Chapter 12 offers three successive figures for the same event:

- **"A time, times, and half a time"**, about three and a half years (12:7; also 7:25).
- **1,290 days**, counted from the abolition of the daily offering and the setting up of the "abomination" (12:11). That event is dated to Kislev 167 BCE (1 Macc 1:54).
- **1,335 days**, 45 days longer (12:12).

Together with the 1,150 days implied in 8:14, the figures grow longer step by step. Many scholars, following H. L. Ginsberg and others, think each new number was added when the previous date passed without the expected end. The figures all point to dates in 164–163 BCE. They show a community counting down to an imminent end, not a prophecy aimed at the distant future.

### Sealing the book

The command to seal the book until "the time of the end" explains why a book attributed to a 6th-century sage was unknown until the 2nd century. It is a standard device of **pseudepigraphic apocalypses**, also found in 1 Enoch.

### Resurrection

Daniel 12:2 is the clearest statement of bodily resurrection in the Hebrew Bible. Earlier texts, such as Ezekiel 37 and Isaiah 26:19, use resurrection imagery for national restoration. Daniel speaks of individuals being raised for reward or punishment, and only "many," not all. This fits the crisis of martyrdom under Antiochus IV. The same hope appears in **2 Maccabees 7**, where martyred brothers expect God to raise them, and in the Enochic literature (e.g., 1 Enoch 22; 104). Belief in individual resurrection is not attested in Jewish texts from the 6th century BCE, so this is further evidence of a late date.

### The wise

The *maskilim* (12:3, 10; cf. 11:33–35) appear to be the author's own circle, teachers who accepted martyrdom rather than armed revolt. The phrase "lead many to righteousness" echoes Isaiah 53:11.

## Assessment

Daniel 12 records an expectation that failed historically. The persecution ended, the Temple was rededicated in December 164 BCE, and Antiochus died, but the resurrection and the end of history did not follow. The chapter's real historical importance lies elsewhere: it is a precisely dated witness to the birth of Jewish belief in individual resurrection, a belief that would shape Pharisaic Judaism and early Christianity.

The Greek versions add the stories of *Susanna* and *Bel and the Dragon* after this chapter (Daniel 13–14 in Catholic Bibles). They are generally regarded as further legends that circulated in the Daniel tradition, not as historical records.
