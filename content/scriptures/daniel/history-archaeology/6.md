## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The story is set under a king, "Darius the Mede," who is unknown to history. Its administrative details do not match Persian practice, and a royal ban on prayer to any god but the king does not fit Achaemenid religion. The tale is best read as a legend of faithful martyrdom with a happy ending.

## What the Text Claims

- **Darius the Mede** appoints **120 satraps** over the kingdom, with three presidents above them, one of whom is Daniel (6:1–3).
- Jealous officials persuade Darius to sign a decree that for **30 days** no one may pray to any god or man except the king. As a "law of the Medes and Persians," it cannot be revoked (6:6–9).
- Daniel continues praying three times a day toward Jerusalem, is thrown into a den of lions, and survives. His accusers and their families are thrown in and devoured (6:10–24).
- Darius issues a decree honoring Daniel's God, and Daniel prospers "in the reign of Darius and the reign of Cyrus the Persian" (6:25–28).

## External Evidence

### Who ruled Babylon after 539 BCE

The **Nabonidus Chronicle** and the **Cyrus Cylinder** show that Babylon passed directly to **Cyrus II** of Persia. Cyrus's son Cambyses held the title "king of Babylon" briefly in 538. Economic texts from the following years are dated by Cyrus's regnal years. There is no gap in which a Median king could have reigned.

Several identifications of Darius the Mede have been proposed by conservative scholars:

- **Gubaru**, governor of Babylon and Beyond the River, who is attested in economic texts from about 535 to 525 BCE (J. C. Whitcomb, 1959). He is a different person from **Ugbaru**, the general who took Babylon and who, according to the Chronicle, died within weeks. Gubaru was never called king.
- **Cyrus himself**, reading 6:28 as "in the reign of Darius, that is, the reign of Cyrus the Persian" (D. J. Wiseman, 1965). This is grammatically possible, but Cyrus was Persian, not a Mede, and was not the son of Ahasuerus (9:1).

Neither proposal has won acceptance among historians.

### Persian provinces

The **Behistun inscription** of Darius I (c. 520 BCE) lists 23 lands under his rule. **Herodotus** (3.89) says Darius I organized the empire into 20 satrapies. The figure of 120 satraps is not supported. It is closer to the 127 provinces of Esther 1:1 and 1 Esdras 3:2, both later Jewish literary works. The man who actually organized the satrapies was the Persian **Darius I** (522–486 BCE), which suggests the author confused him with his fictional Median Darius.

### The irrevocable law

The idea that a Persian law could not be revoked appears only in Daniel 6 and Esther (1:19; 8:8). No Persian source supports it. The Greek historian Diodorus Siculus (17.30) tells a story of Darius III regretting a death sentence he could not undo, but that concerns a completed execution, not a legal principle.

### Royal worship

The Achaemenid kings presented themselves as chosen by **Ahuramazda**, not as gods. Prostration before the king (*proskynesis*) was court etiquette, not worship. A decree making the king the only legitimate object of prayer fits a **Hellenistic ruler cult** better. Antiochus IV issued coins with the title *Theos Epiphanes* ("God Manifest").

### Lions

Assyrian palace reliefs, such as Ashurbanipal's lion-hunt reliefs from Nineveh (c. 645 BCE, British Museum), show captive lions released from cages for royal hunts. Kings did keep lions. However, there is no evidence of lion pits used for executions.

## Points of Agreement

- **Persian satraps and senior officials** were real, and the Aramaic titles used here are authentic Persian loanwords.
- **Captive lions at royal courts** are attested.
- **Prayer toward Jerusalem** reflects real Jewish practice in the Diaspora (cf. 1 Kings 8:48).

## Points of Conflict

| Daniel 6 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| "Darius the Mede" rules Babylon after Belshazzar | Babylon passed to Cyrus II. No Median ruler is attested |
| 120 satraps | 20 satrapies (Herodotus) or 23 lands (Behistun) under Darius I |
| Laws of the Medes and Persians cannot be revoked | Not attested in any Persian source |
| Prayer to anyone but the king is banned | Persian kings did not claim divinity |
| Darius reigns *before* Cyrus (6:28) | Darius I came to power 17 years after Cyrus took Babylon |

## Assessment

Daniel 6 parallels chapter 3 closely. A faithful Jew defies a royal decree on worship, is condemned, and is miraculously saved, and the king then acknowledges God. A second lions' den story, set under Cyrus, appears in the Greek addition *Bel and the Dragon* (Dan 14 in Catholic Bibles). This doublet suggests a popular legend that was attached to different kings.

The setting is a literary construction. "Darius the Mede" serves the book's scheme in which Media follows Babylon, and his name and satrapies borrow from Darius I. The story's message, faithfulness to the daily prayers even under a ban, matches the situation of Jews under Antiochus IV.
