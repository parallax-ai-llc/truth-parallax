## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (apocalyptic vision).** The chapter makes no claims about events in its stated setting. Its allegory fits the Seleucid kings and the persecution of 167 BCE closely, which dates it to the Maccabean crisis.

## Dating and Setting

The vision is dated to "the first year of Belshazzar" (7:1), a title the overview shows is not historical. It is the last Aramaic chapter, but in theme and structure it belongs with the Hebrew visions of chapters 8–12. Most scholars think it was composed, or at least expanded with the "little horn" material (7:8, 11, 20–22, 24–26), around 167–166 BCE. It refers to the persecution as already under way but does not yet know of the Temple's rededication in 164 BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Four beasts rise from the sea: a lion with eagle's wings, a bear, a leopard with four wings and four heads, and a terrible fourth beast with iron teeth and **ten horns** (7:2–7).
- A **little horn** rises among the ten, uproots **three**, and has "a mouth speaking great things" (7:8).
- The "Ancient of Days" sits in judgment, the beast is destroyed, and "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds to receive everlasting dominion (7:9–14).
- The little horn will "speak words against the Most High," wear out the holy ones, and "think to change the times and the law" for "a time, times, and half a time" (7:25).

## Historical Referents

### The four beasts

In the critical reading the beasts correspond to the same four kingdoms as chapter 2: **Babylon** (lion), **Media** (bear), **Persia** (leopard, whose four heads may be the four Persian kings of 11:2), and the **Macedonian-Greek** empire (the fourth beast). Chapter 8 explicitly identifies Greece as the kingdom from which the "little horn" arises (8:21–23), which supports this reading.

### The ten horns and the little horn

The **ten horns** are most often read as the kings who ruled the Seleucid realm before Antiochus IV, from Seleucus I (312 BCE) on. Lists vary, and some include Alexander. The **little horn** is **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** (175–164 BCE). The **three uprooted horns** are usually identified with figures who stood between him and the throne:

- **Seleucus IV**, his brother, murdered in 175 BCE by his minister Heliodorus.
- **Demetrius**, son of Seleucus IV and the rightful heir, held as a hostage in Rome.
- **Antiochus**, the infant son of Seleucus IV, nominal co-ruler until he was killed in 170 BCE.

These identifications are inferences from the allegory, and scholars differ on the exact list.

### "Change the times and the law"

This fits Antiochus's decree of 167 BCE banning the Sabbath, festivals, circumcision, and Temple sacrifice (1 Macc 1:41–50). The "time, times, and half a time" (three and a half years) approximates the period of the Temple's profanation (167–164 BCE).

### "A mouth speaking great things"

Antiochus's coins carry the title **ΘΕΟΥ ΕΠΙΦΑΝΟΥΣ** ("of God Manifest"), and some later issues add *Nikephoros* ("Bringer of Victory"). Polybius (26.1) reports that people called him *Epimanes*, "the madman."

## Mythological Background

The imagery draws on older Canaanite myth rather than on historical events. Beasts rising from the chaotic sea, an aged divine judge, and a younger figure riding the clouds recall the Ugaritic texts, in which **El** is the aged head of the divine council and **Baal** is "rider of the clouds" who defeats Sea (*Yam*). J. A. Emerton (1958) made the classic argument for this background.

## Traditional Readings

From at least the late 1st century CE, Jewish and Christian interpreters identified the fourth beast with **Rome** (4 Ezra 12:11–12; Josephus *Antiquities* 10.276), and Christians read the little horn as a future Antichrist. Conservative scholars such as Steinmann continue to hold this view. The critical objection is that Daniel itself never mentions Rome as a kingdom, while chapter 8 uses the same "little horn" for a Greek king who desecrates the Temple.

## Assessment

Daniel 7 is apocalyptic allegory, not history. Its value as a historical source lies in what it reveals about its own time: a Jewish author during the persecution of Antiochus IV, using ancient myth to declare that the Seleucid tyrant's power would last only a short, measured time. The precise fit with Seleucid succession up to c. 167 BCE, and the absence of any later events, is the main evidence for its date.
