## Overview

This page does not read Daniel as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the events and setting Daniel describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

The verdict of mainstream critical scholarship is clear. Daniel is set in the Babylonian and early Persian courts of the 6th century BCE. In its final form, though, it was almost certainly composed in Judea between **c. 167 and 164 BCE**, during the persecution under the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes. The court tales (chapters 1–6) probably circulated earlier, perhaps in the 3rd century BCE, as Diaspora legends. The visions (chapters 7–12) were written during the crisis itself.

Read this way, Daniel is a highly accurate witness to the 2nd century BCE and an unreliable witness to the 6th.

## Evidence for a 2nd-Century Date

### The prophecies are precise up to 167 BCE, then wrong

Daniel 11 traces the wars between the Ptolemies ("king of the south") and the Seleucids ("king of the north") in remarkable detail. It covers the marriage alliance of Berenice (11:6), Antiochus III's campaigns (11:10–19), and Antiochus IV's second Egyptian campaign, where Rome forced him to withdraw in 168 BCE (11:29–30, the "ships of Kittim"). It then describes the desecration of the Jerusalem Temple in 167 BCE (11:31).

After that point the accuracy collapses. Daniel 11:40–45 predicts that Antiochus will invade Egypt again and die "between the sea and the glorious holy mountain," that is, in the land of Israel. In fact he died in Persia (at Tabae) in late 164 BCE. Historians read this pattern the same way whenever it appears in ancient texts. The author wrote *after* the events he describes accurately and *before* the ones he gets wrong, so the book can be dated to between the desecration (167) and Antiochus's death (164). The term for prophecy written after the events is *vaticinium ex eventu*.

### Calendar calculations that tighten as time passes

Daniel 8:14 gives 2,300 "evenings and mornings," about 1,150 days. That is roughly the period from the Temple's profanation (Kislev 167 BCE, 1 Macc 1:54) to its rededication (Kislev 164 BCE, 1 Macc 4:52). Daniel 12:11 then gives 1,290 days and 12:12 gives 1,335 days. Many scholars read these successive figures as revisions added when earlier expected dates passed. That only makes sense if a living community was waiting for them.

### Canon and citation history

- **Placement:** In the Hebrew Bible, Daniel sits among the Writings (*Ketuvim*), not the Prophets (*Nevi'im*). The prophetic collection was largely closed by the 3rd century BCE, so this placement fits a late arrival.
- **Ben Sira:** Ben Sira (c. 180 BCE) praises Israel's heroes at length (Sirach 44–49). He names Ezekiel, Job, and "the Twelve," but says nothing of Daniel, which is hard to explain if a celebrated book of Daniel already existed.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** Eight manuscripts of Daniel survive at Qumran. The oldest (4QDan<sup>c</sup>) dates to the late 2nd century BCE, only a few decades after the proposed composition date. This shows the book spread quickly, but it does not support a 6th-century origin.

### Language

The Aramaic section (2:4b–7:28) and the Hebrew are consistent with the late Persian or Hellenistic period. Three of the musical instruments in Daniel 3 have Greek names (*qitharos* from *kitharis*, *pesanterin* from *psaltērion*, *sumponyah* from *symphōnia*). Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that Greek words could have reached Babylon earlier through trade. However, *symphōnia* as the name of an instrument is not attested before the 2nd century BCE.

## Historical Problems in the Narrative

### Daniel 1:1: a siege that is not recorded

Daniel opens with Nebuchadnezzar besieging Jerusalem "in the third year of Jehoiakim" (c. 606 BCE). The Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5) records Nebuchadnezzar's victory at Carchemish in 605 BCE. It records no siege of Jerusalem that year. Jeremiah 25:1 and 46:2 place Carchemish in Jehoiakim's *fourth* year. The first attested Babylonian capture of Jerusalem is in 597 BCE.

### Belshazzar: real, but not "king" and not Nebuchadnezzar's son

Daniel 5 presents Belshazzar as king of Babylon and as the son of Nebuchadnezzar (5:2, 11, 13, 18, 22). Cuneiform sources tell a different story:

- **Bēl-šarra-uṣur** was the son of **Nabonidus**, the last Neo-Babylonian king. Nabonidus was a usurper unrelated to Nebuchadnezzar's dynasty. This is attested in the Nabonidus Cylinders from Ur and in the Nabonidus Chronicle.
- While Nabonidus spent about ten years at the Arabian oasis of Tayma, Belshazzar governed Babylon as regent. The *Verse Account of Nabonidus* says his father "entrusted the kingship" to him. Even so, Babylonian documents never give him the title **šarru** (king). Dates were reckoned by Nabonidus's regnal years, and the New Year festival lapsed because the king was absent.

This needs to be stated fairly. Nineteenth-century critics doubted Belshazzar existed at all, and cuneiform discoveries proved them wrong. The book therefore preserves a genuine memory of his name and prominence. The offer to make Daniel "third ruler in the kingdom" (5:7, 16, 29) is sometimes read as awareness of a father–son co-regency.

Defenders say "father" can mean "ancestor" or "predecessor," or propose that Nabonidus married a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar. That marriage is not attested. Most historians conclude that the author knew Belshazzar's name but had blended him into the more famous dynasty of Nebuchadnezzar.

### Darius the Mede: no matching historical figure

Daniel 5:31 and 6:1 say that "Darius the Mede" received the kingdom after Babylon fell. Daniel 9:1 calls him "son of Ahasuerus, of the seed of the Medes." Contemporary sources say otherwise:

- The **Nabonidus Chronicle** and the **Cyrus Cylinder** report that Babylon fell in October 539 BCE to the Persian **Cyrus II**. His general Ugbaru (Gubaru) entered the city without a battle.
- No Median king ruled Babylon. The Median kingdom had already been absorbed by Cyrus around 550 BCE.
- "Ahasuerus" is Xerxes I, who was the *son* of the Persian king Darius I, not his father. Darius I (522–486 BCE) came to power seventeen years after Babylon fell.

Proposed identifications include the governor Gubaru, Cyrus himself (D. J. Wiseman), and Xenophon's "Cyaxares II." None fits all the data, and Cyaxares II is probably Xenophon's own invention. H. H. Rowley's study (1935) remains the standard demonstration that "Darius the Mede" is a literary construct. The author appears to have merged memories of Darius I, who did reconquer a rebellious Babylon, with a theological scheme in which Media must rule after Babylon.

### The four-kingdom scheme and Persian history

Daniel's sequence of Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece (chapters 2, 7, 8) treats Media as a separate empire that *succeeded* Babylon. Historically, Media was Babylon's contemporary and ally, and Persia absorbed it before Babylon fell. Daniel 11:2 also knows only four Persian kings after Cyrus. In fact roughly ten reigned before Alexander. The chain of empires matches a Hellenistic literary pattern that is also found in Greek and Roman writers.

### Other details

- **"Chaldeans" as a class of wise men** (2:2, 4:7): In Neo-Babylonian texts *Kaldu* is an ethnic and tribal term. Using "Chaldean" to mean astrologer or diviner reflects later Greek usage.
- **120 satraps** (6:1): The Behistun inscription lists 23 provinces under Darius I, and Herodotus counts 20.
- **Nebuchadnezzar's madness** (ch. 4): No Babylonian source records it. However, the Qumran *Prayer of Nabonidus* (4Q242) tells how **Nabonidus** suffered an affliction for seven years at Tayma and was healed through a Jewish exiled diviner. This strongly suggests the story was first told about Nabonidus and later transferred to the better-known Nebuchadnezzar.

## The Figure of Daniel

There is no extra-biblical trace of a Judean courtier named Daniel in the Babylonian or Persian administration. Ezekiel 14:14, 20 and 28:3 name a "Dan(i)el" as a proverbially righteous and wise man alongside Noah and Job. The spelling there (*dn'l*) matches **Dan'el**, the just king-judge of the Ugaritic *Tale of Aqhat* (14th century BCE). Most scholars think the author of Daniel chose this ancient name of wisdom for his hero.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Porphyry of Tyre** (3rd century CE), in *Against the Christians*, argued that Daniel was written under Antiochus IV. His argument survives because Jerome quoted it in order to refute it in his commentary on Daniel.
- **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 11.337) tells how Alexander the Great was shown the book of Daniel in Jerusalem. Historians regard this episode as legendary.
- **Conservative scholarship** defends a 6th-century date. Representative works are E. J. Young's commentary, Kitchen (2003), and Steinmann (2008). Its main points are the early Qumran copies, the knowledge of Belshazzar, the "third ruler" detail, and the claim that the book's Aramaic resembles Imperial Aramaic.
- **Critical scholarship** answers that the Qumran copies fit a 2nd-century date. It adds that partial knowledge of Belshazzar is consistent with older traditions reaching a later author, and that the failed prediction in 11:40–45 is decisive.

## Reliability Assessment

As a record of the 6th century BCE, Daniel is **not historically reliable**. It confuses the parentage and title of Belshazzar. It invents a Median king who never ruled Babylon. It reverses the order of Persian kings and records a siege that the Babylonian Chronicle does not support.

As a source for **Jewish experience under Antiochus IV**, Daniel is valuable and largely accurate. Indeed, the precision of chapter 11 up to 167 BCE is the strongest evidence for when it was written.

The court tales are best read as edifying legends that keep some genuine historical names (Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Cyrus) inside a theological story, not as court records.
