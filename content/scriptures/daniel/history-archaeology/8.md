## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High for the events it "foresees," which end in 164 BCE (prophecy written after the events).** The vision explicitly names Media-Persia and Greece and describes Alexander, the division of his empire, and the persecution of Antiochus IV accurately. Its timetable for the Temple's restoration is close to, but not exactly, what happened.

## Dating and Setting

The vision is dated to Belshazzar's third year and set at **Susa** by the river **Ulai** (8:1–2). The text returns to Hebrew here. Most scholars date the chapter to c. 167–164 BCE, before the Temple was rededicated. The date and place are part of the literary frame: Susa had been the old Elamite capital, but it became the great Persian royal residence (and the citadel "Shushan" of Esther and Nehemiah) only under Darius I.

## What the Text Claims

- A **ram with two horns**, the longer rising last, charges west, north, and south (8:3–4). The angel identifies it as "the kings of **Media and Persia**" (8:20).
- A **male goat** from the west with one great horn, moving without touching the ground, shatters the ram (8:5–7). It is "the king of **Greece**," and the great horn is "the first king" (8:21).
- The great horn breaks, and **four horns** arise "toward the four winds" (8:8, 22).
- From one of them grows a **little horn** that expands toward the south, the east, and "the beautiful land." It casts down some of the "host of heaven," removes the **regular burnt offering** (*tamid*), and overthrows the sanctuary (8:9–12).
- The sanctuary will be restored after **2,300 evenings and mornings** (8:14).
- This king "shall be broken, but by no human hand" (8:25).

## External Evidence

### Persia and Alexander

The longer second horn fits **Persia** overtaking **Media** under Cyrus (c. 550 BCE). The goat's swift charge matches **Alexander the Great**'s conquest of the Persian empire (334–330 BCE), as narrated by Arrian and Diodorus. Alexander died in 323 BCE, and after decades of wars among his generals (the *Diadochi*), the empire settled into several kingdoms. The "four horns" reflect the usual tradition of four main successor realms (Macedonia, Thrace and Asia Minor, the Seleucid East, and Ptolemaic Egypt), which is a simplification of a messy process.

### Antiochus IV

- **South:** campaigns against Ptolemaic Egypt in 170/169 and 168 BCE.
- **East:** his final campaign in Persia and Elymais, 165–164 BCE.
- **Beautiful land:** Judea. **1 Maccabees 1:20–24** records that he plundered the Temple in 169 BCE, and **1:44–50** that he banned the sacrifices. On 15 Kislev 167 BCE a "desolating sacrilege" was set up on the altar, and on 25 Kislev pagan sacrifice began (1 Macc 1:54, 59).
- **Rededication:** Judas Maccabeus purified and rededicated the Temple on **25 Kislev 164 BCE**, three years to the day after the profanation (1 Macc 4:52–54).
- **Death:** Antiochus died in late 164 BCE at Tabae in Persia of a sudden illness, after a failed attempt to plunder a temple (Polybius 31.9; 1 Macc 6:1–16; 2 Macc 9). A Babylonian king list (Sachs and Wiseman, 1954) records the news of his death in Kislev of year 148 of the Seleucid era (late 164 BCE).

### The 2,300 evenings and mornings

If "evening and morning" counts the two daily sacrifices, 2,300 offerings equal **1,150 days**, about three years and two months. That is slightly longer than the three years between profanation and rededication given in 1 Maccabees. Scholars explain the difference in several ways: the count may start from an earlier interruption of the sacrifices, or the figure may be a forecast made before the rededication. If it is read as 2,300 full days (about six years and four months), it fits no known period. Either way, the number is aimed at the restoration of the Temple in the 160s BCE, not at a distant future.

## Points of Agreement

- **Media and Persia, then Greece**, in that order, with the angel explicitly naming them.
- **Alexander's rapid conquest** and early death, and the empire's division.
- **A Hellenistic king** who campaigns south and east, profanes the Jerusalem Temple, and ends the daily offering, exactly as Antiochus IV did.
- **Death "by no human hand"**, matching Antiochus's death from illness, not in battle.

## Points of Conflict

- **Susa in Belshazzar's reign** belongs to the literary frame. Susa was not a Babylonian royal city, and its prominence as a Persian capital dates from Darius I.
- **The 1,150 days** do not match exactly the three years recorded in 1 Maccabees.
- **"The four horns"** compress a complicated forty-year struggle into a neat four-part division.

## Assessment

Daniel 8 is the clearest proof of the book's real perspective. The angel himself names Media-Persia and Greece, and every detail of the "little horn" corresponds to Antiochus IV. Even many conservative interpreters accept that this chapter refers to Antiochus, although they may also read it as a pattern for a future Antichrist. For historians, a vision that describes 6th- to 2nd-century events so precisely, then stops at the Temple's restoration, is best explained as written by someone living through the crisis of 167–164 BCE.
