## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter combines two etiological stories: one explains the place names Massah and Meribah, the other the hostility between Israel and Amalek. Neither Rephidim nor the Amalekites are attested outside the Bible, and the site most closely tied to the Meribah tradition, Kadesh-barnea, shows no occupation in the Late Bronze Age.

## What the Text Claims

- The people camp at **Rephidim**, where there is no water, and quarrel with Moses (17:1–3).
- Moses strikes "the rock at Horeb" with his staff and water comes out; the place is named **Massah** ("testing") and **Meribah** ("quarreling") (17:5–7).
- **Amalek** attacks at Rephidim. Joshua leads the fighting while Moses holds up his hands, supported by Aaron and Hur, until Amalek is defeated (17:8–13).
- YHWH vows to "blot out the remembrance of Amalek," and Moses builds an altar called "YHWH is my banner" (17:14–16).

## External Evidence

### Rephidim and Meribah

Rephidim cannot be located. Byzantine pilgrims placed it in the Wadi Feiran oasis in southern Sinai, but this depends on the later Jebel Musa tradition for Mount Sinai. The Meribah story has a twin in Numbers 20:1–13, which sets it at **Kadesh**, and "Meribath-kadesh" appears in Deuteronomy 32:51 and Ezekiel 47:19.

### Kadesh-barnea

Kadesh-barnea is generally identified with **Tell el-Qudeirat** at the spring of Ein el-Qudeirat in northeastern Sinai, the richest water source in the region. It was excavated by Moshe Dothan (1956) and Rudolph Cohen (1976–1982). Cohen found a sequence of Iron Age fortresses, the earliest perhaps of the 10th century BCE and the latest destroyed in the early 6th century, but no Late Bronze Age remains. Cohen published the result candidly. The place where Israel supposedly stayed "many days" (Deut 1:46) produced no evidence of a 13th-century presence.

### Amalek

The Amalekites appear only in the Bible, as raiders of the Negev and Sinai (Gen 36:12; 1 Sam 15; 30). No Egyptian, Assyrian or other source names them, and no archaeological culture can be assigned to them.

## Points of Agreement

- **Water from limestone.** In Sinai, water seeping through porous limestone can collect behind a surface crust, and breaking the crust can release it. Travellers have reported this, and it may underlie the motif.
- **Desert raiding.** Conflict between herding groups over water in the Negev and Sinai is entirely plausible in any period.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 17 | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Massah/Meribah at Rephidim, "at Horeb" | Numbers 20 places Meribah at Kadesh; the tradition has more than one setting |
| Long sojourn in the Kadesh region | Ein el-Qudeirat shows no Late Bronze occupation |
| Amalek as a major enemy | No extra-biblical attestation |

## Assessment

The water story is told twice in the Pentateuch with different settings, a sign of a floating tradition attached to place names. The Amalek episode explains a later feud and justifies the command of 1 Samuel 15; the instruction to "write this as a memorial in a book" (17:14) reflects a literate, scribal culture. Joshua's sudden appearance as a fully formed war leader also suggests that the story was built to anticipate the conquest narratives. The Kadesh-barnea excavations do not disprove that small groups ever passed that way, but they remove the one place where archaeology could most reasonably have expected to find them.
