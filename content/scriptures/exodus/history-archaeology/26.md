## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The tabernacle's proportions are exactly half those of Solomon's Temple, which strongly suggests that the Temple was the model. On the other hand, a striking parallel with the Egyptian royal war tent of Ramesses II shows that a portable sanctuary of this general shape is not an anachronism. No archaeological trace of the tabernacle exists.

## What the Text Claims

- Ten curtains of fine linen in blue, purple and scarlet with **cherubim** woven in, covered by goat-hair curtains and outer coverings of rams' skins and *tachash* skins (26:1–14).
- Forty-eight upright **frames** of acacia overlaid with gold, set in silver bases, form a structure about 30 cubits long and 10 wide (26:15–30).
- A **veil** separates the holy place from the **most holy place**, a 10-cubit cube housing the ark (26:31–35).

## External Evidence

### Proportions and the Temple

According to 1 Kings 6:2, 20, Solomon's Temple was 60 by 20 cubits with an inner sanctuary forming a 20-cubit cube. The tabernacle (30 by 10, inner room 10 by 10 by 10) is exactly half. Critics since Wellhausen take this as evidence that the Priestly writer modelled the tabernacle on the Temple.

### The Egyptian war tent

Michael Homan has highlighted Egyptian reliefs of **Ramesses II's military camp at Qadesh** (c. 1274 BCE), found at Abu Simbel and other temples. They show a rectangular enclosure with an east-facing entrance and, at its centre, the king's tent, divided into a reception room and an inner room in a 2:1 proportion. In the inner room the king's cartouche is flanked by winged falcon figures, much as the cherubim flank the ark. Homan argues that the tabernacle reflects this Late Bronze Egyptian model. Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen make similar arguments for an early date.

### Materials

Acacia (Hebrew *shittim*) is the main tree of the Sinai and Arabah, so the material suits a desert setting. The meaning of *tachash* is unclear; suggestions include dugong or dolphin hide, or a fine dyed leather.

## Points of Agreement

- **Portable shrines existed.** Egyptian and Arabian evidence (including the possible tent shrine at Timna) shows that tent sanctuaries were real.
- **Local materials.** Acacia wood and goat hair are what desert pastoralists would use.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 26 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Tabernacle predates the Temple | Its dimensions are a scaled version of the Temple's |
| Massive gold-plated acacia frames | Each frame (10 by 1.5 cubits) would be very heavy if solid; some propose open frames instead |
| Lavish dyed fabrics in the desert | Blue and purple dyes came mainly from Phoenician murex workshops |

## Assessment

Two explanations compete. On the critical view, the Priestly writers created the tabernacle as a portable ideal Temple. On Homan's and Kitchen's view, an ancient tent-shrine tradition was later embellished. The two are not wholly exclusive: Frank Moore Cross suggested that David's tent for the ark (2 Sam 6:17) may lie behind the description. The half-scale Temple dimensions, however, show that whatever older memory existed, the text as we have it was shaped around the Jerusalem sanctuary.
