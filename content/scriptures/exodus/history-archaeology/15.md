## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed (as evidence for early tradition).** The Song of the Sea is not an eyewitness report, but it is widely regarded as one of the oldest compositions in the Hebrew Bible. It shows that a victory of YHWH at the sea was celebrated in Israel very early. The short itinerary that follows (Marah, Elim) cannot be located.

## What the Text Claims

- Moses and Israel sing: YHWH "has thrown horse and rider into the sea"; Pharaoh's chariots and officers "sank in the *yam suf*" (15:1–5).
- The deep "congealed," and the enemy drowned (15:8–10).
- The peoples tremble: the inhabitants of **Philistia**, the chiefs of **Edom**, the leaders of **Moab**, and all the inhabitants of **Canaan** (15:14–16).
- YHWH will plant his people on "the mountain of your inheritance, the sanctuary" (15:17).
- **Miriam** the prophetess sings, "Sing to YHWH... horse and rider he has thrown into the sea" (15:20–21).
- At **Marah** the water is bitter until Moses throws in a piece of wood; they camp at **Elim**, with twelve springs and seventy palms (15:22–27).

## External Evidence

### Archaic poetry

Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman argued that the Song (15:1b–18) shows features of early Hebrew poetry: archaic verb forms, old pronominal suffixes (e.g. *-mo*), and parallelism like that of the Ugaritic texts of the 13th century BCE. They dated it to the 12th–11th centuries BCE. Many scholars still accept an early date, putting the Song alongside the Song of Deborah (Judg 5) among Israel's oldest texts.

Others are more cautious. Some think the archaic features may be deliberate archaising, and Martin Noth and others dated the Song later. The reference to a sanctuary on YHWH's mountain (15:17) has been read as alluding to the Jerusalem Temple, which would point to the monarchy.

### What the poem says, and does not say

The Song describes the Egyptians being thrown or sinking into the sea, and the waters standing "like a heap." It does not describe Israel walking through on dry ground between walls of water. Some scholars think the image is of a storm at sea capsizing the enemy, a motif with parallels in the Canaanite myth of Baal's victory over Yam (Sea). The mythic background of YHWH as divine warrior against the waters is clear.

### Miriam's song

The two-line Song of Miriam (15:21) is often considered the oldest form of the tradition. It celebrates a victory without naming Pharaoh or giving details. Women's victory songs with drums are a known Israelite custom (Judg 11:34; 1 Sam 18:6–7).

### The nations named

The listing of Philistia, Edom, Moab, and Canaan assumes the political map of the early Iron Age. Philistines settled c. 1175 BCE, and Edom and Moab as organised polities are attested from the late 13th century BCE (Edom in Papyrus Anastasi VI, Moab in Ramesses II's reliefs at Luxor). This fits an early Iron Age date for the poem, not the time of the exodus itself.

### Marah and Elim

Neither site has been identified. Traditional identifications (Ain Hawarah, Wadi Gharandel) on the western Sinai coast are late. No archaeological evidence connects them with the exodus.

## Points of Agreement

- The Song's language contains authentically early features.
- The political map of the poem matches the early Iron Age.
- Victory songs by women are a genuine ancient Israelite form.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 15 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| The Song sung at the time of the crossing | Earliest proposed date is the 12th century BCE, after any proposed exodus date |
| Peoples of Philistia tremble | Anachronistic for a 15th- or 13th-century exodus |
| Marah and Elim | Unidentified |

## Assessment

Chapter 15 is the closest thing to early evidence that the exodus tradition offers. If Cross and Freedman are right, Israelites in the 12th–11th centuries BCE were already celebrating a victory of YHWH over Egypt at the sea. That does not confirm the prose narrative of chapter 14, and the poem uses the mythic imagery of a storm god defeating the sea. It does show that the memory of deliverance from Egypt is not a late invention. Many scholars therefore hold that some group, perhaps the "small exodus" group that joined Israel from the south, brought this song and its story into Israel very early.
