## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (law code).** Chapter 21 opens the Covenant Code (20:22–23:33), the oldest law collection in the Bible. It does not describe events. Its value for history lies in its close relationship to Mesopotamian law, which shows Israelite scribes working within a long cuneiform legal tradition.

## What the Text Claims

- A Hebrew debt-slave serves **six years** and goes free in the seventh, unless he chooses to stay and has his ear pierced at the door (21:2–6).
- A daughter sold as a slave-wife has protected rights (21:7–11).
- Death penalties for murder, striking or cursing parents, and kidnapping, with asylum at an altar for accidental killing (21:12–17).
- Rules for injuries, including a blow causing a woman to miscarry, and the formula "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (21:18–27).
- An ox that gores a person is stoned; if it was known to gore and the owner did not restrain it, the owner is also liable (21:28–36).

## External Evidence

### Parallels with the Code of Hammurabi and the Laws of Eshnunna

The parallels are close and often follow the same case structure ("If a man...").

| Exodus 21 | Mesopotamian law |
|-----------|------------------|
| Debt-slave released after six years (21:2) | Hammurabi §117: debt-servants released in the fourth year |
| Kidnapping punished by death (21:16) | Hammurabi §14: kidnapping a child punished by death |
| Striking a father (21:15) | Hammurabi §195: the son's hand is cut off |
| Miscarriage caused by a blow (21:22–25) | Hammurabi §§209–214; similar cases in Middle Assyrian and Hittite laws |
| Eye for eye (21:23–25) | Hammurabi §§196–200 apply talion between social equals |
| Goring ox, warned owner (21:28–32, 35–36) | Eshnunna §§53–55; Hammurabi §§250–252 |

The goring-ox laws are the most striking. Exodus 21:35, where the live ox is sold and its price divided along with the dead animal, matches Laws of Eshnunna §53 almost word for word in substance.

### How did the borrowing happen?

David P. Wright has argued that the Covenant Code was composed by a scribe who directly used a copy of Hammurabi's laws, most likely in the Neo-Assyrian period (8th–7th centuries BCE), when Hammurabi's stele text was still copied in scribal schools. Others, such as Raymond Westbrook, preferred to explain the parallels as a shared legal tradition passed down orally and in scribal training across the region.

## Points of Agreement

- **Genuine ancient legal culture.** The Covenant Code belongs firmly to the world of cuneiform law, both in form (casuistic law) and content.
- **Older than Deuteronomy.** Deuteronomy 15:12–18 revises the slave law, for example extending release to women, which shows the Covenant Code is earlier.

## Points of Conflict

- The text presents these laws as revealed at Sinai to a people just out of Egypt. Their content assumes a settled agrarian society with houses, fields, oxen and silver payments, not a desert camp.
- Exodus differs from Hammurabi in notable ways: no class distinctions in the talion law, and a theological framing. These differences look like deliberate revisions of an existing tradition.

## Assessment

The chapter cannot be dated to a wilderness period. Its relationship with Mesopotamian law is real and well documented. Whether through direct use of Hammurabi (Wright) or shared tradition (Westbrook), the Covenant Code is best placed in the monarchic period and is the earliest layer of biblical law.
