## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No source outside the Bible mentions Moses. The story of his birth closely follows a widespread legend pattern, best known from the Mesopotamian birth legend of Sargon of Akkad. His Egyptian name and the Midianite setting may preserve older tradition, but the narrative itself is best read as a hero legend.

## What the Text Claims

- A Levite woman hides her son, then sets him in a basket of papyrus sealed with bitumen and pitch among the reeds of the Nile (2:1–3).
- Pharaoh's daughter finds him, hires his own mother as nurse, and names him **Moses**, "because I drew him out of the water" (2:5–10).
- Grown up, Moses kills an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew and flees to **Midian** (2:11–15).
- He marries Zipporah, daughter of the priest **Reuel** (2:16–22).
- The king of Egypt dies; God hears the Israelites' groaning (2:23–25).

## External Evidence

### The Sargon birth legend

A Neo-Assyrian text known as the *Legend of Sargon* tells how Sargon of Akkad (24th–23rd centuries BCE) was born in secret to a priestess. She placed him in a basket of reeds, sealed its lid with bitumen, and set him in the river. He was drawn out by a water-drawer named Aqqi, who raised him, and he went on to become king. The surviving copies date from the 8th–7th centuries BCE, close to the time when many scholars think the Moses story took shape.

The parallels are specific: secret birth, a reed basket, bitumen sealing, the river, rescue by a stranger, and rise to greatness. Brian Lewis showed that this "exposed hero" pattern also appears in stories of Cyrus, Romulus and Remus, Oedipus, and others.

### The name Moses

The Hebrew explanation of *Mosheh* from *mashah*, "to draw out" (2:10), is a folk etymology. Grammatically it would mean "one who draws out," not "one drawn out," and an Egyptian princess would hardly coin a Hebrew pun. Most Egyptologists derive the name from Egyptian ***msi / ms***, "born of" or "child," as in Ramesses ("Ra is born") and Thutmose ("Thoth is born"). J. Gwyn Griffiths set out the case in 1953. An Egyptian name suggests that the figure behind the story had real Egyptian connections, or at least that the tradition was formed among people familiar with Egypt.

### Midian

Midianites are associated with northwest Arabia and the region east of the Gulf of Aqaba. Archaeology there, at sites such as Qurayyah, has found a distinctive painted pottery of the Late Bronze and early Iron Ages. The Midianite setting, and the later prominence of Moses' Midianite father-in-law (ch. 18), is one basis for the theory that the worship of YHWH came from the south (see chapter 3).

## Points of Agreement

- Papyrus boats and baskets sealed with bitumen and pitch are realistic for Egypt and the Near East.
- Foreigners were raised at the Egyptian court. Sons of Asiatic princes were brought up in the royal nursery (the *kap*) in the New Kingdom.
- The Egyptian origin of the name Moses is widely accepted.
- Flight from Pharaoh to the eastern deserts echoes the Egyptian *Story of Sinuhe*, in which an official flees to Canaan.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 2 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| A historical Moses | No extra-biblical attestation of any kind |
| Birth and rescue story | Matches the folklore pattern of the Sargon legend and other exposed-hero tales |
| Hebrew meaning of the name | Folk etymology; the name is Egyptian |
| The father-in-law is Reuel | Elsewhere he is Jethro (3:1; 18:1) or Hobab (Num 10:29; Judg 4:11), suggesting different source traditions |
| "The king of Egypt died" | No king is named, so the event cannot be tied to Egyptian chronology |

## Assessment

Chapter 2 is classic hero legend. The likeness to the Sargon story is close enough that many scholars think the author knew the Mesopotamian tale, perhaps through Assyrian contact in the 8th–7th centuries BCE. Conservative scholars such as Hoffmeier stress the Egyptian details and argue that the motif of rescuing an abandoned child is too common to prove borrowing.

Even many critical scholars think there may have been a real leader with an Egyptian name behind the figure of Moses, since an invented Israelite hero would more likely have received a Hebrew name. But nothing in this chapter can be verified, and its narrative belongs to legend rather than biography.
