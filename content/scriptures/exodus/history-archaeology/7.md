## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No Egyptian text records the Nile turning to blood or any of the plagues. The chapter has authentic Egyptian colouring, such as court magicians and the centrality of the Nile, but the narrative is a theological contest between YHWH and the gods of Egypt, not a record of events.

## What the Text Claims

- Moses is 80 and Aaron 83 when they speak to Pharaoh (7:7).
- Aaron's staff becomes a serpent (*tannin*); Pharaoh's magicians do the same, but Aaron's staff swallows theirs (7:8–13).
- Aaron strikes the Nile, and all its water, even water "in vessels of wood and stone," turns to blood; the fish die and the river stinks (7:14–21).
- The Egyptian magicians do the same by their secret arts, and Pharaoh's heart is hardened (7:22–24).

## External Evidence

### The Ipuwer papyrus

The *Admonitions of Ipuwer* (Papyrus Leiden I 344) is often cited as an Egyptian account of the plagues because it contains the line "the river is blood" and describes chaos, death, and servants rising against masters. Egyptologists reject this reading.

- The surviving copy dates to the 19th Dynasty (13th century BCE), but the composition is generally dated to the late Middle Kingdom, centuries before any proposed exodus date.
- It belongs to a known Egyptian genre of "lamentations" that describe the world turned upside down as a literary theme, not as a report of a specific event.
- It does not mention Israelites, Moses, a departure of slaves, or a disaster at the sea.

Roland Enmarch's edition and commentary is the standard modern treatment.

### Natural explanations

Greta Hort (1957–58) proposed that the plagues were a chain of natural events starting with an unusually high Nile carrying red soil and toxic algae (a "red tide"), which killed the fish and drove out the frogs. Variations of this theory remain popular. They can explain the general sequence, but they require ignoring parts of the text (e.g. water in vessels turning to blood, the magicians' duplication) and do not explain why no Egyptian source records such a catastrophe.

### Court magicians

The Hebrew word for Pharaoh's magicians, *ḥarṭummim*, derives from Egyptian *ḥry-tp*, "chief lector priest," a real title of ritual specialists. The *Westcar Papyrus* contains tales of lector priests performing wonders before the king, including bringing a wax crocodile to life. The serpent contest echoes this tradition.

## Points of Agreement

- The Egyptian loanword for magicians is authentic.
- Stories of court wonder-workers competing before Pharaoh are a genuine Egyptian literary type.
- Red discoloration of the Nile during the annual flood was a real phenomenon.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 7 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| The whole Nile turns to blood | No Egyptian record; Ipuwer is an earlier literary lament, not a report |
| Magicians also turn water to blood after all water was already blood | Internal tension, often explained by combined sources |
| Moses aged 80 | Schematic number (three periods of 40 years in Moses' life, Deut 34:7) |
| Plague narrative as Egyptian history | Psalms 78 and 105 give different lists and orders of the plagues |

## Assessment

The first plague introduces a cycle built on a repeated literary pattern: warning, action, the magicians' response, and Pharaoh's hardened heart. Source critics find two interwoven strands. In one (non-Priestly), Moses strikes the river and only the Nile is affected. In the other (Priestly), Aaron stretches out his staff over all the waters of Egypt. Conservative scholars such as Hoffmeier read the plagues as targeting Egyptian deities, and the Nile was indeed divine in Egyptian thought (Hapi). That reading has theological merit, but it does not provide historical evidence. The appeal to Ipuwer, still common in popular literature, should be abandoned.
