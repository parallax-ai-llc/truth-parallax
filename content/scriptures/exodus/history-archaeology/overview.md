## Overview

This page does not read Exodus as Jews, Christians, or Muslims read it. It asks a different question: **what do Egyptian records, archaeology in Egypt and Sinai, and comparative ancient Near Eastern texts say about the events Exodus describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

The short answer is that there is **no direct evidence** for the exodus as the Bible tells it. No Egyptian text mentions Israelite slaves, Moses, the plagues, the death of Egypt's firstborn, or the loss of an army at the sea. No archaeological trace of two million people crossing and camping in Sinai has been found. Most critical scholars therefore conclude that the exodus story is not a record of a single historical event on the scale described.

That is not the end of the matter. The story's Egyptian colouring is in places authentic, Semitic people really did live and labour in the eastern Nile Delta, and Egypt really did expel a Semitic-ruled population (the Hyksos) around 1550 BCE. Many scholars therefore think the narrative grew out of real memories, perhaps of a smaller group, that were later enlarged into the founding story of all Israel.

## The Date Problem

The Bible gives two incompatible chronological clues.

- **The 15th-century date.** 1 Kings 6:1 places the exodus 480 years before the fourth year of Solomon (c. 966 BCE), which yields c. 1446 BCE. Conservative scholars such as Bryant Wood defend this date. But 480 is twelve generations of forty years, which looks schematic, and in 1446 Canaan was firmly under Egyptian control under Thutmose III and Amenhotep II.
- **The 13th-century date.** Exodus 1:11 says the Hebrews built "Pithom and Raamses." Pi-Ramesses, the Delta capital at modern **Qantir**, was built by Ramesses II (c. 1279–1213 BCE). Most scholars who think there is any history behind the story, including conservative Egyptologists such as K. A. Kitchen and James Hoffmeier, therefore prefer a date under Ramesses II.

The **Merneptah Stele** (c. 1208 BCE) lists "Israel" as a people in Canaan defeated by Merneptah. Any exodus must therefore come before that date, which leaves little room for a 13th-century exodus followed by forty years in the wilderness and a conquest.

A further problem is that some details fit a much later period. **Tell el-Maskhuta**, the usual candidate for Pithom or Succoth, has a Hyksos-era phase and then no substantial occupation until the late 7th century BCE. "The way of the land of the Philistines" (13:17) assumes Philistines on the coast, who arrived only around 1175 BCE. Donald Redford argues that the geography of the story best fits the Saite period (7th–6th centuries BCE).

## Semites in Egypt

Semitic-speaking people from Canaan were a constant presence in Egypt.

- The **Hyksos** ("rulers of foreign lands") were a West Semitic dynasty that ruled the northern Delta from **Avaris** (Tell el-Dab'a) c. 1640–1530 BCE. Manfred Bietak's excavations show a Canaanite-style material culture there.
- A Middle Kingdom household list (Brooklyn Papyrus 35.1446) names dozens of Asiatic servants, including a woman called *Shiphrah*, the same name as one of the Hebrew midwives (1:15).
- The tomb of the vizier **Rekhmire** (time of Thutmose III) shows foreign captives making mud bricks.
- **Leiden Papyrus 348**, a Ramesside letter, orders grain rations for "the 'Apiru who are dragging stone" for a building project of Ramesses II.

None of these sources mentions Israel. They show that the kind of event Exodus describes is plausible in general, not that it happened.

## The Hyksos Expulsion and Manetho

The Egyptian priest **Manetho** (3rd century BCE), quoted by Josephus in *Against Apion* 1, describes the Hyksos being driven out of Avaris and going to Judea, where they built Jerusalem. Josephus took this as an Egyptian account of the exodus. Manetho also tells a separate story of lepers led by a renegade priest, Osarseph, who later took the name Moses. These accounts are late, polemical, and probably shaped by contact with Jewish tradition, so they cannot confirm the biblical story. But the expulsion of the Hyksos by Ahmose (c. 1530 BCE) is real, and several scholars, including Redford, think the Canaanite memory of it fed into the exodus tradition.

## The Exodus Story's Inner Problems

- **Population.** Exodus 12:37 gives 600,000 men on foot besides women and children, suggesting two million or more people. That would exceed Egypt's estimated population at the time, and the Sinai could not have supported them. Some propose that *'elef* ("thousand") originally meant a clan or military unit, which would reduce the numbers to a few thousand (Mendenhall, Humphreys).
- **No archaeological trace in Sinai.** Surveys and excavations at **Kadesh-barnea**, where Israel is said to have stayed for decades, found no Late Bronze Age remains.
- **Composite plagues.** Source criticism finds different numbers of plagues in different strands, and Psalms 78 and 105 give different lists and orders.
- **The sea.** The Hebrew *yam suf* means "Sea of Reeds," and the crossing has been placed at several lakes in the eastern Delta as well as the Gulf of Suez.

## A Smaller Exodus?

Archaeology shows that most early Israelites were indigenous Canaanites who settled the central hill country in the 12th–11th centuries BCE (Finkelstein, Dever, Faust). Many scholars now propose a **"small exodus"**: a group that left Egypt and later joined these highland settlers, bringing its story and its god with it.

- **The Levites** are the best candidate. Several Levite names are Egyptian (Moses, Hophni, Phinehas, Merari), and Richard Elliott Friedman and others argue that the Levites were the exodus group.
- **YHWH from the south.** Egyptian topographical lists of Amenhotep III at **Soleb** and of Ramesses II at **Amarah-West** mention "the land of the Shasu of YHW," apparently a nomadic region in Edom or Midian. Old biblical poems also bring YHWH from Seir, Edom, and Teman (Judg 5:4; Deut 33:2; Hab 3:3). This fits the Midianite setting of Moses' call (Exod 2–3).

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Ancient writers** such as Manetho, Hecataeus of Abdera (in Diodorus 40.3), and Tacitus (*Histories* 5.3) offer Egyptian or Greek versions of the expulsion. All are late and hostile or derivative.
- **Conservative scholarship** (Kitchen 2003; Hoffmeier 1997, 2005) argues that the Egyptian details are authentic, that slaves and defeats would not appear in Egyptian royal records, and that nomads leave little trace. On this view absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **Critical scholarship** (Redford 1992; Finkelstein and Silberman 2001; Grabbe 2007) replies that the scale of the story would have left traces, that Egyptian control of Sinai and Canaan in the Late Bronze Age leaves no room for the events, and that the Egyptian details mostly fit the 1st millennium BCE.
- **A middle position**, represented in the essays in Levy, Schneider, and Propp (2015), holds that the story is not history as told but preserves a core of cultural memory.

## Reliability Assessment

As a record of a mass migration of two million Israelites out of Egypt in the 15th or 13th century BCE, Exodus is **not historically reliable**. There is no Egyptian, Sinai, or Canaanite evidence for it, and much counts against it.

As a reflection of the **Egyptian world in general**, the book is mixed. Some names and practices are authentic. Others fit the Saite or Persian period better than the Late Bronze Age.

As **cultural memory**, the story may well preserve real experiences: Semitic labour in the Delta, the Hyksos expulsion, and a small group, perhaps Levites, who carried the worship of a southern god YHWH into Israel. The archaic Song of the Sea (Exod 15) shows that a story of deliverance at the sea was celebrated very early in Israel's history.
