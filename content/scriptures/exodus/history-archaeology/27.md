## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (cultic instruction).** The chapter specifies the altar of burnt offering and the tabernacle courtyard. Horned altars are well attested archaeologically in Iron Age Israel and Judah, but the specific portable bronze-covered altar of this chapter is not, and it raises practical problems.

## What the Text Claims

- An **altar** of acacia wood, 5 by 5 cubits and 3 high, with **horns** at its four corners, overlaid with bronze, with a bronze grating and carrying poles (27:1–8).
- A **courtyard** 100 by 50 cubits enclosed by linen hangings on bronze-based posts, with an embroidered screen at the east gate (27:9–19).
- Pure beaten olive oil is to keep a lamp burning continually before the veil (27:20–21).

## External Evidence

### Horned altars

Several Iron Age horned altars have been excavated:

- **Beersheba:** a large altar of dressed stones with horns, found dismantled and reused in a later wall. Yohanan Aharoni linked its dismantling to Hezekiah's reform (2 Kgs 18:4), though this is debated.
- **Tel Dan:** a sacred precinct with a large altar platform, and a horn from a stone altar.
- **Megiddo** and **Tel Miqne-Ekron:** smaller horned limestone altars, many of them probably for incense.

The horns were the holiest part of the altar, which fits the blood rites of 29:12 and the custom of seizing the altar horns for asylum (1 Kgs 1:50).

### Olive oil

The specification of "beaten" oil reflects real production methods. Large-scale olive-oil industry is well attested in Iron Age Israel and Philistia, notably the 7th-century installations at Tel Miqne-Ekron.

## Points of Agreement

- **Horns on altars** match Iron Age Levantine practice.
- **An east-facing entrance** fits the orientation of many ancient temples, including Solomon's.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 27 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Wooden altar plated with bronze used for burnt offerings | Sustained fires would burn the wood; most proposed solutions assume a hollow frame filled with earth or stones |
| Bronze altar in contrast to 20:24–25 | The earlier altar law calls for earth or unhewn stone |
| Large courtyard of linen and bronze | A huge investment in bronze castings for a desert people |

## Assessment

The altar resembles the bronze altar of Solomon's Temple (1 Kgs 8:64; 2 Kgs 16:14), scaled to be portable. That again suggests the Priestly author modelled the wilderness sanctuary on the Temple. The real horned altars of Iron Age Judah show that the author knew actual cultic forms, but they are evidence for the period of the monarchy, not for the wilderness.
