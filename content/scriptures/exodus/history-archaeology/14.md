## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No Egyptian source records the loss of a chariot army at the sea, and no archaeological trace of the event exists. The place names are partly identifiable, but several point to the 1st millennium BCE, and the chapter combines two different accounts of how the sea was crossed.

## What the Text Claims

- Israel turns back and camps before **Pi-hahiroth**, between **Migdol** and the sea, in front of **Baal-zephon** (14:1–2).
- Pharaoh pursues with 600 chosen chariots and all the chariots of Egypt (14:5–9).
- Moses stretches out his hand; YHWH drives back the sea with a **strong east wind all night** and makes it dry land (14:21).
- The waters form "a wall to them on their right hand and on their left" (14:22, 29).
- The Egyptians follow; their chariot wheels clog; the sea returns, and "not one of them remained" (14:23–28).

## External Evidence

### The sea: *yam suf*

The Hebrew *yam suf* means "Sea of Reeds" (*suf* is related to Egyptian *ṯwf*, "papyrus, reeds"). The Septuagint translated it as "Red Sea," which gave the familiar name. Elsewhere *yam suf* clearly means the Gulf of Aqaba (1 Kgs 9:26). Proposed crossing points include:

- the **Gulf of Suez**, the traditional view;
- the **Bitter Lakes** or **Lake Timsah**, along the line of the later Suez Canal;
- the **Ballah Lakes** or ancient lagoons of the eastern Delta, favoured by Hoffmeier;
- **Lake Sirbonis** (Bardawil) on the Mediterranean coast, proposed by Otto Eissfeldt, where Greek writers said armies had been swallowed by shifting sands.

### Place names

- **Migdol** is a Semitic word for "tower," used by Egyptians for frontier forts. Migdols on the eastern frontier are attested in New Kingdom texts and, prominently, in the 7th–6th centuries BCE (Jer 44:1; 46:14). Excavations at **Tell Qedwa** (T21) and other sites in north Sinai have revealed Saite-period forts.
- **Baal-zephon** was a Canaanite storm god worshipped in Egypt; a sanctuary of his near Tahpanhes (Daphnae) is known from later sources, including a Phoenician letter of the 6th century BCE.
- **Pi-hahiroth** remains unidentified.

Redford argues that the itinerary matches the eastern Delta frontier of the Saite period. Hoffmeier argues that New Kingdom equivalents existed.

### Two accounts combined

Source critics identify:

- a **non-Priestly** version in which YHWH drives back the sea with an east wind all night, the Egyptians panic, and the sea returns at dawn (14:21b, 24–25, 27b); and
- a **Priestly** version in which Moses stretches out his hand, the waters split into walls, and Israel walks through (14:16, 21a, 22–23, 26–27a, 29).

The first is a natural event made miraculous by its timing; the second is a cosmic miracle.

### Wind setdown

Carl Drews and Weiqing Han (2010) modelled how a strong, steady east wind over a shallow lagoon in the eastern Delta could push water back and expose a land bridge for several hours. This shows that the wind version is physically conceivable in the right setting. It does not show that it happened.

## Points of Agreement

- Lakes, lagoons, and marshes on the eastern Delta frontier are real and changed over time.
- Migdols and frontier forts existed on Egypt's eastern border.
- Chariot forces were the core of the New Kingdom army.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 14 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| Pharaoh's army destroyed | No Egyptian record; no known pharaoh died this way (Ramesses II and Merneptah were buried in the Valley of the Kings) |
| Location of the crossing | Unknown; several candidates |
| Walls of water vs. an all-night east wind | Two versions combined |
| Itinerary toponyms | Several best attested in the 7th–6th centuries BCE |

## Assessment

The crossing of the sea is the central event of Israel's founding story, but it has no external confirmation. The text itself never says that Pharaoh died (Ps 136:15 says he was "overthrown"). Conservative scholars note that Egypt would not record a defeat and that a crossing at a marshy lagoon would leave no trace. Critical scholars respond that the scale of the event in the text is far beyond a local incident, and that the geography fits a later writer. The oldest form of the tradition may be the short poetic celebration in 15:21, which says only that YHWH threw horse and rider into the sea.
