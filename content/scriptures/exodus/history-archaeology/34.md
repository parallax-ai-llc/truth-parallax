## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (law and theological narrative).** The chapter renews the covenant after the golden calf. Its list of commandments differs from the Decalogue of chapter 20 and overlaps heavily with the Covenant Code. Its ban on asherahs gains context from inscriptions showing that YHWH was once worshipped alongside "his asherah."

## What the Text Claims

- Moses cuts two new tablets; YHWH passes by and proclaims himself "merciful and gracious, slow to anger" (34:1–7).
- Israel must make no covenant with the inhabitants of the land, and must tear down their altars, pillars and **asherahs** (34:11–16).
- A list of cultic commands follows: no molten gods, the festivals, firstborn offerings, Sabbath, and "you shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk" (34:17–26).
- Moses writes "the ten words" on the tablets (34:27–28). When he comes down, the skin of his face shines (34:29–35).

## External Evidence

### Asherah inscriptions

At **Kuntillet ʿAjrud** (c. 800 BCE), inscriptions bless "by YHWH of Samaria and his asherah" and "YHWH of Teman and his asherah." A tomb inscription from **Khirbet el-Qom** near Hebron (8th century BCE) also links YHWH with "his asherah." Scholars debate whether "asherah" here means the goddess Asherah or a wooden cult symbol, but either way it was associated with YHWH in mainstream worship. The command in 34:13 reflects later reforming efforts to separate the two (compare 2 Kgs 23:4–7).

### The divine attributes formula

The formula in 34:6–7 is echoed in several later texts (Num 14:18; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Neh 9:17). Its many echoes in late books suggest it became a standard liturgical confession over time.

### Moses' face

The Hebrew verb *qaran* ("shone") is related to *qeren*, "horn." Jerome's Vulgate rendered it "horned," which led to the horned Moses of medieval and Renaissance art. Some scholars compare the divine radiance (*melammu*) of Mesopotamian gods and kings.

## Points of Agreement

- **YHWH and asherah.** The extra-biblical inscriptions confirm that asherah worship was part of Israelite and Judahite religion, which explains why the law targets it.
- **Agrarian festivals.** The festival list (34:18–26) matches the farming calendar of chapter 23.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 34 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| "The ten words" (34:28) | The commands listed differ from Exod 20 and are largely cultic |
| Renewal of the same covenant | Laws largely repeat 23:12–19, suggesting a parallel version rather than a renewal |
| Asherahs as foreign | Inscriptions show asherah linked with YHWH himself |

## Assessment

In 1773 Goethe proposed that 34:11–26 was the original text of the tablets, and later scholars, including Wellhausen, called it the "Ritual Decalogue." Most now see it as a cultic law collection related to the Covenant Code, reshaped into the covenant-renewal story. The asherah prohibition is best understood against the religious reforms of the late monarchy.
