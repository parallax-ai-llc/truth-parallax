## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** These plagues, a livestock epidemic, skin sores, and a destructive hailstorm, are unattested outside the Bible. The chapter contains internal tensions that point to combined sources and to literary escalation rather than eyewitness reporting.

## What the Text Claims

- A pestilence kills "all the livestock of Egypt," but none of Israel's (9:1–7).
- Soot thrown in the air causes boils on people and animals; the magicians cannot stand before Moses (9:8–12).
- A hailstorm "such as had never been in all the land of Egypt" strikes, with fire flashing; Goshen is spared (9:13–26).
- The **flax and barley** are destroyed because the barley was in the ear and the flax in bud, but the **wheat and spelt** survive because they ripen later (9:31–32).
- Pharaoh confesses sin but hardens his heart again (9:27–35).

## External Evidence

### Hail in Egypt

Hail is rare in Egypt but not unknown, especially in the north. The text itself stresses its exceptional character. No Egyptian document records a catastrophic hailstorm of this kind. The "fire" with the hail recalls the storm imagery of the divine warrior in Canaanite and biblical poetry (e.g. Ps 18:12–13), which may explain the description.

### Agricultural detail

The note on crops (9:31–32) is the most precise piece of local knowledge in the plague cycle. In Egypt, barley and flax ripen earlier (around February–March) than wheat and emmer. This places the hail in late winter, consistent with a spring Passover. The detail shows knowledge of Egyptian or general Near Eastern farming, though the same seasonal pattern holds in Canaan.

### Natural explanations

Natural-chain theorists (Hort and others) suggest the livestock disease was anthrax spread by insects and that the boils were a related skin infection. Animal epidemics are well known in ancient sources; Hittite plague prayers of Mursili II (14th century BCE) describe a long epidemic. But naturalistic readings cannot account for the selective sparing of Israel, which is central to the story's meaning.

## Points of Agreement

- Livestock disease and skin infections were real threats in antiquity.
- The ripening sequence of barley, flax, wheat, and spelt is accurate.
- Egypt grew flax extensively for linen.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 9 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| "All the livestock of Egypt" die (9:6) | Yet livestock are struck again by boils (9:10) and hail (9:19–25) and die again as firstborn (11:5; 12:29) |
| Unprecedented hail | No Egyptian record |
| Israel's livestock wholly spared | A theological distinction, not testable |
| Pharaoh's repeated hardening | A literary motif with theological purpose |

## Assessment

The repeated killing of the same livestock is a classic sign that separate plague accounts were combined without full harmonisation. Each plague escalates, with the magicians now physically unable to stand (9:11) and Pharaoh making a first confession (9:27). This is narrative art, building toward the climax of the firstborn. The precise agricultural note shows a writer who knew the farming year, but it is no evidence that the events happened. Mainstream scholarship regards the plague cycle as a theological composition, not a record of Egyptian disasters.
