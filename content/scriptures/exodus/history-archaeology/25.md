## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Chapters 25–31 belong to the Priestly source and describe an idealised sanctuary. Most critics see it as a projection of the Jerusalem Temple back into the wilderness. Egyptian portable shrines show that the basic idea of a carried chest and tent shrine is ancient and plausible, but no physical evidence for the tabernacle exists.

## What the Text Claims

- The people bring a freewill offering of gold, silver, bronze, dyed yarn, fine linen, goat hair, skins and acacia wood (25:1–7).
- They are to make a sanctuary "that I may dwell among them," following a **pattern** shown to Moses (25:8–9).
- The **ark** is an acacia chest overlaid with gold, carried on poles, with a gold **mercy seat** (*kapporet*) and two **cherubim** above it (25:10–22).
- A gold-plated **table** for the bread of the Presence, and a pure gold **lampstand** with six branches and almond-blossom cups (25:23–40).

## External Evidence

### Egyptian parallels

- **Portable chests and shrines.** The tomb of Tutankhamun (14th century BCE) contained a gilded wooden shrine-chest with an Anubis figure, mounted on carrying poles, and nested gilded shrines. Egyptian temple reliefs show gods' barque shrines carried on poles by priests.
- **Winged guardians.** Egyptian shrines and furniture often show protective winged figures spread over a sacred object.

Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that such parallels, mostly from the 2nd millennium, make a Late Bronze origin for the tabernacle plausible. Michael Homan has also argued that the tabernacle design matches Egyptian military tents, discussed further in chapter 26.

### Cherubim and the empty throne

In the Levant, a king or god seated on a throne flanked by winged sphinxes appears on the **Megiddo ivories** (Late Bronze) and the **Ahiram sarcophagus** from Byblos (c. 1000 BCE). The cherubim over the ark reflect this imagery of a divine throne, with the ark as its footstool (compare 1 Chr 28:2; Ps 132:7).

## Points of Agreement

- **An ancient ark tradition.** Older narratives (1 Sam 4–6; 2 Sam 6) describe an ark carried into battle and brought to Jerusalem by David. This suggests a real cult object existed in early Israel.
- **Plausible technology.** Gold-sheathed wooden furniture on poles is well known from Egypt.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 25 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Lavish gold furnishings made in the desert | Quantities of gold and skilled metalwork assume royal resources |
| Ark with *kapporet* and gold cherubim | The older ark stories never describe the ark this way |
| Lampstand with seven lamps | The seven-branched form is first depicted in the 1st century BCE |

## Assessment

Since Julius Wellhausen, critical scholars have read the tabernacle as a portable version of the Jerusalem Temple, written by Priestly authors in the exile or after it to show that true worship originated at Sinai. Egyptian parallels show that its forms are not anachronistic in themselves, and an older, simpler ark and tent tradition probably lies behind it. But the detailed sanctuary of chapter 25 is best understood as a theological blueprint rather than a description of a real object.
