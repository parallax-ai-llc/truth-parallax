## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter describes a ritual ratifying the covenant and a vision of God by the elders. It cannot be verified, and critics regard it as a composite of at least two traditions. Its ritual elements, such as standing stones and covenant meals, are real ancient practices, but they do not anchor the event in time.

## What the Text Claims

- Moses writes down "all the words of YHWH," builds an altar and sets up **twelve pillars** for the twelve tribes (24:3–4).
- Young men offer sacrifices; Moses throws half the blood on the altar and half on the people: "the blood of the covenant" (24:5–8).
- Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu and **seventy elders** see the God of Israel, with "a pavement of sapphire stone" under his feet, and they eat and drink (24:9–11).
- Moses goes up to receive the stone tablets and stays **forty days and forty nights**; the glory appears as fire on the mountain (24:12–18).

## External Evidence

### Standing stones

Standing stones (*masseboth*) were common in Canaanite and Israelite cult. Examples include the Late Bronze stelae shrine at **Hazor** excavated by Yigael Yadin, and the stone or stones in the Iron Age shrine at **Arad**. Exodus 24:4 treats them positively, whereas Deuteronomy 16:22 forbids them. This suggests the tradition is older than Deuteronomy's reform.

### Covenant rites

Ancient treaties were often confirmed by sacrifice and a shared meal. The blood rite, dividing blood between the altar and the people, has no exact parallel, but blood and slaughter rituals sealed agreements elsewhere (compare Gen 15 and the Aramaic Sefire treaties of the 8th century BCE, which use animal-cutting curses).

### "Sapphire" pavement

The Hebrew *sappir* probably means lapis lazuli, a deep blue stone imported from Afghanistan and used across the Near East for divine imagery and temple decoration. The image of the deity above a blue floor resembles Ezekiel's vision (Ezek 1:26).

## Points of Agreement

- **Authentic ritual vocabulary.** Altars with standing stones, sacrifice and communal meals before a deity are well attested in the Levant.
- **Pre-Deuteronomic elements.** The approving use of pillars points to an older layer of tradition.

## Points of Conflict

- **Two endings.** Verses 1–2 and 9–11 (the elders' vision and meal) and verses 3–8 (the blood ritual) appear to be separate traditions joined together.
- **Covenant theology.** Lothar Perlitt argued that the idea of a written covenant document at Sinai belongs to the Deuteronomistic era (7th–6th centuries BCE).
- **Forty days.** A conventional round number used throughout the Bible.

## Assessment

The chapter preserves old cultic material, especially the twelve standing stones and the elders' meal before God, combined with later covenant theology. It shows how Israel imagined the founding of its covenant, using real ritual forms, but it gives no datable historical event.
