## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The departure from Egypt has no confirmation outside the Bible, and the numbers given are impossible for the Late Bronze Age. The chapter is valuable mainly as evidence for the origin and history of the Passover festival, which is old and well attested later.

## What the Text Claims

- God institutes a lamb sacrifice with blood on the doorposts, and the festival of unleavened bread (12:1–28).
- At midnight YHWH kills all the firstborn of Egypt (12:29–30).
- The Israelites leave, carrying unleavened dough and Egyptian silver and gold (12:31–36).
- They travel from **Raamses to Succoth**, about **600,000 men on foot**, besides children, with a "mixed multitude" (12:37–38).
- Israel lived in Egypt **430 years** (12:40–41).

## External Evidence

### The origin of Passover

Most scholars think Passover combines two older rites:

- **Pesach**, an apotropaic (protective) sacrifice of a lamb at night with blood smeared to ward off a destroyer, probably from pastoral life in spring.
- **Massot**, an agricultural festival of unleavened bread at the start of the barley harvest.

The exodus story gave both rites a historical meaning. The festival is attested outside the Bible in the **Elephantine papyri**: a letter of 419 BCE (often called the "Passover Papyrus") from Hananiah to the Jewish garrison in Upper Egypt gives instructions about the festival of unleavened bread. The Elephantine community may also refer to *pesach* in ostraca.

### 600,000 men

With women and children, 600,000 men implies **two to three million** people. Egypt's whole population in the New Kingdom is usually estimated at roughly 3–4.5 million. The Late Bronze Age population of Canaan has been estimated at under 100,000 (Broshi and Gophna). Such a crowd would form a column hundreds of kilometres long and could not survive in Sinai.

- George Mendenhall (1958) proposed that *'elef* originally meant a clan or military unit, not "thousand," reducing the total to a few thousand.
- Colin Humphreys (1998) offered a mathematical reconstruction producing about 20,000 people.
- Critical scholars generally see the figure as a later, perhaps symbolic, number. It matches the census totals in Numbers 1 and 26 (603,550 and 601,730), which may reflect monarchic-era population figures or gematria.

### Raamses and Succoth

Raamses is Pi-Ramesses (Qantir). Succoth is often identified with Egyptian *Tjeku*, the region of the Wadi Tumilat, and its centre at **Tell el-Maskhuta**. As noted for chapter 1, that site was largely unoccupied in the Late Bronze Age.

### 430 years

The Masoretic text gives 430 years in Egypt. The **Septuagint** and **Samaritan Pentateuch** read 430 years "in Egypt and in Canaan," halving the stay in Egypt. Genesis 15:13 gives 400 years, and the genealogy of 6:16–20 allows only four generations. The variation shows that the chronology was schematic and was adjusted by later scribes.

## Points of Agreement

- Passover and unleavened bread are ancient Israelite festivals, attested in Persian-period Egypt.
- Pi-Ramesses is a real starting point in the eastern Delta.
- A "mixed multitude" (12:38) fits the reality that groups leaving Egypt would include people of various origins.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 12 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| 600,000 men plus families | Demographically impossible; exceeds estimates for Egypt's and Canaan's populations |
| 430 years in Egypt | Textual variants and internal chronology disagree |
| Departure via Succoth (Tell el-Maskhuta) | Site not occupied in the Late Bronze Age |
| Mass departure of slaves | No Egyptian record; no trace in Sinai |

## Assessment

Chapter 12 is largely ritual legislation, with Priestly regulations (12:1–20, 43–49) wrapped around a short narrative. Its historical core is the festival, not the departure. The large numbers reflect the writers' belief that all Israel came out of Egypt. Archaeology shows that most Israelites descended from indigenous Canaanites, so many scholars hold that only a small group, if any, experienced an exodus. The story became the shared memory of all Israel through the festival celebrated each year. Conservative scholars such as Kitchen accept a real exodus but generally concede that the numbers need reinterpreting.
