## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The picture of Semitic people doing forced labour in the eastern Delta is plausible, and the city names are real. But no Egyptian source mentions Israelites, a policy of killing Hebrew boys, or a king who feared their numbers, and the store-city names point to different periods.

## What the Text Claims

- Jacob's descendants, seventy persons, multiply until "the land was filled with them" (1:1–7).
- A "new king, who did not know Joseph" fears them (1:8–10).
- Taskmasters force them to build the store cities **Pithom and Raamses** (1:11).
- They are made to work with mortar, brick, and field labour (1:13–14).
- The king orders the Hebrew midwives **Shiphrah and Puah** to kill newborn boys, then orders all boys thrown into the Nile (1:15–22).

## External Evidence

### Raamses: Pi-Ramesses at Qantir

"Raamses" matches **Pi-Ramesses** ("House of Ramesses"), the Delta capital founded by Ramesses II (c. 1279–1213 BCE). Excavations at **Qantir**, led by Edgar Pusch, have exposed palaces, workshops, and chariot stables. The city was abandoned by about 1130 BCE when its branch of the Nile silted up, and its monuments were carried to Tanis. The name therefore fits the 13th–12th centuries BCE well. It also clashes with the 15th-century date implied by 1 Kings 6:1, unless the name is an update by a later scribe, as some conservative scholars suggest.

### Pithom: Tell el-Maskhuta or Tell el-Retaba

"Pithom" is Egyptian *Pr-Itm*, "House of Atum." Two sites in the Wadi Tumilat compete for it.

- **Tell el-Maskhuta** was excavated by John S. Holladay in the 1970s. It had a Hyksos-period settlement and then no substantial occupation until about 610 BCE, under Necho II. Inscriptions naming Pithom have been found there, but they are later. Redford argues that the biblical writer knew the place as it was in the 7th–6th centuries BCE.
- **Tell el-Retaba**, a few kilometres west, was a Ramesside fortress. Hoffmeier and Kitchen favour it as the Pithom of Ramesses II's time.

### Semitic labour and slaves

- **Brooklyn Papyrus 35.1446** (late Middle Kingdom, c. 1750 BCE) lists household servants of a Theban estate, many with West Semitic names. W. F. Albright noted that one is *Shiphrah*, the same name as the midwife in 1:15.
- **Leiden Papyrus 348** (time of Ramesses II) instructs an official to give grain to "the 'Apiru who are dragging stone" to a building project. 'Apiru were a social class of uprooted people, not an ethnic group, and most scholars now reject an equation with "Hebrews," though the word may be related.

## Points of Agreement

- Asiatic people did live and work in the eastern Delta in large numbers, both as free migrants and as captives.
- Pi-Ramesses and a Ramesside building programme in the Delta are real.
- The midwives' names are genuine Northwest Semitic names of the 2nd millennium BCE.
- Royal building projects in Egypt did use conscripted and foreign labour.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 1 | Historical record |
|----------|-------------------|
| Hebrews build "Raamses" | Pi-Ramesses is 13th-century, conflicting with the 1 Kings 6:1 date of c. 1446 BCE |
| Pithom is a contemporary store city | Tell el-Maskhuta was largely unoccupied from c. 1500 to 610 BCE |
| Seventy people become a nation that fills the land | No Egyptian record of a large Hebrew population |
| A decree to kill all Hebrew boys | Not attested. Such an order would also destroy the labour force the king wants |
| The unnamed "Pharaoh" | The king is never named, unlike Assyrian and Babylonian kings named elsewhere in the Bible |

## Assessment

Exodus 1 has an authentic Egyptian backdrop: Delta cities, brickmaking, and Semitic labourers. Conservative scholars such as Kitchen see this as evidence of an early tradition rooted in the time of Ramesses II. Critical scholars note that the place names can be explained as the knowledge of a later writer, since Pi-Ramesses remained famous and Pithom was a living town in the 7th–6th centuries BCE. The anonymity of Pharaoh and the folktale features of the midwife story (the clever women who trick the king) suggest legend rather than chronicle. The chapter shows familiarity with Egypt, not proof of Israelite slavery there.
