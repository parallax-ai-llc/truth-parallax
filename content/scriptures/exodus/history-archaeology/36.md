## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (cultic narrative).** The chapter repeats the specifications of chapter 26 as a record of their fulfilment. Its vocabulary includes several Egyptian loanwords, which conservative scholars cite as evidence of an early Egyptian background. Critical scholars respond that such loanwords entered Hebrew over many centuries and do not fix a date.

## What the Text Claims

- The people bring so much material that Moses has to forbid further offerings (36:2–7).
- The craftsmen make the ten linen curtains with woven cherubim, the goat-hair curtains and the skin coverings (36:8–19).
- They build the acacia frames with silver bases, the bars, the veil and the entrance screen (36:20–38).

## External Evidence

### Egyptian loanwords

Several key terms in the tabernacle account are widely recognised as Egyptian in origin:

- *shesh*, "fine linen" (36:8), from Egyptian *šs*;
- *shittim*, "acacia" (36:20), usually derived from Egyptian *šnḏt*.

Conservative scholars such as James Hoffmeier and K. A. Kitchen argue that this cluster points to a tradition formed in contact with Egypt. Critics note that Egyptian words were borrowed into Hebrew throughout the monarchy, and that linen and acacia were standard trade terms. The loanwords show Egyptian cultural influence, but they cannot date the text.

### Weaving technology

Egypt produced very fine linen and, by the 2nd millennium BCE, tapestry-woven textiles with figured designs, including examples from the tomb of Tutankhamun. Woven figures like the cherubim of 36:8 were technically possible in the Late Bronze Age, as well as later.

### The surplus offering

The motif of a people giving more than enough for a sanctuary appears in royal building inscriptions and in the Chronicler's account of gifts for the Temple (1 Chr 29:6–9). It is a literary theme of ideal piety.

## Points of Agreement

- **Egyptian cultural background.** The vocabulary and techniques fit Egyptian craft traditions, which shaped Levantine culture in both the Late Bronze and Iron Ages.
- **Plausible construction methods.** Frames, tenons, sockets and bars describe real joinery.

## Points of Conflict

- The quantity of fine linen, dyed wool and silver (100 talents for the bases, 38:27) far exceeds what a desert population could produce or carry.
- The chapter repeats chapter 26 almost verbatim; its function is literary and theological rather than documentary.

## Assessment

Chapter 36 adds little new historical information beyond the account of the instructions. Its Egyptian loanwords are a genuine and interesting feature. Read cautiously, they show that the tabernacle tradition drew on Egyptian-influenced craft vocabulary, not that it was written in the Late Bronze Age.
