## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (cultic instruction).** The chapter adds several items to the tabernacle plan. Incense altars are well attested in Iron Age Israel, but the ingredients of the incense and anointing oil depend on long-distance trade with Arabia and South Asia, and the half-shekel tax corresponds to Second Temple practice.

## What the Text Claims

- A gold-plated **incense altar** with horns stands before the veil; incense is burned on it morning and evening (30:1–10).
- In a census, every man aged twenty and over pays a **half-shekel** "ransom" so that no plague strikes (30:11–16).
- A bronze **laver** is placed for priests to wash (30:17–21).
- A holy **anointing oil** of myrrh, cinnamon, aromatic cane and cassia, and an **incense** of stacte, onycha, galbanum and frankincense (30:22–38).

## External Evidence

### Incense altars

Small limestone altars, often horned, are common in Iron Age II sites such as Megiddo and Tel Miqne-Ekron. At the Judahite shrine of **Arad**, two limestone altars stood at the entrance to the inner niche. A 2020 analysis of residues found frankincense on the larger altar and cannabis on the smaller. This confirms that incense was burned in Judahite sanctuaries in the 8th century BCE.

### Ingredients

Frankincense and myrrh come from South Arabia and the Horn of Africa. Cinnamon and cassia ultimately come from South and Southeast Asia. Large-scale caravan trade in incense across Arabia, made possible by the domesticated camel, flourished from roughly the 8th century BCE. The recipe points to a time of established long-distance trade.

### The half-shekel

The shekel here is a weight of silver, not a coin; coinage only began in the 6th century BCE. Nehemiah 10:32 records a Temple tax of a **third** of a shekel; by the late Second Temple period the tax was a half-shekel (Matt 17:24; Josephus). The danger of a census recalls David's census in 2 Samuel 24.

## Points of Agreement

- **Incense in Israelite worship** is now archaeologically certain.
- **Washing basins** are known from temples; Solomon's Temple had bronze stands and a large "sea" (1 Kgs 7:23–39).

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 30 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Incense altar part of the original plan | It is absent from the furniture lists of chs. 25–27 and appears after the closing words of ch. 29 |
| Exotic spices in the wilderness | Depend on the Arabian and Indian Ocean trade of the 1st millennium |
| Half-shekel tax from Sinai | Matches a Second Temple institution |

## Assessment

Since Wellhausen, many critics have regarded this chapter as a supplement to the original Priestly plan, because it introduces the incense altar only after chapter 29 has concluded the instructions. The Arad residues show that incense cult is old in Judah, but the specific provisions here, especially the tax, reflect the later Temple community.
