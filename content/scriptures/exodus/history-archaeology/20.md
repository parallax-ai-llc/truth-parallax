## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (law and covenant text).** The chapter is a legal and theological composition, not a narrative of datable events. The historical questions are when the Decalogue took shape and what ancient models its covenant form follows. Most critical scholars place its present form in the late monarchy or exile.

## What the Text Claims

- God speaks the **Ten Words** directly to the people (20:1–17), beginning with the self-introduction "I am YHWH your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt."
- The Sabbath command is grounded in **creation**: God rested on the seventh day (20:11).
- The terrified people ask Moses to mediate (20:18–21).
- An **altar law** permits altars of earth or unhewn stone "in every place where I cause my name to be remembered," without steps (20:22–26).

## External Evidence

### Treaty form

In 1954–55 George Mendenhall argued that the Sinai covenant follows the structure of **Hittite vassal treaties** of the 14th–13th centuries BCE: a self-identification of the great king, a historical prologue recounting his favours, stipulations, and so on. Exodus 20:2 reads like such a prologue. Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that this match with Late Bronze treaties, rather than later ones, supports a 2nd-millennium date. Later research (D. J. McCarthy; Lothar Perlitt; Moshe Weinfeld) pointed out that the full treaty pattern is clearest in **Deuteronomy**, which closely echoes the **Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties of Esarhaddon** (672 BCE). Many now see covenant theology as a 7th-century development, read back into Sinai.

### Two versions

Deuteronomy 5:6–21 repeats the Decalogue with significant differences. Most strikingly, the Sabbath there is grounded in the memory of slavery in Egypt, not in creation. Exodus 20:11 echoes the Priestly creation account (Gen 2:2–3), suggesting a late revision.

### Aniconism

The ban on images is not borne out by Israel's material culture for much of the Iron Age: cult stands, figurines and the Kuntillet ʿAjrud drawings show that strict imageless worship was not universal. Tryggve Mettinger argued for an early "de facto" aniconism (standing stones, empty throne) that hardened into a programmatic ban later.

## Points of Agreement

- **Ancient Near Eastern form.** The self-presentation of a sovereign followed by obligations is a genuine ancient genre.
- **Simple altars.** Earth and fieldstone altars like those in 20:24–25 are known from Iron Age sites, and the altar law presupposes many local sanctuaries, an older practice than Deuteronomy's single sanctuary.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 20 | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Decalogue given once, verbatim | Two differing versions (Exod 20; Deut 5) |
| Imageless worship from the start | Iron Age figurines, cult images and drawings |
| Treaty form proves Late Bronze date | Closest parallels in form and phrasing are Neo-Assyrian |

## Assessment

The Decalogue is best read as a distillation of Israelite ethics and cult, probably shaped in stages, with its present wording no earlier than the late monarchy. The altar law preserves an older layer that Deuteronomy later restricted. The treaty comparisons show that the covenant idea belongs to a real ancient political vocabulary, but they do not settle its date.
