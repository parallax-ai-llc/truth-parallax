## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Nothing in the chapter can be verified directly. Yet the striking claim that Moses' father-in-law was a Midianite priest who presided over worship of YHWH is the kind of detail later writers were unlikely to invent, and it supports the widely held view that YHWH worship had roots among the peoples of the south.

## What the Text Claims

- **Jethro, priest of Midian**, brings Zipporah and her sons to Moses at "the mountain of God" (18:1–5).
- Jethro blesses YHWH, declares him "greater than all gods," and offers sacrifices; Aaron and the elders eat bread with him "before God" (18:10–12).
- Jethro advises Moses to appoint officials over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens to judge minor cases (18:13–26).

## External Evidence

### Midian

Biblical and classical sources locate Midian in northwestern Arabia, east of the Gulf of Aqaba. Surveys and excavations at **Qurayyah** in the Hejaz identified a distinctive painted pottery ("Qurayyah ware," often called "Midianite ware") of roughly the 13th–12th centuries BCE. The same pottery turns up at **Timna** in the Arabah, where Egyptian copper mining was active.

### The Timna shrine

At Timna, Beno Rothenberg excavated an Egyptian shrine to Hathor. In a later phase, after the Egyptians left (12th century BCE), the shrine was reused: Hathor images were defaced, remains of red and yellow cloth suggest a tent or awning over the shrine, Midianite pottery appears, and a small copper snake was found. Rothenberg interpreted this as a Midianite tent shrine. The reading is suggestive rather than certain, but it gives a real archaeological context for Midianites in the region.

### The Kenite-Midianite hypothesis

Several biblical poems locate YHWH's home in the south: he "came from Sinai, dawned from Seir, shone from Mount Paran" (Deut 33:2; compare Judg 5:4–5; Hab 3:3). Moses' father-in-law is also called a Kenite (Judg 1:16). Since the 19th century this has supported the theory that Israel adopted YHWH from Midianites or Kenites. Egyptian lists of Amenhotep III at Soleb (14th century BCE) mention a "land of the Shasu of *Yhw*," apparently in the same southern region.

## Points of Agreement

- **Midianite presence** in the southern Levant and Arabah in the 13th–12th centuries is archaeologically real.
- **A southern origin for YHWH** is supported by biblical poetry and the Shasu lists, and fits a Midianite priest blessing YHWH.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 18 | Issue |
|-----------|-------|
| Jethro (18:1) | Also called Reuel (2:18) and linked with Hobab (Num 10:29; Judg 4:11), a sign of merged traditions |
| Visit placed before Sinai | Deuteronomy 1:9–18 sets the appointment of judges after Horeb and omits Jethro |
| Officers over "thousands" | A military-administrative hierarchy suited to a monarchic state |

## Assessment

Chapter 18 has been shaped by later concerns, especially the organisation of courts, which resembles the reform credited to Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 19:5–11). But the core memory of a positive bond with a Midianite priest sits awkwardly with later hostility to Midian (Num 25; 31) and is therefore often taken as old. It does not prove a historical Moses, but it is one of the stronger threads linking Israel's god to the desert south.
