## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable for the theophany; moderate interest for the setting.** A divine encounter cannot be tested historically. But the chapter's location of God's mountain in the region of Midian connects with Egyptian evidence for a place called "YHW" among southern nomads, which is one of the most discussed clues to the origins of Israel's god.

## What the Text Claims

- Moses tends the flock of **Jethro, priest of Midian**, and comes to **Horeb, the mountain of God** (3:1).
- God appears in a bush that burns without being consumed (3:2–6).
- God sends Moses to bring Israel out of Egypt to a land of "the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Amorites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (3:7–10, 17).
- God reveals his name: "I am who I am" (*ehyeh asher ehyeh*) and **YHWH** (3:13–15).
- The Israelites will ask Pharaoh for a three-day journey to sacrifice in the wilderness and will plunder the Egyptians (3:18–22).

## External Evidence

### "The land of the Shasu of YHW"

Egyptian topographical lists name groups of **Shasu**, pastoral nomads of the southern Levant and northwest Arabia.

- A list of **Amenhotep III** (c. 1390–1352 BCE) in his temple at **Soleb** in Nubia includes "the land of the Shasu of *Yhw*."
- A later copy in a list of **Ramesses II** at **Amarah-West** repeats it, alongside "the land of the Shasu of Seir."

Most scholars read *Yhw* as a place name, perhaps a mountain or district in Edom or Midian, and many think it is linked to the divine name YHWH. Raphael Giveon collected the Shasu texts. This is the oldest possible occurrence of the name, and it places it in the very region where Exodus 3 puts the mountain of God.

### The Midianite-Kenite hypothesis

Several biblical clues point the same way: Moses meets God while serving a Midianite priest; Jethro later offers sacrifices and advises Moses (ch. 18); the Kenites, a clan linked to Moses' in-laws, are friendly to Israel; and archaic poems bring YHWH from the south (Judg 5:4–5; Deut 33:2; Hab 3:3, "God came from Teman"). Inscriptions at **Kuntillet 'Ajrud** in northern Sinai (c. 800 BCE) mention "YHWH of Teman." Many scholars conclude that YHWH was first worshipped by southern pastoral groups and was brought into Canaan later.

### Where was Sinai or Horeb?

The traditional site, Jebel Musa in southern Sinai, is first attested in Byzantine Christian tradition (4th century CE). Scholars have proposed sites in northern Sinai, the Negev, and northwest Arabia (Midian). None has produced archaeological evidence of the events.

### The divine name in Exodus 3 and 6

Exodus 3 (usually assigned to the non-Priestly or Elohist strand) and Exodus 6:2–3 (Priestly) both present the name YHWH as newly revealed to Moses. Yet Genesis (the Yahwist strand) uses YHWH from the start (Gen 4:26). This double introduction is one of the classic arguments for multiple sources in the Pentateuch.

## Points of Agreement

- Egyptian texts confirm that nomadic Shasu lived in the regions south and east of Canaan, and one of their territories was called *Yhw*.
- The link between Moses, Midian, and the mountain of God fits the southern origins of YHWH suggested by the oldest biblical poetry and by Kuntillet 'Ajrud.
- The request for a three-day journey to worship in the wilderness is a realistic petition for time off; Egyptian work records note absences of labourers for religious festivals.

## Points of Conflict

| Exodus 3 | Historical assessment |
|----------|----------------------|
| The name YHWH revealed to Moses | Genesis already uses YHWH; the chapter reflects one source's view |
| The mountain of God at Horeb | Location unknown; Jebel Musa is a late Christian identification |
| List of six peoples of Canaan | A stock literary formula; "Hittites" in Canaan reflects later usage for Syrian peoples, not the Anatolian empire |
| Israel's "plundering" of Egypt | No record |

## Assessment

The burning bush itself lies outside historical method. The chapter's historical value is in its geography of religion. Combined with the Soleb and Amarah-West lists, it supports a widely held view that YHWH was originally a god of the southern desert, worshipped by groups such as the Shasu, Midianites, or Kenites. Conservative scholars note that the Egyptian lists do not prove the name refers to Israel's god, and that is correct. The link is a hypothesis, but a strong and much-discussed one.
