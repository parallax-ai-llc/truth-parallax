## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (law code).** The chapter continues the Covenant Code with laws on theft, damage, deposits, sexual offences, and protection of the poor. It records legal norms, not events. Its parallels in cuneiform law and one Hebrew inscription show that it reflects real practice in an agrarian society of the Iron Age.

## What the Text Claims

- A thief who slaughters or sells a stolen ox pays fivefold, a sheep fourfold; a burglar killed at night incurs no bloodguilt, but one killed after sunrise does (22:1–4; Hebrew 21:37–22:3).
- Rules for grazing damage, fire, deposits, and borrowed animals, with disputed cases brought "before God" for an oath (22:5–15).
- A man who seduces an unbetrothed virgin must pay the bride-price (22:16–17).
- Sorceresses, bestiality and sacrifice to other gods are capital offences (22:18–20).
- Protection for the stranger, widow and orphan; no interest from the poor; a garment taken as pledge must be returned by sunset (22:21–27).

## External Evidence

### Cuneiform parallels

- **Theft:** Hammurabi §8 requires thirtyfold or tenfold restitution for stolen animals, depending on the owner's status. Exodus has a similar multiple-restitution logic with lower rates.
- **The night burglar:** Laws of Eshnunna §§12–13 distinguish between an intruder caught by day (fined) and by night (may be killed), exactly the distinction in 22:2–3.
- **Deposits and herding:** Hammurabi §§120–126 and §§263–267 cover goods lost in storage and animals lost to a lion in the fold, settled by oath before a god, matching 22:7–13.
- **Seduction:** Middle Assyrian Laws A §§55–56 deal with the violation of an unbetrothed girl and payment to her father.
- **Bestiality:** Hittite Laws §§187–200 list forbidden and permitted unions with animals.

### The Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon

A late 7th-century BCE Hebrew ostracon found at Mesad Hashavyahu (near Yavneh-Yam) records a harvest worker's petition to an official: his garment had been taken, and he asks for it to be returned. The text does not quote Exodus, but it shows that seizure of a poor man's cloak was a live issue in Judah, as in 22:26–27 (compare Deut 24:12–13; Amos 2:8).

## Points of Agreement

- **Real legal practice.** The laws match known Near Eastern legal categories and procedures, including oaths "before God" (*elohim*) in disputed cases.
- **Agrarian setting.** Fields, vineyards, threshing floors and stored grain (22:5–6) fit Iron Age village life.

## Points of Conflict

- The setting at Sinai conflicts with the content: a people living on manna in tents would not need laws on burning neighbours' standing grain.
- The humanitarian clauses about the stranger, widow and orphan (22:21–24) have a hortatory style that many scholars attribute to later, Deuteronomistic-style expansion.

## Assessment

Chapter 22 is a well-preserved example of ancient Near Eastern case law adapted to Israelite village society. Its closest parallels are with Eshnunna and Hammurabi, and the ostracon shows that its concerns were real in 7th-century Judah. It is good evidence for social history, but not for a wilderness setting.
