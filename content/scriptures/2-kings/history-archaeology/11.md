## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** A queen mother of Omride descent seizing power in Jerusalem after her son's death fits the political situation of 841 BCE. The account of the counter-coup reads like a detailed court record. But no extra-biblical source mentions Athaliah or Joash's coronation, and the story of the hidden infant heir follows a well-known legitimation pattern.

## What the Text Claims

- After Ahaziah's death, his mother **Athaliah** kills the royal family and rules for six years (11:1–3).
- Ahaziah's sister Jehosheba hides the infant **Joash** in the temple (11:2–3).
- In the seventh year the priest **Jehoiada** organizes the **Carites** and the guards and presents Joash in the temple (11:4–12).
- Athaliah cries "Treason!" and is executed at the horses' entrance to the palace (11:13–16).
- Jehoiada makes a covenant between the LORD, the king and the people. The "**people of the land**" destroy the **temple of Baal** and kill its priest Mattan (11:17–20).
- Joash becomes king at seven (11:21).

## External Evidence

No extra-biblical source mentions Athaliah, Jehoiada or Joash of Judah's accession.

### The political context

If the Tel Dan inscription is read as most scholars restore it, Ahaziah of Judah died in 841 BCE in the same upheaval as Joram of Israel (see chapter 9). This left a power vacuum in Jerusalem. A daughter of the Omride house seizing power at that moment, when her family's northern power base had just been wiped out, is politically coherent.

### The Carites

The "Carites" (*kārî*, 11:4, 19) are royal guards. Some scholars connect them with **Carians** from south-western Anatolia, who are well known as mercenaries for Egyptian pharaohs from the 7th century BCE (Herodotus 2.152). If that identification is right, the term may be anachronistic for the 9th century. Others connect it with the Cherethites (Cretans) of David's guard (2 Sam 20:23).

### The temple setting

The narrative assumes the layout of the palace–temple complex in Jerusalem: gates, the "pillar" where kings stood (11:14; compare 23:3), and a horse gate. The Temple Mount cannot be excavated, so these details cannot be checked. Comparable palace–temple complexes are known from Iron Age sites in Syria, such as Tell Tayinat.

## Points of Agreement

- A crisis of succession in Judah in 841 BCE is consistent with the Tel Dan inscription and with Jehu's coup.
- Powerful queen mothers (*gĕbîrâ*) are a recognized feature of Near Eastern monarchies, and Judah's king lists consistently name the king's mother.
- The "people of the land" (*ʿam hāʾāreṣ*), apparently the landed Judean gentry, intervene in royal succession here and again in 21:24 and 23:30. This repeated, specific role suggests a real social institution.

## Points of Conflict

- **The rescued infant** is a classic legitimation motif. A rightful heir is hidden from a murderous usurper and then revealed (compare Moses, and Near Eastern royal legends). Some historians, such as Christoph Levin, suspect that Joash's Davidic descent was disputed and that the story defended it.
- **Destruction of a Baal temple in Jerusalem** duplicates Jehu's action in Samaria (10:18–28). It may be the Deuteronomistic editor's framing.
- **Six-year reign.** Athaliah is the only ruler of Judah without the standard regnal formula. The editor treats her reign as illegitimate.
- **Carites**: possibly anachronistic if Carian.

## Assessment

The chapter has a strong claim to being based on an old Jerusalem source, perhaps from priestly circles, given its focus on temple guards and procedure. The overthrow of a queen of northern descent by a priest-led coalition fits the turbulent aftermath of 841 BCE. The hidden heir and the paired Baal-temple purge are the elements most likely shaped by later legitimation and theology. Without outside evidence, the account remains plausible but unverified.
