## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High.** The **Babylonian Chronicle** independently confirms the key events of 2 Kings 24:10–17. These are Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Jerusalem, its capture in March 597 BCE, the seizure of its king, the appointment of a successor and the removal of heavy tribute to Babylon. Jehoiakim's earlier rebellion also fits Babylonian setbacks recorded in the Chronicle.

## What the Text Claims

- **Jehoiakim** becomes Nebuchadnezzar's vassal for three years, then rebels (24:1).
- Bands of Chaldeans, Arameans, Moabites and Ammonites raid Judah (24:2).
- The king of Egypt no longer comes out of his land, because Babylon has taken everything from the Wadi of Egypt to the Euphrates (24:7).
- **Jehoiachin** reigns three months. Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, and Jehoiachin surrenders in Nebuchadnezzar's **8th year** (24:8–12).
- Temple and palace treasures are taken. **10,000** captives, including craftsmen and smiths, are deported, or **7,000** fighting men and **1,000** craftsmen (24:13–16).
- Nebuchadnezzar makes **Mattaniah** king, renaming him **Zedekiah**. Zedekiah later rebels (24:17–20).

## External Evidence

### The Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5)

This chronicle of Nebuchadnezzar's early years records, in paraphrase:

- **605 BCE**: Nebuchadnezzar defeats the Egyptian army at **Carchemish** and conquers "all of Hatti."
- **604**: the kings of Hatti bring tribute. **Ashkelon** is captured and destroyed. Excavations by Lawrence Stager found a dramatic destruction layer of this date.
- **601**: Nebuchadnezzar fights the king of Egypt. Both sides suffer heavy losses, and in the following year he stays home to refit his chariots and horses.
- **Year 7 (598/597)**: in Kislev he marches to Hatti and lays siege to "the city of **Judah** (*Yahudu*)." On the **2nd of Adar** (16 March 597 BCE) he seizes the city and **captures the king**. He appoints "a king of his own choice" and takes heavy tribute to Babylon.

### Lachish and the Egyptian question

**Lachish Letter 3**, from the final years of Judah, reports that the army commander **Coniah son of Elnathan** went down to Egypt. It shows that Judah continued to look to Egypt during Zedekiah's reign.

### Exiles in Babylonia

The **Al-Yahudu tablets** (published 2014) are cuneiform legal and economic texts dating from 572 BCE onward. They come from a settlement in Babylonia called "the town of Judah," where deportees with Yahwistic names lived and worked the land. They confirm that Judean deportees were settled as communities.

## Points of Agreement

| Topic | 2 Kings 24 | Babylonian Chronicle |
|-------|-----------|----------------------|
| Siege of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar | Yes | Yes |
| King captured | Jehoiachin surrenders and is taken | "Seized the king" |
| Replacement king | Mattaniah/Zedekiah, chosen by Nebuchadnezzar | "A king of his own choice" |
| Treasure carried off | Temple and palace treasures | "Heavy tribute" |
| Jehoiakim's rebellion | After three years of vassalage | Plausible after the costly battle of 601 |
| Egypt stays home (24:7) | Yes | Egypt's power ends at Carchemish (605) |

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 24 | Evidence or problem |
|------------|---------------------|
| Capture in Nebuchadnezzar's **8th** year (24:12) | Chronicle: **7th** year. Jeremiah 52:28 also says the 7th. The difference probably reflects how accession years were counted |
| 10,000 deportees (24:14) vs 8,000 (24:16) | Jeremiah 52:28 gives 3,023. Probably different ways of counting (men only, or households) |
| Jehoiakim "slept with his fathers" (24:6) | 2 Chronicles 36:6 has him bound for Babylon. Jeremiah 22:19 predicts a donkey's burial |

## Assessment

2 Kings 24 rests on accurate knowledge of the events of 605–597 BCE. The exact correspondence with the Babylonian Chronicle on the capture of the city and the replacement of its king is one of the strongest points of contact between the Bible and a foreign source. The theological explanation in 24:3–4 (Manasseh's sins) is the editor's. The narrative facts, deportation of the elite and craftsmen and the installation of Zedekiah, are well grounded. The varying figures for the deportees show that several sources were combined.
