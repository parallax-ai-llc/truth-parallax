## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter is a cluster of prophetic legends: a chariot of fire, the parting of the Jordan, the healing of a spring, and bears that maul forty-two boys. These episodes are not open to historical testing. The geography (Gilgal, Bethel, Jericho, the Jordan) is real and fits the region, and the "sons of the prophets" may reflect genuine prophetic guilds.

## What the Text Claims

- Elijah and Elisha travel from Gilgal to **Bethel** to **Jericho**, meeting groups of "sons of the prophets" at each place (2:1–7).
- Elijah parts the Jordan with his mantle and is taken up in a whirlwind with a **chariot and horses of fire** (2:8–12).
- Elisha takes the mantle, parts the Jordan again, and is recognized as Elijah's successor (2:13–18).
- Elisha heals Jericho's bad water with salt (2:19–22).
- Boys at Bethel mock him as "baldhead"; he curses them, and two she-bears maul 42 of them (2:23–25).

## External Evidence

No extra-biblical source mentions Elijah or Elisha.

### Jericho

Tell es-Sultan was largely unoccupied in the Late Bronze Age. It was resettled during Iron II. 1 Kings 16:34 places a refounding under Ahab. Iron II remains on the mound are modest and much eroded, and the more substantial settlement dates to the 8th–7th centuries BCE. The perennial spring at the foot of the tell, **Ein es-Sultan**, is still called "Elisha's Spring" in local tradition. That name derives from this story and is not independent evidence.

### Bethel

Bethel (usually identified with Beitin) was a major northern sanctuary, set up as a royal shrine according to 1 Kgs 12:29. The site was excavated in the 1930s and 1950s. The results were poorly published and their dating is debated. A recent re-evaluation by Finkelstein and Singer-Avitz argues for significant Iron IIB occupation in the 8th century BCE.

### Prophetic groups

Groups of ecstatic or professional prophets are attested elsewhere in the ancient Near East. The **Mari** letters (18th century BCE) mention prophetic figures (*muḫḫûm*, *āpilum*), and Neo-Assyrian oracle collections preserve prophecies to Esarhaddon. These sources show that prophets attached to shrines and courts were a real social phenomenon. They say nothing about the persons in this chapter.

## Points of Agreement

- The route from the Jordan valley up to Bethel follows real roads and sites.
- Jericho was a small, inhabited town in the Omride period, as the narrative assumes.
- Bands of prophets associated with sanctuaries fit what is known of Near Eastern prophecy.

## Points of Conflict

- **Bodily ascent to heaven** and the parting of the Jordan are miracle claims. They deliberately echo Moses and Joshua at the same river (Exod 14; Josh 3), which marks them as literary typology.
- **The bear story** is a moralizing legend about respect for a holy man. Its numbers (two bears, 42 boys) are formulaic. The number 42 appears again in 10:14.
- **Elijah's ascent and his later letter.** 2 Chronicles 21:12–15 has Elijah send a letter to Jehoram of Judah, who on the chronology of Kings reigned after Elijah's ascent. The sources do not agree on when Elijah left the scene.

## Assessment

2 Kings 2 is the hinge of the Elijah–Elisha cycle. It transfers prophetic authority from master to disciple through the mantle and a "double portion" of spirit. Form critics treat the episodes as legends told within prophetic circles to honour their founders. They were probably handed down orally before being incorporated into the Deuteronomistic History. The chapter shows how such stories attached to well-known places, Jericho's spring and Bethel's shrine. It does not record events that historians can verify.
