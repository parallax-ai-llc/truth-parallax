## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The healing miracle and the shadow moving back on the steps of Ahaz cannot be tested. The Babylonian embassy, however, fits the career of **Merodach-baladan II**, an enemy of Assyria. The note that Hezekiah "made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city" (20:20) is matched by one of the most impressive monuments of ancient Jerusalem, the **Siloam tunnel** with its inscription.

## What the Text Claims

- Hezekiah falls mortally ill. Isaiah announces his death, then, after Hezekiah's prayer, fifteen more years and deliverance from Assyria (20:1–6).
- A poultice of figs heals him. As a sign, the shadow goes back ten steps on the **steps of Ahaz** (20:7–11).
- **Merodach-baladan son of Baladan, king of Babylon**, sends envoys and a gift. Hezekiah shows them all his treasures (20:12–13).
- Isaiah predicts that everything will be carried off to **Babylon** and Hezekiah's descendants will serve in Babylon's palace (20:16–18).
- Hezekiah's other deeds include "the pool and the **conduit**" that brought water into the city (20:20).

## External Evidence

### Merodach-baladan II

**Marduk-apla-iddina II**, a Chaldean chief, seized the throne of Babylon in 722/721 BCE and held it until Sargon II expelled him in 710. He briefly regained it in 703 before Sennacherib drove him out. Assyrian and Babylonian sources portray him as Assyria's most persistent enemy, who tried to build alliances. An embassy to Judah, either around 714–711 or just before 701, fits an attempt to recruit Hezekiah into an anti-Assyrian front.

### The Siloam tunnel and inscription

- A winding tunnel about **533 metres** long carries water from the **Gihon spring** to the **Siloam pool** at the south-western end of the City of David.
- The **Siloam inscription**, found in 1880 near the tunnel's outlet and now in Istanbul, describes how two teams cut from both ends. They heard each other's voices through the rock and struck "axe against axe." It says the water flowed **1,200 cubits** and that the rock above was **100 cubits** high (in paraphrase). It names no king.
- **Radiometric dating** (uranium–thorium on stalactites and carbon-14 on plant matter in the plaster), published in 2003 by Frumkin, Shimron and Rosenbaum, places the tunnel around **700 BCE**. Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron have argued for a somewhat earlier date, in the 8th century.
- 2 Chronicles 32:30 states that Hezekiah "closed the upper outlet of the waters of Gihon and directed them down to the west side of the city of David."

### Expansion of Jerusalem

Nahman Avigad's excavations in the Jewish Quarter uncovered the **Broad Wall**, about 7 metres thick, built in the late 8th century across the Western Hill. It cut through earlier houses, a detail recalled in Isaiah 22:10 ("you broke down the houses to fortify the wall"). Jerusalem grew several-fold in the late 8th century, probably absorbing refugees from the north and from the Shephelah.

## Points of Agreement

- A Babylonian king of the right name and period sought allies against Assyria.
- Hezekiah carried out major waterworks to secure Jerusalem's water. The Siloam tunnel, dated to around 700, matches 20:20 and 2 Chr 32:30 closely.
- Jerusalem was fortified and expanded in his reign (Broad Wall).
- A fig poultice reflects real ancient medical practice. Figs are used in Ugaritic veterinary texts.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 20 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| The shadow reverses ten steps | Miracle claim. The "steps of Ahaz" may have been a stairway or sundial, but no such object has been found |
| Embassy placed after 701 in the narrative | It must date before 701 ("in those days"). After paying Sennacherib, Hezekiah would hardly have had treasures to show |
| Prophecy of Babylonian exile | Widely regarded as written after 597/586 (*vaticinium ex eventu*) |

## Assessment

The chapter joins a healing legend to two historical memories: a diplomatic contact with Babylon's anti-Assyrian king and Hezekiah's great water tunnel. The Siloam tunnel is the strongest archaeological confirmation of a specific royal building project in the Hebrew Bible. The inscription is a vivid first-hand account by the builders, but it does not name the king. Both the fifteen-year extension (which brings Hezekiah to 29 years, 18:2) and the prediction of exile show that the episode has been shaped to fit the larger narrative.
