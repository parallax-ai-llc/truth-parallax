## Overview

This page does not read 2 Kings as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, non-biblical texts, and independent historical sources say about the events 2 Kings describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

2 Kings covers roughly 850 to 560 BCE: the last century and a half of the northern kingdom of Israel, its destruction by Assyria, and the survival of Judah until the Babylonian conquest of Jerusalem. No other book of the Hebrew Bible can be checked against so many independent sources. Kings, battles and tribute payments named in 2 Kings turn up in Assyrian annals, Babylonian chronicles, Moabite and Aramaic royal inscriptions, Hebrew letters on potsherds, and destruction layers at excavated cities.

The overall verdict of mainstream scholarship is that 2 Kings rests on genuine written sources for its royal names, reign lengths, wars and tribute payments, and that these sources were reworked by a theologically driven editor. Alongside the annal-like material sit prophetic legends, above all the Elisha cycle, which cannot be tested and often read as folklore.

## Dating and Composition

Most scholars treat Joshua through Kings as a single **Deuteronomistic History**, so called because it judges Israel's history by the laws of Deuteronomy. Frank Moore Cross argued for a first edition under Josiah (c. 640–609 BCE), which built to a climax in Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 22–23), and a second edition after 586 BCE that added the fall of Jerusalem and blamed it on Manasseh. Others, following Martin Noth, see one exilic author, or several layers of exilic editing. Thomas Römer combines these views in a model of growth across the late monarchy, exile and Persian period.

All models agree on two points. The book's last event, the release of King Jehoiachin from prison in Babylon (25:27–30), dates to c. 562 BCE, so the book reached its final form after that. And the editor repeatedly sends readers to royal annals for more detail. The accuracy of the regnal data confirms that such records existed and were used.

### The chronology problem

2 Kings dates each king's accession by the regnal year of the king in the other kingdom. Added up, these figures do not fit together, and they disagree in places with the Assyrian dates. These dates are fixed by the eclipse of 763 BCE in the Assyrian eponym lists. Edwin Thiele's conservative reconstruction rescues most figures by assuming co-regencies and different ways of counting years. Critical scholars such as Cogan and Tadmor accept that the numbers contain errors and later adjustments. The anchor points, 841 BCE (Jehu), 722/720 (Samaria), 701 (Sennacherib) and 597 (Jehoiachin), are secure from outside sources.

## The Main Historical Problems

### Prophetic legends versus court records

Chapters 1–8 and 13 are dominated by stories about Elijah and Elisha. They involve fire from heaven, a floating axe head, resurrections and bears. Their geography and politics (Omride Samaria, Aramean raids, Moabite revolt) are realistic, but the stories themselves belong to the genre Alexander Rofé calls prophetic legenda. Several Aramean war stories with an unnamed "king of Israel" may originally have belonged to the later Jehu dynasty and were moved into the Omride period.

### Moab and Mesha (chapter 3)

The **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE) confirms that Omri's dynasty ruled Moab and that King Mesha threw off Israelite control. It disagrees with 2 Kings on timing and outcome. Mesha claims victories and says nothing of the joint Israelite–Judean–Edomite campaign in 2 Kgs 3.

### Jehu's coup (chapters 9–10)

The **Black Obelisk** of Shalmaneser III shows Jehu paying tribute in 841 BCE. The **Tel Dan inscription**, probably set up by Hazael of Damascus, claims that its author killed Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah. 2 Kings says Jehu killed them. This is the clearest case where two ancient sources give rival credit for the same event.

### Assyria and the end of Israel (chapters 15–17)

The annals of **Tiglath-pileser III** name Menahem, Pekah, Hoshea and Ahaz (as Jehoahaz). They confirm the tribute payments, the annexation of Galilee and Gilead, and the installation of Hoshea. For Samaria's fall, the Babylonian Chronicle credits **Shalmaneser V**, while **Sargon II** later claimed the conquest and the deportation of about 27,280 people. 2 Kings's vague "king of Assyria" (17:6) may preserve both phases.

### Hezekiah and Sennacherib (chapters 18–20)

This is the most thoroughly documented episode in the Hebrew Bible. Sennacherib's annals (the **Taylor** and **Oriental Institute Prisms**), the **Lachish reliefs** from his palace at Nineveh, the destruction of **Lachish Level III**, the **Siloam tunnel and inscription**, the **Broad Wall**, the **LMLK storage jars** and a royal **bulla of Hezekiah** all bear on it. Both sources agree that Judah was devastated, that Hezekiah paid heavy tribute, and that Jerusalem was not taken. They disagree on why Sennacherib withdrew. 2 Kings says an angel killed 185,000 Assyrians, a claim with no outside support. Sennacherib's murder by his son (19:37) is confirmed by Babylonian and Assyrian sources, though it happened twenty years later.

### Manasseh and Josiah (chapters 21–23)

Manasseh appears as a loyal Assyrian vassal in the inscriptions of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. That fits a long, stable reign, though not the theological portrait. Josiah is mentioned in no extra-biblical source. His reform is debated archaeologically. The dismantled shrines at Arad and Beersheba may date to Hezekiah instead, and household cult objects such as pillar figurines continue afterwards. His death at Megiddo in 609 BCE fits what the Babylonian Chronicle says about Egyptian movements that year.

### Babylon and the fall of Jerusalem (chapters 24–25)

The **Babylonian Chronicle (ABC 5)** dates Nebuchadnezzar's capture of Jerusalem and of its king to 2 Adar (March) 597 BCE, a direct match with 2 Kgs 24:10–17. The **Lachish ostraca** record the final Babylonian advance. Burn layers in the City of David and the Jewish Quarter of Jerusalem mark the destruction of 587/586. The **Jehoiachin ration tablets** from Babylon list oil rations for "Ya'ukinu, king of the land of Yahudu," which gives an external frame for the book's last scene.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Ancient readers** already compared the accounts. Herodotus (2.141) gives an Egyptian version of Sennacherib's retreat in which field mice gnaw the Assyrians' bowstrings. Josephus harmonized Kings with Berossus and with Chronicles.
- **19th-century discoveries** of Assyrian palaces at Nimrud and Nineveh, the Mesha Stele (1868) and the Siloam inscription (1880) gave the first independent confirmation of names in Kings. They turned the book into a key test case for biblical history.
- **Conservative scholars** such as K. A. Kitchen argue that the tight fit between Kings and Near Eastern records shows it to be a reliable historical work throughout. They harmonize the conflicts (for example, Jehu as Hazael's agent in killing the kings, or a prince Tirhakah in 701).
- **Critical scholars** such as Cogan and Tadmor, Na'aman and Finkelstein agree the annalistic framework is reliable. They stress that it was edited to serve theological ends, with schematic judgments and with legends and reconstructed speeches inserted. They also note the narrative of 701 combines more than one source (the "B1" and "B2" accounts).
- **Minimalist** scholars question how much pre-exilic source material reached the editors. Even they generally accept the 8th–6th century core, because the Assyrian and Babylonian records confirm it.

## Reliability Assessment

2 Kings is the **most historically reliable narrative book of the Hebrew Bible** for the period it covers. Its list of kings, its basic order of events, and many specific details are confirmed by contemporary inscriptions from other nations. These details include tribute amounts, the names of Assyrian officials, and the date of Jerusalem's surrender in 597. It is weakest where it relies on prophetic story collections and where theology overrides events. Examples are the Elisha miracles, the destruction of Sennacherib's army, and the scale of Josiah's reform.

Where it conflicts with outside sources, the conflicts mostly concern credit and explanation: who killed Joram, why Sennacherib left, who took Samaria. The fact that a king reigned, fought or paid tribute is rarely in dispute. The book is best read as a theological interpretation built on a genuine archival backbone.
