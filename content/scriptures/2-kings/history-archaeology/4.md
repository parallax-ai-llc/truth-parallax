## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Four miracle stories: multiplying oil, raising a dead child, purifying poisoned stew, and feeding a hundred men. They are folk legends about a holy man and offer nothing a historian can test. Their value lies in the realistic picture of village life and in the named places, which are real.

## What the Text Claims

- A prophet's widow, threatened with her children's enslavement for debt, fills borrowed jars from a single flask of oil (4:1–7).
- A wealthy woman of **Shunem** builds Elisha a roof chamber. He promises her a son; when the boy dies, Elisha revives him (4:8–37).
- At **Gilgal** during a famine, Elisha makes poisonous stew of wild gourds edible with flour (4:38–41).
- A man from **Baal-shalishah** brings twenty barley loaves; they feed a hundred with some left over (4:42–44).

## External Evidence

No extra-biblical source mentions Elisha or these events.

### Shunem

Shunem (modern Sulam, at the foot of the Hill of Moreh in the Jezreel Valley) is well attested outside the Bible. It appears in the town list of Thutmose III and in an **Amarna letter** (EA 365). In that letter Biridiya of Megiddo complains that he alone sent workers to cultivate fields at Shunama. It also appears in **Sheshonq I's** list of conquered towns at Karnak (late 10th century). The town lay close to the Omride centre at Jezreel, which fits the woman's access to "the king or the commander of the army" (4:13).

### Debt slavery

The creditor coming to take the widow's children (4:1) reflects a real practice. Debt servitude is regulated in Exodus 21:2–11 and Deuteronomy 15:12–18. It also appears in the Laws of Hammurabi (§117, limiting it to three years), and is condemned in Amos 2:6 and Nehemiah 5:1–5.

### Roof chambers and village economy

The "small upper room on the wall" (4:10) matches the two-storey pillared houses common in Iron Age Israel, where living quarters were often upstairs. Barley loaves as first fruits (4:42) and foraging for wild plants in famine fit the agrarian world of the central highlands.

## Points of Agreement

- Shunem, Gilgal and the Jezreel Valley are real places, and the social details are realistic. They include debt, harvest, a prosperous landowning family and prophetic disciples eating communally.
- Bands of prophets living together under a master fit what is known of ancient prophetic groups.

## Points of Conflict

- **The raising of the Shunammite's son** closely duplicates Elijah's revival of the widow's son at Zarephath (1 Kgs 17:17–24). The oil miracle duplicates the jar of flour and flask of oil in 1 Kgs 17:8–16. Scholars disagree on which cycle borrowed from the other. Most agree that the duplication marks these as stock wonder-tales attached to famous prophets.
- **The feeding miracle** has no historical control. Its formula, giving food, an objection, then plenty left over, is literary. Later Gospel feeding stories use the same pattern.
- **Chronological placement.** The chapter has no king's name or date. Its placement in Jehoram's reign is editorial.

## Assessment

2 Kings 4 is the purest example of prophetic legend in the book. These are short tales celebrating the wonder-working power of a man of God, of the type Alexander Rofé classifies as *legenda*. They were probably preserved in prophetic circles in northern Israel. They tell us how such groups saw Elisha and give an authentic snapshot of village life in the Iron Age. They are not records of events. The firmest historical element is geographical: Shunem was a long-established town in the Jezreel Valley, as Egyptian and Amarna texts confirm.
