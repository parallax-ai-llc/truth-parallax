## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The sudden flight of the Aramean army at a divinely sent noise is a miracle story, and no outside source records the siege or its end. Some background details are realistic: Samaria's city gate as a marketplace, grain measures and prices, and a Syrian fear of "the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Egypt." These reflect the political map of the 9th–8th centuries.

## What the Text Claims

- Elisha predicts that the next day a *seah* of fine flour and two *seahs* of barley will sell for a shekel at the gate of Samaria (7:1).
- An officer who scoffs is told he will see it but not eat it (7:2).
- Four men with skin disease, outside the gate, find the Aramean camp deserted (7:3–5).
- The Lord had made the Arameans hear chariots and horses. They concluded that the king of Israel had hired "**the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Egypt**" (7:6–7).
- Samaria plunders the camp. Prices fall as predicted, and the officer is trampled in the gate (7:16–20).

## External Evidence

No extra-biblical source records this siege or its sudden end.

### "Kings of the Hittites"

In 9th–8th century Assyrian usage, *Hatti* means north Syria and south-east Anatolia. This region was ruled by the **Syro-Hittite (Neo-Hittite) kingdoms**, such as Carchemish, Hamath and Gurgum, many of whose kings used Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions. Shalmaneser III's annals list such rulers fighting alongside or against Damascus. The phrase in 7:6 is therefore anachronistic only if read as the Late Bronze Age Hittite empire. As a term for the northern kings, it fits the 9th–8th centuries well.

### Egypt

Egypt under the 22nd Dynasty was politically divided, and its intervention in the Levant was limited. The Kurkh Monolith lists 1,000 troops of *Muṣri* at Qarqar (853 BCE). Most scholars take *Muṣri* there as Egypt, though some propose a region in Anatolia. An Aramean fear of Egyptian mercenaries is plausible but not specifically confirmed.

### Gate, market and measures

Excavated gates at Iron Age sites such as Dan, Megiddo and Gezer show large chambered complexes with benches and open plazas. Texts from Ruth 4 to Amos 5:10–15 confirm that markets, courts and public business took place at gates. The *seah* was a standard dry measure, and the shekel was a weight of silver. Coinage did not yet exist.

## Points of Agreement

- Commerce and crowds concentrated at the city gate, as excavations of chambered gate complexes suggest.
- Social outcasts with skin disease are kept outside the walls (compare Lev 13:46).
- The political horizon fits the period: Aram at war with Israel, the Syro-Hittite north as a rival power, and Egypt as a possible paymaster.
- Price collapses when a siege is lifted are realistic.

## Points of Conflict

- **The cause of the flight** is supernatural. A historian might suspect that rumours of an Assyrian or northern threat caused the withdrawal. Assyrian campaigns against Damascus in 841, 838 and around 796 BCE could have forced Aramean forces to leave Israel suddenly. This is speculation, and the text gives no hint of it.
- **Period.** As with chapter 6, the unnamed kings and the Ben-hadad setting suggest this story belongs to the Jehu-dynasty wars, not to Jehoram's reign.
- **Literary pattern.** A scoffer who doubts the prophet and dies is a stock motif of prophetic legend.

## Assessment

Chapter 7 completes the siege story of 6:24–33 as a lesson about the reliability of the prophetic word. The four outcasts and the trampled officer give it the character of a well-crafted folk tale. Its background details show a narrator familiar with the Iron Age Levant: gates, measures, and the geopolitics of Hatti and Egypt. The event itself cannot be confirmed. If it has a historical basis, it is most likely a sudden Aramean withdrawal, possibly caused by Assyrian pressure on Damascus around 800 BCE.
