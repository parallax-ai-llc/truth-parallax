## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: High.** The rapid succession of coups in Samaria is confirmed in its key points by the royal inscriptions of **Tiglath-pileser III**. So are Menahem's tribute to "Pul," Assyria's annexation of Galilee and Gilead, and Hoshea's replacement of Pekah. Archaeological destruction layers in northern Israel match the conquests of 733–732 BCE. The main problem is the chronology, especially Pekah's twenty-year reign.

## What the Text Claims

- **Azariah** (Uzziah) of Judah reigns 52 years, becomes leprous, and lives apart while **Jotham** governs (15:1–7).
- In Israel, **Zechariah** reigns six months and is murdered by **Shallum**, who reigns one month and is killed by **Menahem** (15:8–15).
- Menahem sacks Tiphsah (15:16).
- **Pul king of Assyria** comes. Menahem pays him **1,000 talents** of silver, raised at **50 shekels** from each wealthy man (15:19–20).
- **Pekahiah** is killed by **Pekah**, who reigns "twenty years" (15:23–27).
- **Tiglath-pileser** takes Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, **Hazor**, Gilead, Galilee and Naphtali, and deports the people to Assyria (15:29).
- **Hoshea** kills Pekah and becomes king (15:30).

## External Evidence

### Pul and Menahem

- "Pul" is **Pulu**, the name under which Tiglath-pileser III appears in the Babylonian King List A and the Ptolemaic Canon ("Poros"). The identification of 15:19 with 15:29 is certain. 1 Chronicles 5:26 wrongly treats them as two kings.
- Tiglath-pileser's annals and the **Iran stele** list **Menahem of Samaria** (*Menihimme Samerinaya*) among western rulers paying tribute, together with Rezin of Damascus and Hiram of Tyre, around 738 BCE.
- 1,000 talents at 50 shekels each implies about 60,000 contributors (at 3,000 shekels to the talent). Fifty shekels was roughly the price of a slave in Neo-Assyrian sale documents. The figure is internally consistent.

### Pekah, Hoshea and the conquests of 733–732

Tiglath-pileser's summary inscriptions state, in paraphrase, that the people of the land of Bit-Humri (Israel) "overthrew **Pekah** (*Paqaha*) their king." Tiglath-pileser then installed **Hoshea** (*Ausi'*) as king and received his tribute. His fragmentary annals list towns of Galilee taken and captives deported. Assyria then reorganized the region into provinces, including *Magiddu* (Megiddo) and *Du'ru* (Dor).

### Archaeology

- **Hazor** Stratum V ended in a violent destruction that Yadin and later excavators attribute to 732 BCE.
- **Megiddo** was rebuilt with Assyrian-style palaces as a provincial capital (Stratum III).
- Other northern sites, such as Kinneret and Bethsaida, show destruction or abandonment at about this time. Surveys of Galilee indicate a sharp drop in settlement.

### Uzziah's later burial plaque

A stone plaque from the Second Temple period (found 1931) reads in Aramaic, "Here were brought the bones of Uzziah king of Judah. Do not open." It shows a later reburial tradition and is not contemporary evidence.

## Points of Agreement

- Menahem paid Tiglath-pileser a large tribute around 738.
- Pekah was overthrown and Hoshea installed with Assyrian approval in 732. Kings has Hoshea kill Pekah, and Tiglath-pileser claims to have placed Hoshea on the throne. The two accounts fit together.
- Tiglath-pileser took Galilee and Gilead and deported their populations. The destruction at Hazor and the creation of provinces confirm this.
- Dynastic instability in Samaria in the 740s–730s is also reflected in Hosea 7:7 and 8:4.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 15 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| Pekah reigns 20 years (15:27) | Impossible between Menahem's tribute (c. 738) and Pekah's fall (732). Some propose a rival reign in Gilead from c. 750 |
| Azariah reigns 52 years | Requires long co-regencies to fit |
| Assyrian "Azriyau" | Older scholars identified an *Azriyau* in Tiglath-pileser's annals with Azariah of Judah. Most now reject this |
| Tiphsah (15:16) | Probably a textual error; the Greek reads *Tappuah* |

## Assessment

Chapter 15 is among the best-documented chapters of the Hebrew Bible. Nearly every major event in Israel's last decades before Hoshea can be matched with Assyrian royal inscriptions or destruction layers. The editor's sources clearly included accurate records of the payment to Pul and the losses of 733–732. The chronological numbers are the main weak point, and they reflect later editing or textual corruption.
