## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** No extra-biblical source mentions **Josiah** or the discovery of a law book. The officials named belong to a recognizable late 7th-century Jerusalem milieu, and a seal impression of a "son of Shaphan" shows that this scribal family existed. The discovery account itself is best understood as a legitimation story for a reform and for the book of Deuteronomy, not as neutral reporting.

## What the Text Claims

- **Josiah** becomes king at eight and reigns 31 years (22:1).
- In his 18th year (c. 622 BCE) he sends the scribe **Shaphan** to the high priest **Hilkiah** to pay the temple workmen (22:3–7).
- Hilkiah reports: "I have found the **book of the law** in the house of the LORD" (22:8).
- Shaphan reads it to the king, who tears his clothes (22:10–13).
- A delegation (Hilkiah, **Ahikam son of Shaphan**, Achbor, Shaphan, Asaiah) consults the prophetess **Huldah**, who lives in the **Mishneh** (Second Quarter). She predicts disaster on Jerusalem but a peaceful death for Josiah (22:14–20).

## External Evidence

### Josiah

No inscription, chronicle or seal names Josiah. His reign falls in the period when Assyrian control of the west was collapsing (after about 630 BCE) and before Egypt reasserted itself (from about 610).

### Shaphan's family and the City of David bullae

Yigal Shiloh's excavations in the City of David (Area G) uncovered a burnt building from the destruction of 586 BCE, known as the "House of the Bullae." It contained 51 clay sealings. One reads "**Belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan**." Jeremiah 36:10–12 names Gemariah son of Shaphan as a scribe with a chamber in the temple, and he is a brother of Ahikam (22:12). The identification is not certain, since the names were common. Many scholars accept it as likely, and it confirms that a family of high officials by that name was active in Jerusalem.

### The Mishneh

The "Second Quarter" (*mišneh*) is usually identified with the new residential district on Jerusalem's **Western Hill**, enclosed by Hezekiah's Broad Wall in the late 8th century (see chapter 20). A late 7th-century writer would naturally refer to it.

### The book and its date

Since W. M. L. de Wette (1805), most scholars have identified the book with an early form of **Deuteronomy**. Its demand for a single sanctuary matches Josiah's centralization in chapter 23. Parallels between Deuteronomy 13 and 28 and the **Succession Treaty of Esarhaddon** (672 BCE) suggest that core parts of Deuteronomy were composed in the 7th century in deliberate imitation of Assyrian treaty language. The treaty is known from copies found at Nimrud and, in 2009, at Tell Tayinat.

## Points of Agreement

- The officials and offices named, a royal scribe, a high priest and the king's "servant," fit the late Judahite administration known from seals and bullae.
- The Shaphan family is plausibly attested by the Gemaryahu bulla.
- Josiah's reign coincides with Assyria's withdrawal, when a reform asserting Judah's independence makes historical sense.
- Deuteronomy's language and treaty structure fit a 7th-century date.

## Points of Conflict

- **"Finding" a lost book.** Rediscovering an ancient document during temple repairs is a known Near Eastern legitimation motif. Mesopotamian kings such as Nabonidus report finding older foundation inscriptions during restorations. Many critics see the episode as a way to present a new law code as ancient. The term "pious fraud" is sometimes used, though others prefer to speak of the authorization of a recently compiled text.
- **Huldah's prophecy** that Josiah would die "in peace" (22:20) was not fulfilled. He was killed by Necho at Megiddo (23:29). This failed detail suggests the oracle was written before 609 and not revised afterwards. Some scholars use it to argue for a Josianic first edition of Kings.
- **Repair procedure (22:4–7)** closely repeats 12:10–15, suggesting a stock literary pattern.

## Assessment

The chapter cannot be verified directly, but its setting is credible. Its cast of officials fits a real late 7th-century administration, as the Shaphan bulla shows. The "found book" is almost certainly a form of Deuteronomy, a work whose treaty language points to the 7th century. Whether it was truly found or newly written, the story served to give the reform an ancient authority. The unfulfilled promise to Josiah is a notable sign that parts of this narrative were composed during his lifetime.
