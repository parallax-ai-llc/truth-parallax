## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate.** The core events, the violent end of the Omride dynasty in about 841 BCE and the deaths of Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah, are confirmed by outside sources. But the **Tel Dan inscription** credits their deaths to an Aramean king, probably Hazael, not to Jehu. The vivid narrative is also shaped by prophetic legitimation.

## What the Text Claims

- A disciple of Elisha anoints **Jehu**, an army commander, as king at Ramoth-gilead (9:1–10).
- Jehu drives to **Jezreel**, where the wounded **Joram** is recovering. He shoots Joram with an arrow, and the body is thrown on Naboth's field (9:14–26).
- **Ahaziah** of Judah flees "by the way of Beth-haggan," is wounded near Ibleam, and dies at **Megiddo** (9:27–28).
- **Jezebel**, painted and looking from a window, taunts Jehu. She is thrown down, trampled, and eaten by dogs (9:30–37).

## External Evidence

### The Tel Dan inscription

Fragments of an Aramaic victory stele were found at **Tel Dan** in 1993 and 1994. The author, a king of Aram, almost certainly **Hazael**, says his father died and that Hadad made him king. The broken lines are generally restored to claim that he killed **[Jeho]ram son of [Ahab], king of Israel**, and **[Ahaz]iahu son of [Jehoram, kin]g of the House of David**.

The restorations are accepted by most scholars, though not all. The phrase **bytdwd** ("House of David") is clearly legible and is the earliest extra-biblical reference to David's dynasty.

### The Black Obelisk and Assyrian annals

**Shalmaneser III's Black Obelisk** (Nimrud, found 1846) shows a figure prostrate before the king with the caption "**tribute of Jehu (Iaua) of Bit-Humri**," i.e. of the house of Omri. His annals date this to his 18th year, **841 BCE**, after he besieged Hazael in Damascus. Jehu was on the throne by 841, which fixes the coup.

### Jezreel

Excavations by David Ussishkin and John Woodhead (1990–1996) uncovered a large rectangular **Omride royal enclosure** with casemate walls, corner towers and a rock-cut moat. It had a short life in the mid-9th century and ended in destruction. Many link the destruction to Hazael's campaigns, and some to Jehu's coup. Either way, Jezreel was a short-lived Omride centre, as the text assumes.

### Jezebel's seal?

An unprovenanced seal inscribed *yzbl* with royal Phoenician-style iconography has been argued (Marjo Korpel, 2006) to have belonged to the queen. The reading and identification are disputed and cannot be relied upon.

## Points of Agreement

- Joram and Ahaziah both died violently in the same year, about 841 BCE, at the time of an Aramean war centred on Ramoth-gilead.
- Jehu was king of Israel by 841 and immediately submitted to Assyria. This fits a usurper seeking an ally against Damascus.
- Jezreel was a fortified Omride royal residence that was destroyed soon after construction.
- Hosea 1:4 ("the blood of Jezreel") independently remembers Jehu's violence there.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 9 | Tel Dan inscription |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Jehu kills Joram with an arrow at Jezreel | The Aramean king claims to have killed Joram |
| Jehu's men kill Ahaziah near Ibleam | The Aramean king claims to have killed Ahaziah |
| Jehu acts on a prophet's anointing | The king credits the god Hadad with his victory |

Proposed solutions:
- **Jehu as Hazael's agent or ally.** Hazael could claim deaths carried out by his collaborator (suggested by William Schniedewind and others).
- **Royal boasting.** Hazael claimed credit for deaths that followed from his war, including wounds Joram received at Ramoth-gilead.
- **Biblical revision.** The prophetic narrative made Jehu the sole agent to show divine judgment on Ahab's house.

## Assessment

This is a rare case where two ancient, independent and near-contemporary sources describe the same event and disagree on who was responsible. Both agree on the outcome: both kings were dead and a new regime held Samaria. Historians generally accept the coup, its date and its violence. The specific details, Jezebel's makeup and Naboth's field, belong to a narrative designed to show prophecy fulfilled (1 Kgs 21:19–24). Jehu's coup may have taken place in the context of Aramean victory, and possibly with Aramean backing.
