## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The floating axe head and the blinding of an Aramean army at Dothan are legends. The siege of Samaria by "Ben-hadad" may preserve a real memory of Aramean pressure on Israel's capital. However, the king's name does not fit the Omride period to which the editor assigns it, and the story probably belongs to a later era.

## What the Text Claims

- Elisha makes a borrowed iron axe head float in the Jordan (6:1–7).
- Elisha reveals the Aramean king's plans to the king of Israel (6:8–12).
- At **Dothan**, an Aramean force surrounds Elisha. They are struck blind and led to Samaria, then fed and released (6:13–23).
- **Ben-hadad king of Aram** besieges **Samaria**. A donkey's head sells for 80 shekels of silver, and two women agree to eat their sons (6:24–29).
- The king of Israel tears his clothes, revealing sackcloth, and swears to kill Elisha (6:30–33).

## External Evidence

### The name Ben-hadad

Assyrian records give a clear sequence of 9th-century kings in Damascus. **Hadadezer** (Adad-idri) ruled from at least 853 to about 845 BCE, and **Hazael** from about 842. A king called "Ben-hadad" in the reign of Jehoram of Israel (c. 851–841) conflicts with this sequence. The **Zakkur stele** from Tell Afis names **Bar-Hadad son of Hazael**, the "Ben-hadad son of Hazael" of 2 Kings 13:3, 24, who reigned around 800 BCE. Many scholars therefore follow J. Maxwell Miller in proposing that the Aramean war stories in 1 Kings 20, 22 and 2 Kings 6–7 originally concerned the Jehu dynasty's wars with Ben-hadad son of Hazael. On this view they were moved to the Omride period. Some conservative scholars instead treat "Ben-hadad" as a throne name of Hadadezer.

### Dothan

Tell Dothan, on the route from the Jezreel Valley into the hills of Samaria, was excavated by Joseph Free (1953–1964). It had substantial Iron Age occupation. The site's position on a main road fits the scene. The archaeology says nothing about the event itself.

### Iron tools and siege famine

Iron tools were in general use by the 9th century. A borrowed iron axe head was a valuable item, which explains the prophet's anxiety. Cannibalism under siege is a recurring motif in ancient texts. It appears in Assyrian treaty curses (the Succession Treaty of Esarhaddon threatens it) and in the covenant curses of Deuteronomy 28:53–57. It is also reported for real sieges, as in Lamentations 4:10 and Josephus on Jerusalem in 70 CE.

## Points of Agreement

- Aramean campaigns against Israel and an attack on Samaria fit the 9th–8th century balance of power. Damascus dominated Israel under Hazael and his son (10:32; 13:3, 7).
- Samaria's strong fortifications made a long siege, not storming, the realistic tactic.
- Inflated food prices in a siege are realistic. Eighty shekels for a donkey's head and five for a quarter-kab of "dove's dung" (6:25) are deliberately extreme sums, which is the point of the story.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 6 | Problem |
|-----------|---------|
| Ben-hadad besieges Samaria under Jehoram | Assyrian sources name Hadadezer, then Hazael, as Jehoram's contemporaries in Damascus |
| An entire Aramean force struck blind at Dothan | Miracle claim; no historical control |
| Unnamed "king of Israel" | Editorially Jehoram, but the text gives no name |
| Siege imagery (cannibalism) | Matches a stock curse motif, so the story may be shaped by literary convention |

## Assessment

The chapter mixes prophetic wonder-tales with a war story whose setting is realistic but whose period is uncertain. The best historical reading is that Samaria did suffer Aramean siege or blockade at some point. This most plausibly happened under Jehoahaz, when "the king of Aram had destroyed them and made them like dust at threshing" (13:7). Prophetic tradition placed Elisha at the centre of that crisis. The editor's placement in Jehoram's reign is likely secondary.
