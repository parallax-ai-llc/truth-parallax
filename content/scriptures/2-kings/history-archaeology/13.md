## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate.** The regnal framework is supported by outside evidence: Israel's subjection to Hazael and his son Ben-hadad, a "deliverer" who relieved the pressure, and Joash of Israel's recovery of cities. **Joash of Samaria** appears in an Assyrian stele, and **Bar-Hadad son of Hazael** in an Aramaic one. The Elisha episodes, the arrow oracle and the bones that revive a corpse, are legends.

## What the Text Claims

- **Jehoahaz** son of Jehu reigns 17 years; Israel is given into the hand of **Hazael** and **Ben-hadad son of Hazael** (13:1–3).
- The LORD gives Israel "a **deliverer**," and they escape from Aram (13:5).
- Jehoahaz's army is reduced to 50 horsemen, 10 chariots and 10,000 foot soldiers (13:7).
- **Joash** (Jehoash) of Israel reigns 16 years (13:10–13).
- The dying Elisha has Joash shoot arrows and strike the ground three times, predicting three victories (13:14–19).
- A corpse thrown into Elisha's grave revives on touching his bones (13:20–21).
- Joash defeats Ben-hadad three times and recovers the cities Hazael had taken (13:22–25).

## External Evidence

### Joash in the Tell al-Rimah stele

A stele of the Assyrian king **Adad-nirari III**, found at Tell al-Rimah in northern Iraq in 1967, lists **"Joash (Iu'asu) the Samarian"** among rulers who paid tribute. This was probably during a western campaign around 796 BCE. Joash is thus confirmed as king of Israel at the end of the 9th century.

### Adad-nirari III and Damascus

Adad-nirari's inscriptions (the Calah slab and the Sheikh Hammad stele, among others) record a heavy tribute imposed on **Mari'** of Damascus. *Mari'* is Aramaic for "lord," probably a title of Hazael's son. Assyrian pressure on Damascus weakened it and freed Israel. Many scholars identify Adad-nirari III as the unnamed "deliverer" of 13:5. Others suggest Zakkur of Hamath, or Joash or Jeroboam II themselves.

### The Zakkur stele

An Aramaic stele of **Zakkur king of Hamath and Lu'ash** (found at Tell Afis, published 1907) describes a siege of his city Hazrach by a coalition led by "**Bar-Hadad son of Hazael, king of Aram**." Zakkur says his god Baalshamayn saved him. The stele confirms the name and parentage given in 2 Kings 13:3, 24.

## Points of Agreement

- Hazael's dominance over Israel under Jehoahaz (13:3, 7, 22) fits the archaeological and textual picture of Aramean supremacy in the late 9th century.
- Hazael was succeeded by his son **Bar-Hadad (Ben-hadad)**, as the Zakkur stele confirms.
- Joash of Israel reigned around 800 BCE and was an Assyrian tributary.
- Damascus declined after Assyrian intervention, which allowed Israel to recover territory (13:25), consistent with Assyrian sources.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 13 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| The "deliverer" is unnamed | The Assyrian king who relieved Israel is not mentioned, while Assyria's role is clear from Assyrian sources |
| Joash's tribute to Assyria | Not mentioned in Kings |
| Elisha's bones revive a dead man | Miracle legend; this kind of relic story is untestable |
| Dual concluding formulas for Joash (13:12–13 and 14:15–16) | An editorial seam, showing the chapter was assembled from separate sources |

## Assessment

Chapter 13 is a good example of the book's method. A thin but accurate annalistic frame is filled out with prophetic stories. The frame includes names, reign lengths, Aramean oppression and recovery, and Assyrian and Aramaic texts confirm it. The editor gives the theological explanation, in which YHWH sends a deliverer, but leaves out the Assyrian political cause. The Elisha material shows how prophetic legend clustered around his death. It has no historical value for events, but it shows that his grave was later venerated.
