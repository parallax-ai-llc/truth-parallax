## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate to high.** The siege and capture of Samaria, the deportation of Israelites to Mesopotamia and Media, and the settlement of foreign peoples in their place are confirmed by Babylonian and Assyrian sources. The account is compressed and ambiguous about **which Assyrian king** took the city. The long theological sermon (17:7–23) and the polemic against the new settlers (17:24–41) are interpretation, not reporting.

## What the Text Claims

- **Hoshea** becomes a vassal of **Shalmaneser** of Assyria, then withholds tribute and sends to "**So, king of Egypt**" (17:3–4).
- The king of Assyria imprisons Hoshea, besieges **Samaria** for three years, and takes it in Hoshea's 9th year (17:4–6).
- Israelites are deported to **Halah**, the **Habor** (the river of **Gozan**), and the cities of the **Medes** (17:6).
- A sermon explains the fall as punishment for idolatry (17:7–23).
- The king of Assyria brings settlers from **Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath and Sepharvaim**. Lions attack them, so an Israelite priest is sent back to teach them the worship of YHWH. Each group continues to worship its own gods too, such as **Nergal** of Cuthah and **Ashima** of Hamath (17:24–41).

## External Evidence

### Shalmaneser V or Sargon II?

- The **Babylonian Chronicle** (ABC 1) records that **Shalmaneser V** "ravaged **Šamara'in**" during his short reign (727–722 BCE). Most scholars identify this with Samaria.
- **Sargon II** (722–705 BCE) repeatedly claims in later inscriptions (the Khorsabad "Display" inscription and the Nimrud Prism) to have conquered Samaria. He says he deported **27,280** people (27,290 in another version), took 50 chariots into his army, resettled the city with people from other lands, and imposed tribute.
- Sargon also reports suppressing a western revolt in 720 led by Yau-bi'di of Hamath, in which Samaria took part.

The common reconstruction (Tadmor, Na'aman, Becking) is that Shalmaneser took Samaria in 722 and died soon after. Sargon then had to reconquer a rebellious Samaria in 720, carried out the mass deportation, and made it the province of *Samerina*. 2 Kings 17:6 names no king at the capture ("the king of Assyria"). It may unknowingly merge the two phases.

### Deportees and settlers

- **Gozan** is Tell Halaf on the Habor (Khabur) river. Neo-Assyrian documents from Gozan and elsewhere contain people with Hebrew (Yahwistic) names, consistent with Israelite deportees. The cities of the Medes fit Sargon's campaigns in western Iran.
- Sargon also says that in 716/715 BCE he settled Arab tribes in Samaria.
- Assyrian-style **cuneiform legal tablets** from Gezer (mid-7th century) include people with Babylonian names. This confirms a foreign presence in the former kingdom.
- **Cuthah** (Tell Ibrahim, Babylonia) was indeed the cult centre of **Nergal**, which matches 17:30 exactly.

### Samaria's archaeology

Unlike Hazor or Lachish, Samaria shows no thorough destruction layer that can be clearly tied to 722/720. The city continued as an Assyrian provincial seat. Surveys show the hill country of Samaria lost some population but was far from emptied.

## Points of Agreement

- Hoshea was an Assyrian vassal who rebelled, and Samaria fell after a siege around 722–720.
- Deportations went to the Habor/Gozan region and Media, as Kings says.
- Foreign groups were settled in the province, in line with the Assyrian policy of two-way deportation.
- At least some of the settlers' gods are correctly assigned to their cities of origin.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 17 | Evidence or problem |
|------------|---------------------|
| One siege and conquest under "Shalmaneser" | Sargon II claims the conquest and the deportation (720) |
| "So, king of Egypt" | No pharaoh named So is known. Candidates are Osorkon IV of Tanis, or the city of **Sais** misread as a king |
| Israel wholly deported (17:18, 23) | 27,280 is a fraction of the population. Many Israelites stayed, and some fled to Judah |
| Settlers' worship as a mixture | A polemic against later Samaritans. The Samaritan community's own traditions reject this account |

## Assessment

The core facts of 17:3–6 and 17:24 hold up well. The Assyrian inscriptions agree on the siege, the capture, the deportations and the resettlement. They differ on which king should get the credit, but this is explained by Assyrian royal propaganda as much as by biblical compression.

The rest of the chapter is the Deuteronomistic editor's theological centrepiece. It explains the end of the northern kingdom as covenant failure, and it presents the people of Samaria as religiously mixed. That portrait served Judean polemic in the Persian period. It is not a neutral description, and the archaeological evidence suggests substantial Israelite continuity in the region.
