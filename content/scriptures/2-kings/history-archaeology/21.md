## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Moderate.** **Manasseh** is confirmed by Assyrian inscriptions as a loyal vassal king of Judah, and his long reign fits a period of Assyrian peace and Judean recovery. The chapter's account of him is almost entirely a theological indictment, however. It blames him for the later fall of Jerusalem, and its religious charges cannot be measured in detail.

## What the Text Claims

- **Manasseh** reigns **55 years** in Jerusalem (21:1).
- He rebuilds the high places, erects altars to Baal and an **Asherah**, worships the "host of heaven," and builds altars in the temple courts (21:3–5).
- He makes his son "**pass through the fire**," and practises divination and necromancy (21:6).
- He sheds "very much innocent blood" (21:16).
- Prophets announce that Jerusalem will be wiped "as one wipes a dish" (21:10–15).
- **Amon** reigns two years and is assassinated. The "people of the land" kill the conspirators and enthrone **Josiah** (21:19–26).

## External Evidence

### Manasseh in Assyrian records

- **Esarhaddon's prism inscriptions** (around 673–672 BCE) list "**Manasseh (*Menasî*) king of Judah**" among 22 kings of Hatti and the seacoast. They were ordered to transport building timber and stone for Esarhaddon's palace arsenal at Nineveh.
- **Ashurbanipal's** annals (Prism C) list Manasseh among the western kings who sent gifts and troops to support his first campaign against Egypt (667 BCE).

Both show Manasseh as a compliant vassal.

### Judah in the 7th century

After the devastation of 701, the Shephelah recovered only partly. Settlement expanded into the **Beersheba Valley**, the Judean Desert and the Negev, possibly linked to the Assyrian-controlled Arabian trade. Israel Finkelstein has described Manasseh's reign as a period of economic integration into the Assyrian system, which included the large olive-oil industry at Ekron.

### Popular religion

Hundreds of **Judean pillar figurines**, small clay female figures often linked with Asherah, come from 8th–7th century Judah, including Jerusalem. Inscriptions from Kuntillet ʿAjrud and Khirbet el-Qom invoke "YHWH and his Asherah." These finds show that worship of Asherah alongside YHWH was widespread. The biblical writer treats it as Manasseh's innovation.

### Child sacrifice

The phrase "pass through the fire" (compare 16:3; 23:10) is debated. It may mean literal child sacrifice or a dedication rite. Child sacrifice is attested in Phoenician–Punic contexts, such as the tophets of Carthage. Francesca Stavrakopoulou argues that it was part of traditional Judahite religion, which later writers attributed to "bad" kings.

## Points of Agreement

- Manasseh was king of Judah in the 670s–660s, as Assyrian texts confirm.
- His long reign is consistent with his loyalty to Assyria during its height.
- The religious practices condemned, such as Asherah worship, astral cults and divination, were real features of 7th-century Judah and the wider region.
- The repeated role of the "people of the land" in royal succession fits Judah's internal politics.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 21 | Evidence or problem |
|------------|---------------------|
| No mention of Assyrian vassalage | Assyrian sources show Manasseh supplying labour, materials and troops |
| Manasseh as unprecedented apostate | Asherah and astral worship were long established. "Innovation" is the editor's framing |
| Manasseh solely responsible for 586 (21:10–15; 23:26; 24:3) | A theological explanation, written after the fact |
| Chronicles: Manasseh is taken to Babylon in chains and repents (2 Chr 33:11–16) | Not mentioned in Kings and not supported by Assyrian sources. Many think it is a Chronicler's theological construction |

## Assessment

The chapter is a clear case of theology overriding history. Assyrian texts and archaeology show a long, relatively stable and prosperous reign within the Assyrian empire. The Deuteronomistic editor needed an explanation for Jerusalem's later destruction after the reforms of the pious Josiah. Manasseh became the scapegoat. The specific religious practices described were real in 7th-century Judah, but the claim that they were introduced by Manasseh alone is not supported.
