## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** Naaman and the healing in the Jordan are known only from this story, which serves a theological purpose. The background, however, is accurate: a powerful Aramean kingdom centred on Damascus, raids into Israel, the cult of the storm god Hadad-Rimmon, and the rivers of Damascus.

## What the Text Claims

- **Naaman**, commander of the army of the king of Aram, has a skin disease (*ṣāraʿat*). An Israelite girl captured in a raid tells him of a prophet in Samaria (5:1–3).
- The king of Aram sends a letter and rich gifts to the **king of Israel**, who fears a pretext for war (5:5–7).
- Elisha tells Naaman to wash seven times in the Jordan. Naaman protests that **Abana and Pharpar**, rivers of Damascus, are better (5:10–12).
- Healed, Naaman asks for two mule-loads of earth to worship YHWH on, and asks pardon for bowing in the house of **Rimmon** with his master (5:15–19).
- Elisha's servant Gehazi takes a bribe and is struck with Naaman's disease (5:20–27).

## External Evidence

### Aram-Damascus

Assyrian inscriptions confirm Damascus as the leading Aramean power in the 9th century. **Hadadezer** (Adad-idri) of Damascus led the anti-Assyrian coalition at Qarqar in 853 BCE, alongside Ahab, according to Shalmaneser III's **Kurkh Monolith**. **Hazael** succeeded him around 842. Seasonal Aramean raids into Israel (5:2) are consistent with the long border wars described in Kings and implied by the Tel Dan inscription.

### Rimmon and Hadad

Rimmon is the Hebrew form of **Ramman** ("the thunderer"), an Akkadian epithet of the storm god Adad/Hadad. The chief god of Damascus was Hadad, as royal names like Ben-hadad and Hadadezer show. Zechariah 12:11 preserves the combined form "Hadad-rimmon." The Hebrew vocalization *rimmôn* ("pomegranate") may be a deliberate pun.

### Abana and Pharpar

The Abana is usually identified with the **Barada**, which flows through Damascus, and the Pharpar with the **Awaj**, south of the city. Both are perennial rivers. Naaman's preference reflects real geography.

### Territorial theology

Naaman asks for Israelite soil to worship YHWH in Damascus (5:17). This reflects a widespread ancient belief that gods were tied to their lands (compare 1 Sam 26:19; 2 Kgs 17:26). The Mesha Stele likewise treats Kemosh as the god of Moab's land.

## Points of Agreement

- The political setting is accurate: rivalry and uneasy diplomacy between Damascus and Samaria, and a king's letter of introduction accompanied by gifts of silver, gold and garments.
- Religious details fit Damascus: Hadad-Rimmon's temple, and a king who leans on his officer's arm in worship.
- Treating Israelite soil as sacred is consistent with Iron Age beliefs about gods and territory.

## Points of Conflict

- **No names.** Neither the king of Aram nor the king of Israel is named. This is typical of the Elisha legends and makes the story impossible to date. Editorially it sits in Jehoram's reign, but a Damascus at peace with Israel would fit several periods.
- **Naaman** is not attested outside this chapter. The name itself (*nʿmn*, "pleasant") is a well-formed West Semitic name, and the same root is common in personal names across the Levant.
- **The healing and Gehazi's punishment** are miracle claims. The theme, that a foreigner recognizes Israel's God while an Israelite servant fails, is didactic.
- **Gehazi** appears again in 8:4–5 telling the king of Elisha's deeds. The disease imposed on him "forever" (5:27) does not fit that later scene, which suggests the stories were originally independent.

## Assessment

The Naaman story is a carefully constructed narrative about the universality of Israel's God. It has an accurate grasp of Aramean geography and religion. These details show that the storytellers knew the world of Damascus well, as they would have during a century of contact and war. They do not make the healing itself historical. The story is best classed as a didactic prophetic tale with a realistic 9th–8th century backdrop.
