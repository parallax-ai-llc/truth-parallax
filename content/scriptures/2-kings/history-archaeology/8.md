## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The regnal notes on Jehoram and Ahaziah of Judah, Edom's revolt and the war at Ramoth-gilead read like annal extracts and fit the 9th-century context. **Hazael's usurpation** in Damascus is confirmed by an Assyrian inscription. However, the predecessor he overthrew is called Ben-hadad in Kings but **Hadadezer** in Assyrian sources, and the story of Elisha in Damascus is prophetic legend.

## What the Text Claims

- The Shunammite woman's land is restored after a seven-year famine (8:1–6).
- Elisha goes to **Damascus**. Sick **Ben-hadad** sends **Hazael** to ask if he will recover. Elisha weeps and foretells Hazael's kingship and cruelty to Israel (8:7–13).
- Hazael smothers Ben-hadad with a wet cloth and becomes king (8:14–15).
- **Jehoram son of Jehoshaphat** reigns in Judah and marries a daughter of Ahab. **Edom** and **Libnah** revolt (8:16–24).
- **Ahaziah** of Judah, son of Athaliah, reigns one year. He joins **Joram** of Israel against Hazael at **Ramoth-gilead**, where Joram is wounded and retires to **Jezreel** (8:25–29).

## External Evidence

### Hazael "son of a nobody"

A basalt statue inscription of **Shalmaneser III** from Assur reports, in paraphrase, that **Hadadezer** "passed away" and that **Hazael, son of a nobody**, seized the throne. Shalmaneser then attacked him in 841 BCE. The phrase "son of a nobody" (*mār lā mammāna*) is the standard Assyrian label for a usurper without royal lineage. This confirms that Hazael was not of the royal line and took power around 843–841 BCE.

### Hazael's own monuments

Ivory and bronze objects inscribed for "our lord Hazael" were carried off as booty and found at Arslan Tash, at the Heraion of Samos and at Eretria. The **Tel Dan inscription** (see chapter 9) is generally attributed to him.

### Edom and Libnah

There is no extra-biblical record of Edom's revolt against Judah. The Edomite lowlands were an important copper-producing region in the 10th–9th centuries (Khirbat en-Nahas, excavated by Thomas Levy). Edom's highland monarchy is archaeologically visible mainly from the 8th century. Libnah, in the Shephelah, is usually identified with Tel Burna or Tell es-Safi. Its revolt may be linked to Philistine Gath's rise in this period.

## Points of Agreement

- Hazael was a usurper who seized the throne of Damascus in the 840s, as Kings says.
- Hazael became Israel's main enemy (8:12; 10:32; 13:3). Assyrian, Aramaic and archaeological evidence all depict him as the dominant power in the southern Levant in the second half of the 9th century.
- Ramoth-gilead, in the contested Transjordan, as the front line between Israel and Damascus is geographically sound.
- The Omride–Davidide marriage alliance (Athaliah) fits the period of Omride dominance over Judah. Many historians think Judah was then effectively a junior partner.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 8 | Evidence or problem |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Hazael kills "Ben-hadad" | Assyrian sources name his predecessor **Hadadezer** and say only that he "passed away." The murder is not mentioned |
| A prophet of Israel designates Hazael | Theological framing (compare 1 Kgs 19:15). The Assyrian text knows only a usurpation |
| Jehoram of Judah begins to reign in Joram of Israel's 5th year (8:16) | Conflicts with 1:17, where Joram of Israel accedes in Jehoram of Judah's 2nd year. The data imply a co-regency or editorial error |
| Ahaziah's mother Athaliah is "daughter of Omri" (8:26) | Means "of the house of Omri." 8:18 makes Athaliah Ahab's daughter |

## Assessment

The chapter combines prophetic material about Elisha's influence on kings and foreign affairs with authentic annalistic notes. The confirmation of Hazael as a usurper of the 840s is a significant point in the text's favour. The name of the murdered king is the main discrepancy. Conservative scholars suggest that "Ben-hadad" was a dynastic title or throne name. Critical scholars think the prophetic storyteller applied the familiar royal name of Damascus. On balance the political outline is reliable, and the conversation in Damascus is not.
