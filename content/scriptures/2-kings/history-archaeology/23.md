## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed to moderate.** Josiah's death at Megiddo in 609 BCE at the hands of **Necho II**, and Egypt's control over Judah's succession that followed, fit the Babylonian Chronicle and Herodotus well. The scale of the cult reform is much harder to confirm. Archaeological traces are few and ambiguously dated, and the account is the Deuteronomistic History's idealized climax.

## What the Text Claims

- Josiah reads the book to the people and makes a covenant (23:1–3).
- He removes vessels of Baal, Asherah and the host of heaven from the temple. He removes the idolatrous priests, and defiles the high places "from Geba to Beersheba" (23:4–9).
- He defiles **Topheth** in the Valley of Hinnom and removes the **horses dedicated to the sun** (23:10–11).
- He destroys Solomon's shrines to Ashtoreth, Chemosh and Milcom (23:13–14).
- He destroys the altar at **Bethel**, fulfilling 1 Kings 13 by name, and the high places in Samaria's towns, killing their priests (23:15–20).
- He celebrates an unprecedented **Passover** (23:21–23).
- **Necho** goes up "to the king of Assyria at the Euphrates." Josiah goes to meet him, and Necho kills him at **Megiddo** (23:29).
- Necho deposes **Jehoahaz** at **Riblah**, takes him to Egypt, enthrones **Eliakim/Jehoiakim**, and imposes tribute (23:31–35).

## External Evidence

### 609 BCE: Necho and the Assyrian remnant

The **Babylonian Chronicle** (ABC 3, the "Fall of Nineveh" chronicle) records that in 609 BCE a large **Egyptian army** crossed the Euphrates to help the Assyrian king **Ashur-uballit II** attempt to retake **Harran** from the Babylonians. Necho was therefore going *to help* Assyria, which is what the Hebrew says ("to the king of Assyria"). The KJV's "against" is misleading. Herodotus (2.159) says that Necos defeated the "Syrians" at **Magdolos**, often taken as a distorted form of Megiddo, and took Kadytis.

### Egyptian control, 609–605

Necho's base at **Riblah** in Syria and his deposition and appointment of Judah's kings show that Egypt controlled the Levant until Babylon's victory at Carchemish in 605. The late 7th-century fort of **Meṣad Ḥashavyahu** on the coast, with Greek pottery and a Hebrew petition ostracon, raises a debated question. It may show Judahite expansion under Josiah, or Greek mercenaries in Egyptian service.

### Archaeology of the reform

- **Arad**: Yohanan Aharoni originally dated the burial of the shrine's altar and incense altars to Hezekiah and Josiah respectively. Ze'ev Herzog's revised stratigraphy places the shrine's dismantling in the late 8th century, which would be Hezekiah's reign, not Josiah's.
- **Beersheba**: the dismantled horned altar also seems to predate Josiah.
- **Pillar figurines** and other household cult objects continue into the 6th century. This suggests that any reform had a limited effect on popular religion.
- **Bethel**: surveys and re-evaluations of the old excavations suggest the site was only sparsely occupied in the late 7th century.

Christoph Uehlinger argues for a "well-grounded minimum." He accepts a reform focused on the Jerusalem temple and astral cults, while rejecting the full nationwide programme.

## Points of Agreement

- Necho II marched north through Megiddo in 609 to support Assyria, and Josiah died there.
- Egypt deposed Jehoahaz and installed Jehoiakim, taking tribute. This fits Egyptian control of the region between 609 and 605.
- Astral cults, Asherah and solar symbolism were real features of Judahite religion in the period.
- A Judahite reform asserting independence makes sense during Assyria's collapse.

## Points of Conflict

| 2 Kings 23 | Evidence or problem |
|------------|---------------------|
| Nationwide reform, including Samaria | Little archaeological trace. Josiah's control of the former northern kingdom is doubted |
| Bethel's altar destroyed, as foretold by name (1 Kgs 13:2) | A prophecy written after the event. Bethel was sparsely settled at the time |
| Arad and Beersheba shrines | May have gone out of use under Hezekiah, not Josiah |
| Josiah dies "in peace" (22:20) | He is killed at Megiddo |
| No battle described | 2 Chronicles 35:20–24 develops a battle and a warning from Necho |

## Assessment

This chapter is the summit of the Deuteronomistic History. It is written to show Josiah as the ideal king who fulfils Deuteronomy. Historians generally accept that Josiah carried out some religious centralization in Jerusalem during the vacuum left by Assyria's decline, but the comprehensive purge described is likely exaggerated. The account of his death is sober, brief and consistent with Babylonian sources. Its very abruptness is a mark of historical memory, since it contradicts the theology of reward. Frank Moore Cross saw 23:25–27 as the junction between a Josianic first edition and an exilic supplement explaining why disaster still came.
