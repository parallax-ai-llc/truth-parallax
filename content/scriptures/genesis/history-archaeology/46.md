## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter combines a theophany at Beersheba with a schematic list of 70 descendants. The list is a priestly construction based on later tribal clans, and the Beersheba setting does not match the site's archaeology for the 2nd millennium.

## What the Text Claims

- At Beersheba, God tells Jacob not to fear going down to Egypt, for he will make him a great nation there and bring him back (46:1–4).
- Jacob's family travels to Egypt with their livestock (46:5–7).
- A list of Jacob's descendants by each wife totals 66, plus Joseph's family, making **70** (46:8–27).
- Joseph meets Jacob in Goshen and coaches his brothers to tell Pharaoh they are herdsmen, "for every shepherd is an abomination to the Egyptians" (46:28–34).

## External Evidence

### Beersheba

Tel Beersheba, excavated by Yohanan Aharoni and later Ze'ev Herzog, was first settled in Iron I (12th–11th centuries BCE) and became a fortified Judahite town in Iron II. No Middle or Late Bronze settlement has been found there. Conservative scholars reply that the patriarchs camped at wells rather than towns, so a lack of settlement is no problem. Critics note that the story presents Beersheba as an established cult site, which matches its role in the monarchy (Amos 5:5; 8:14).

### The seventy

- The list is clearly schematic. It includes Hezron and Hamul (46:12), grandsons of Judah through Perez, who by the logic of chapter 38 could not have been born yet. Benjamin, a young man in chapters 43–44, already has ten sons (46:21).
- The names correspond closely to the clan lists of Numbers 26, which most scholars consider priestly material from the exile or later.
- The number 70 is symbolic: the nations in Genesis 10 number 70, as do the elders of Exodus 24. The Septuagint, followed by Acts 7:14, gives 75. One of the Dead Sea Scrolls (4QExod^b) also reads 75 in Exodus 1:5.

### Asiatic herders in the Delta

- Bietak's excavations at Tell el-Dab'a (Avaris) revealed Levantine-style houses, burials with donkeys, and Middle Bronze Canaanite pottery from the late 12th dynasty onward. A Levantine population grew here into the Hyksos (15th dynasty) state.
- Manetho, as quoted by Josephus (*Against Apion* 1.73–105), already linked the Hyksos with the ancestors of the Jews. Most modern scholars see a possible distant memory of Hyksos-era Asiatics in Egypt behind the Genesis–Exodus tradition, but no direct link to Jacob's family.

### "Shepherds are an abomination"

No Egyptian text expresses a general contempt for shepherds. Egyptian art sometimes shows herdsmen as uncouth, and Egyptians were wary of nomads, but the statement may be a narrative device or reflect late-period stereotypes. Herodotus (2.47, 2.164) describes swineherds as a despised class in Egypt, not shepherds as such.

## Points of Agreement

- Migration of Levantine herders with flocks into the eastern Delta is well documented, especially in the Middle Bronze Age.
- Beersheba as a gateway to the south and Egypt makes geographical sense.
- The names in the list are real Israelite clan names known from other biblical sources.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 46 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| Jacob sacrifices at Beersheba | No Bronze Age settlement. Its cult importance belongs to the monarchy |
| 70 descendants, including great-grandsons | Schematic list, internally impossible chronology |
| 70 (MT) versus 75 (LXX, Acts, 4QExod^b) | Textual instability in the totals |
| Shepherds an abomination to Egyptians | No Egyptian evidence of this as a general attitude |

## Assessment

Genesis 46 frames the move to Egypt as the fulfilment of divine promise and connects the patriarchal stories with the Exodus. The genealogy is a priestly bridge built from later tribal clan lists, not an ancient census. Archaeology shows that Asiatic communities in the eastern Delta were real and substantial, especially in the Hyksos era, and many scholars think some memory of them lies behind the tradition of Israel's ancestors in Egypt. That general background is as far as external evidence goes.
