## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (theophany legend).** The chapter describes God and two angels eating a meal with Abraham, followed by a theological dialogue about divine justice. These are not events archaeology could confirm or refute. What can be studied is the setting (Mamre near Hebron), the folk-tale pattern, and what the dialogue suggests about when it was written.

## What the Text Claims

- YHWH appears to Abraham "by the oaks of Mamre" as three men; Abraham hurries to provide bread, curds, milk and a calf (18:1–8).
- One visitor promises that Sarah, who is past menopause, will have a son within a year; Sarah laughs (18:9–15).
- YHWH reveals the planned investigation of Sodom and Gomorrah (18:16–21).
- Abraham bargains for the city, from fifty righteous people down to ten (18:22–33).

## External Evidence

### Mamre

The site traditionally identified as Mamre, **Ramat el-Khalil** north of Hebron, has a large Herodian-period stone enclosure around a well and a tree. Later the emperor Constantine built a basilica there. These structures show that the site was a pilgrimage centre by the Roman period. They say nothing about the Bronze Age. Hebron itself (Tel Rumeida) was a walled town in the Middle Bronze Age and was occupied again in the Iron Age, so the general location is real.

### The divine-visitor folk motif

Stories in which disguised gods test human hospitality, reward the hosts, and punish the inhospitable are found across the ancient Mediterranean:

- In the Ugaritic **Legend of Aqhat** (13th century BCE tablets), the childless patriarch Danel is promised a son by El and later hosts the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Hasis with food and drink.
- In Ovid's **Baucis and Philemon** (*Metamorphoses* 8), an elderly couple welcome Zeus and Hermes in disguise; their neighbours, who refused hospitality, are destroyed by a flood.

Genesis 18–19 follows the same pattern: generous hosts (Abraham, then Lot) are rewarded, and an inhospitable city is destroyed. This places the chapter in a well-known genre rather than in a particular historical moment.

### The bargaining dialogue

Westermann and many others regard 18:22b–33 as a late theological reflection added to the older story. Its question, whether a whole community should perish for the guilt of the majority or be spared for a righteous few, closely resembles debates in Jeremiah (5:1) and Ezekiel (14:12–20; 18). This suggests an exilic or early post-exilic setting, when the destruction of Jerusalem raised exactly this problem.

## Points of Agreement

- Hebron and its surroundings were inhabited in the Middle Bronze Age, the period often proposed for the patriarchs, and also in the Iron Age when the stories were written down.
- The meal (bread, curds, milk, a calf) reflects real pastoral food in the southern hill country.
- Hospitality to strangers was a strongly held norm in ancient Near Eastern societies.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 18 | Historical and critical view |
|------------|------------------------------|
| A dated visit by God to Abraham | A legend type known from Ugarit to Greece |
| Sarah bears a child at about 90 (cf. 17:17) | Biologically impossible; the motif of the miraculous birth to a barren mother recurs (Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah) |
| Dialogue set in the patriarchal age | Concerns and vocabulary point to the 6th century BCE |
| Curds and meat served together | Not a historical clue in itself, but often noted as predating, or ignoring, later dietary law |

Conservative scholars such as Gordon Wenham defend the unity of the chapter and read the dialogue as an integral part of Abraham's role as intercessor. They do not, however, claim external evidence for the episode.

## Assessment

Genesis 18 is best read as a sacred legend linked to a known cult site near Hebron, expanded with a theological dialogue about collective guilt. The geography is real and the customs are plausible, but no part of the story is historically verifiable. Its value for the historian lies in what it shows about Israelite religious thought in the period of composition, especially the exilic debate over divine justice.
