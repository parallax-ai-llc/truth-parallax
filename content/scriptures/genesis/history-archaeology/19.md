## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** No site has been securely identified as Sodom or Gomorrah, and none of the candidate sites was destroyed at a time that fits the biblical chronology for Abraham. The story is best understood as an etiological legend explaining the barren, salt-crusted landscape of the Dead Sea basin and the origins of Israel's neighbours, Moab and Ammon. A genuine memory of ruined Bronze Age towns in the region is possible but cannot be shown.

## What the Text Claims

- Two angels visit Lot in Sodom; the men of the city demand to "know" them, and the angels strike them with blindness (19:1–11).
- Lot and his family flee to the small town of **Zoar** (19:15–23).
- YHWH rains "brimstone and fire" on Sodom and Gomorrah and overthrows "the cities of the plain" (19:24–25, 28).
- Lot's wife looks back and becomes a **pillar of salt** (19:26).
- Lot's daughters conceive by their father in a cave; their sons are the ancestors of **Moab** and **Ammon** (19:30–38).

## External Evidence

### Bab edh-Dhra and Numeira

On the southeastern side of the Dead Sea, W. E. Rast and R. T. Schaub excavated two Early Bronze Age towns. **Bab edh-Dhra** was a walled town with a vast cemetery of shaft tombs and charnel houses; **Numeira** was a smaller walled settlement destroyed by fire. Both were abandoned at the end of the Early Bronze III period (traditionally c. 2350 BCE, possibly earlier on recent radiocarbon chronologies). Some scholars, including the excavators in a cautious way and conservative writers such as Bryant Wood more confidently, have connected these sites with Sodom and Gomorrah.

There are serious problems. The destructions are several centuries earlier than any date usually proposed for Abraham. The end of Early Bronze III also saw the collapse of towns across the whole southern Levant, so these sites are not unusual. And no inscription names either site.

### Tall el-Hammam

The excavator of **Tall el-Hammam**, a large Bronze Age site northeast of the Dead Sea, Steven Collins, identifies it with Sodom. In 2021 a team led by Ted Bunch published a paper in *Scientific Reports* arguing that a cosmic airburst destroyed the city around 1650 BCE and suggesting that the event might lie behind the Sodom story. Other specialists sharply criticised its images, mineralogical evidence and comparison with the 1908 Tunguska event. After an author correction (2023) and an editor's note of concern, the journal **retracted the paper in April 2025**, saying it no longer had confidence in its conclusions. The authors dispute the retraction. The airburst hypothesis therefore cannot be used as evidence for the Sodom story. The northern location also conflicts with the traditional southern setting implied by Zoar and by Genesis 14:3.

### Zoar and the landscape

- **Zoar** is generally placed at **Ghor es-Safi** at the southeastern end of the Dead Sea. The Byzantine town of Zoara appears there on the 6th-century CE **Madaba Map**.
- A Byzantine monastery dedicated to Lot was built at **Deir ʿAin ʿAbata** above Safi, around a cave associated with the story. This shows the strength of the later tradition, not its origin.
- The rock-salt mountain **Jebel Usdum (Mount Sedom)** on the southwestern shore has eroded salt pillars that local tradition has long called "Lot's wife." Its Arabic name may preserve the name Sodom.
- Bitumen, sulphur and salt are naturally abundant around the Dead Sea, and the basin lies on an active fault. Earthquakes and fires involving bitumen have been proposed as natural events behind the tale, but these remain speculation.

## Points of Agreement

- The region contained real walled towns in the Early Bronze Age, some destroyed by fire.
- The physical details of the story (salt, sulphur, a desolate plain) match the actual Dead Sea environment.
- Moab and Ammon were real Iron Age neighbours of Israel, attested in Assyrian records and, for Moab, in the **Mesha Stele** (9th century BCE).

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 19 | Historical and archaeological view |
|------------|------------------------------------|
| Cities destroyed in Abraham's lifetime | Candidate destructions date c. 2350 BCE or earlier (Bab edh-Dhra, Numeira) or c. 1650 BCE (Tall el-Hammam, identification disputed) |
| A unique divine catastrophe | Bab edh-Dhra and Numeira ended during a region-wide urban collapse |
| A woman turned to salt | Etiology for natural salt pillars at Mount Sedom |
| Moab and Ammon born of incest | Ethnic polemic typical of origin stories about rival neighbours |

## Assessment

Genesis 19 combines a folk tale of divine visitors and an inhospitable city (see chapter 18) with explanations of a striking landscape and a disparaging account of neighbouring peoples. Archaeology confirms that the Dead Sea plain once held flourishing towns that ended in ruin, and Israelites who saw the area may have linked its desolation with ancient sin. But no excavated site fits the story's location and date together, and the main scientific paper behind the Tall el-Hammam airburst theory has been retracted. Most historians therefore treat Sodom and Gomorrah as legendary.
