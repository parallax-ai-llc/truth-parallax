## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (myth and legend).** The chapter opens with divine beings fathering giants and then introduces the flood story, whose overall shape comes from Mesopotamian tradition. Its account of a wooden vessel large enough to preserve every kind of land animal does not describe a historical event.

## What the Text Claims

- The "sons of God" (*bene ha-elohim*) take human wives; human lifespan is limited to 120 years (6:1–3).
- The **Nephilim** were on the earth "in those days," the mighty men of old (6:4).
- God sees human wickedness and regrets making humankind (6:5–7); Noah finds favour (6:8).
- God tells Noah to build an ark of *gopher* wood, 300 × 50 × 30 cubits (roughly 135 × 22 × 13 m), with three decks, a roof, and a door, sealed with pitch (6:14–16).
- Noah is to bring his family and **two of every kind** of living creature (6:18–20).

## External Evidence

### Sons of God and the divine council

"Sons of God" is a standard term for the members of a divine assembly, as in Job 1:6 and Psalm 82. The Ugaritic texts (14th–13th centuries BCE) use the equivalent phrase *bn ilm* for the children of the high god El. Genesis 6:1–4 thus preserves a fragment of an older, polytheistic tradition about gods mating with humans, which most of the Hebrew Bible elsewhere avoids.

Unions of gods and humans producing heroes are a familiar theme in the ancient world, from Gilgamesh, who is two-thirds divine, to the Greek "race of heroes" in Hesiod's *Works and Days*. The Nephilim reappear as giant inhabitants of Canaan in Numbers 13:33, which shows that the term was attached to local legends of a giant race (compare the Anakim and Rephaim). Later Jewish literature, especially the **Book of the Watchers** (1 Enoch 1–36, 3rd century BCE), expanded these four verses into a full story of fallen angels.

### The Mesopotamian flood hero's boat

- In **Atrahasis**, the god Enki warns Atrahasis to tear down his house and build a boat, roof it, and seal it with bitumen.
- In **Gilgamesh** Tablet XI, Ea tells Utnapishtim to build a vessel whose width equals its length; the result is a cube of about 60 m (10 *nindan*) on each side, with seven decks, sealed with bitumen and oil.
- The **Ark Tablet**, an Old Babylonian tablet (c. 1900–1700 BCE) published by Irving Finkel of the British Museum in 2014, gives instructions for a giant round **coracle** made of rope and coated in bitumen, and says the animals entered "two by two."

The shared details (divine warning, specific measurements, a roof, bitumen sealing, animals boarding in pairs) show that Genesis 6 draws on the same tradition. The measurements differ because each version adapts the boat to its own imagined shape.

### Two flood sources begin

Chapter 6 contains the first clear doublet of the flood narrative. In 6:5–8, God is called YHWH and "regrets" making humans (the **non-Priestly** or J version). In 6:9–22, God is Elohim, Noah is introduced with a *toledot* heading, and the instructions specify two of every kind (the **Priestly** version). In 7:2–3, the non-Priestly source instead requires **seven pairs** of clean animals. The two versions run in parallel throughout chapters 6–9.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter accurately reflects ancient Near Eastern ideas of a divine council and of semi-divine heroes.
- Its boat-building instructions belong recognisably to the Mesopotamian flood tradition. Bitumen sealing (Hebrew *kofer*, Akkadian *kupru*) is an authentic Mesopotamian technique.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 6 | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Divine beings father a race of giants | No evidence; a mythological motif |
| A wooden vessel c. 135 m long, seaworthy in a global flood | The longest known wooden ships (around 100 m, built in the 19th century) needed iron bracing and still leaked badly |
| Two of every kind of land animal aboard | Millions of species, many with specialised habitats and diets, could not be gathered, housed, or fed |
| Human lifespan limited to 120 years (6:3) | Contradicted by the much longer lifespans in Genesis 11 after the flood |

## Assessment

Genesis 6 combines two very different traditions: a mythological fragment about divine–human unions and the opening of a flood story taken from Mesopotamia and divided between two Israelite sources. Neither can be read as history. The archaeological value of the chapter lies in showing how closely Israelite writers knew the Mesopotamian flood tradition, down to the use of bitumen and the animals entering in pairs.
