## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (non-historical genre: etiological myth).** Genesis 3 explains why humans die, why snakes crawl, why childbirth is painful, and why farming is hard labour. Stories that answer "why things are as they are" are called etiologies. No historical or archaeological test can apply to a talking serpent or a garden guarded by cherubim. The chapter's motifs, however, have clear ancient Near Eastern parallels.

## What the Text Claims

- A serpent, "more crafty than any other wild animal," persuades the woman to eat from the forbidden tree; the man also eats (3:1–6).
- Their eyes are opened, and they recognise their nakedness (3:7).
- God curses the serpent to crawl on its belly, decrees pain in childbirth and the man's rule over the woman, and curses the ground (3:14–19).
- The man names the woman Eve (*ḥawwah*), "mother of all living" (3:20).
- The humans are expelled so that they cannot eat from the tree of life; cherubim and a flaming sword guard the way (3:22–24).

## External Evidence

### Lost immortality in Mesopotamian myth

Two Mesopotamian stories turn on a human who comes close to immortality and loses it:

- **Adapa** (attested in a 14th-century BCE copy from the Amarna archive in Egypt, and in later Assyrian copies): the sage Adapa is offered the "bread of life" and "water of life" by the god Anu but refuses them, on the advice of his patron god Ea, who had warned him they would be food of death. He returns to earth mortal.
- **Gilgamesh**, Tablet XI: after the flood story, Gilgamesh obtains a plant that restores youth. While he bathes, **a snake** smells the plant and carries it off, sloughing its skin as it goes. Gilgamesh weeps, having lost his chance.

Both share with Genesis 3 the theme of humans losing access to life through a food or plant, and Gilgamesh adds a serpent as the agent. The Genesis version differs by making disobedience to a single God the cause.

### Serpents in the ancient Near East

Snakes were associated with wisdom, healing, fertility, and chaos across the region. Bronze serpent figurines are known from Late Bronze and Iron Age sites, including Timna, and the bronze serpent Nehushtan was worshipped in Jerusalem until Hezekiah destroyed it (2 Kgs 18:4). Genesis 3 presents the serpent simply as one of God's creatures, not as a deity. The later identification of the serpent with Satan appears only in Second Temple and early Christian literature (Wisdom 2:24; Revelation 12:9).

### Cherubim as guardians

The cherubim (*kerubim*) placed east of Eden (3:24) belong to the composite winged guardian figures common across the Near East: Assyrian human-headed winged bulls and lions (*lamassu*), and winged sphinxes guarding thrones and sacred spaces in Syria and Phoenicia. A Phoenician-style ivory carving of winged sphinxes from Samaria (9th–8th centuries BCE), and the cherub throne of the Jerusalem Temple (1 Kgs 6:23–28), show how Israelites imagined such figures. The Akkadian term *karibu* ("one who blesses"), used for intercessory figures, is often compared to the Hebrew word.

### Literary unity and source

Genesis 3 continues the Yahwist (non-Priestly) account of chapter 2. Its name for God (YHWH Elohim), its vivid storytelling, and its concern with the human condition are typical of that layer. Several scholars have noted that the story's themes of wisdom, transgression, and exile from the land would have resonated with Judeans of the late monarchic or exilic period, though the date of composition remains debated.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter uses imagery that fits the cultural world of the ancient Near East: winged guardians, sacred trees, serpents with special knowledge, and a divine council (3:22, "like one of us").
- The theme of humanity losing immortality matches well-documented Mesopotamian traditions.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 3 | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| Death enters the world through human disobedience | Death and predation existed for hundreds of millions of years before humans |
| Snakes condemned to crawl | Snakes evolved from legged reptiles long before humans; fossil snakes with vestigial hind limbs are known |
| Pain in childbirth as a punishment | Explained biologically by human pelvic anatomy and large brain size |
| Agriculture as the primal human condition (3:17–19, 23) | Humans lived as hunter-gatherers for most of their history; farming began c. 10,000 BCE |

## Assessment

Genesis 3 is a profound story about human limits, knowledge, and mortality. It belongs to the same family of ancient tales as Adapa and Gilgamesh's lost plant. It is not, and was probably not meant as, a historical report. Its usefulness for the historian lies in the cultural and religious imagery it shares with Israel's neighbours, and in showing how Israelite writers reworked those motifs.
