## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (religious narrative).** The binding of Isaac is a theological story about faith and sacrifice, not a historical report. Its location cannot be identified with confidence, and nothing in it can be checked against external evidence. What history can illuminate is its religious background: the practice of child sacrifice in the ancient Levant and Israel's later rejection of it.

## What the Text Claims

- God commands Abraham to offer Isaac as a burnt offering "on one of the mountains" in the **land of Moriah** (22:1–2).
- An angel stops Abraham; a ram is offered instead (22:9–13).
- Abraham names the place "YHWH will see/provide," explained by the saying "on the mount of YHWH it shall be seen/provided" (22:14).
- News comes of the children of Abraham's brother **Nahor**, including **Bethuel**, father of Rebekah (22:20–24).

## External Evidence

### Moriah

"Moriah" appears elsewhere only in 2 Chronicles 3:1, which places Solomon's Temple on "Mount Moriah." This identification is centuries later than most of Genesis and was probably intended to connect the Temple with Abraham. The Samaritans locate the event on **Mount Gerizim**, and the ancient versions (Septuagint, Peshitta) render the name differently. The three-day journey from Beersheba (22:4) fits Jerusalem loosely but proves nothing.

### Child sacrifice in the ancient Levant

- The **Hebrew Bible** itself condemns child sacrifice repeatedly (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31; Jer 7:31; 19:5), showing that it was practised in Judah, especially at the Tophet in the Valley of Hinnom.
- According to 2 Kings 3:27, King **Mesha of Moab** sacrificed his firstborn son on the city wall during a siege. Mesha himself is known from the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE), though the stele does not mention this act.
- At **Carthage** and other Phoenician colonies, *tophet* precincts contained thousands of urns with the burnt bones of infants and young animals, together with dedicatory stelae. Most specialists accept that these were sacrifices, though a minority argue they were cemeteries for children who died naturally.

Many critical scholars read Genesis 22 as an etiology of the substitution of animals for children, or as a story that reaffirms God's claim on the firstborn (Exod 22:29; 34:20) while showing that the claim is redeemed. Jon Levenson argued that the story assumes, rather than rejects, the idea that the firstborn son belongs to God.

### The genealogy of Nahor

The list of Nahor's twelve sons (22:20–24) includes names associated with Aramean and north Arabian groups (Uz, Buz, Kemuel "father of Aram"). It reflects Israel's view of its kinship with Aramean peoples. As noted in chapter 24, the Arameans as a people are not attested before about 1100 BCE.

## Points of Agreement

- Child sacrifice was a real, though exceptional, practice in the Iron Age Levant.
- Burnt offerings on hilltop altars were the normal form of sacrifice in the region.

## Points of Conflict

- The location is uncertain and the identification with the Temple Mount is late.
- The story's style is spare and literary; the dialogue and the angel's intervention are not historical data.
- The vocabulary of the ending (22:15–18) is widely seen as a later expansion, repeating promises in language similar to Deuteronomy.

## Assessment

Genesis 22 is one of the most powerful narratives in the Bible, and it is usually assigned to the Elohist (E) strand with Yahwistic additions. As history, it can be neither confirmed nor refuted. Its importance for the historian lies in what it shows about Israelite debates over the meaning of sacrifice and the firstborn in the Iron Age, debates confirmed by the prophets' attacks on child sacrifice and by archaeological finds from the wider Phoenician world.
