## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (Babel story); mixed (place names in the genealogy).** The Babel story is an etiology for the diversity of languages, set against the background of Babylon's great ziggurat. Its building details are authentic, but the idea that all languages arose in one event is contradicted by linguistics. The genealogy of Shem to Terah uses names that match places around Harran, while "Ur of the Chaldeans" contains a clear anachronism.

## What the Text Claims

- All the earth has one language. People settle in **Shinar**, build with **baked brick and bitumen**, and plan a city and a tower "with its top in the heavens" (11:1–4).
- God confuses their language and scatters them; the city is called **Babel** because there God "confused" (*balal*) the language (11:5–9).
- A genealogy runs from Shem through Arpachshad, Shelah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, and Nahor to **Terah**, with lifespans declining from 600 to 205 years (11:10–32).
- Terah's sons are Abram, Nahor, and Haran; Haran dies in **Ur of the Chaldeans** (11:27–28).
- Terah leads Abram, Sarai, and Lot from Ur towards Canaan, but they settle in **Harran**, where Terah dies (11:31–32).

## External Evidence

### Building with brick and bitumen

Genesis 11:3 notes that the builders used "brick for stone, and bitumen for mortar." This is an accurate observation of Mesopotamian construction. Southern Mesopotamia has almost no building stone, and monumental buildings were made of mud brick faced with **kiln-fired brick** set in bitumen. In Canaan, by contrast, stone was the normal building material. The writer is explaining a foreign practice to a Levantine audience.

### Ziggurats and Etemenanki

Every major Mesopotamian city had a stepped temple tower, a **ziggurat**. The ziggurat of Babylon, **Etemenanki** ("House of the foundation of heaven and earth"), stood next to Marduk's temple Esagila. It was rebuilt by Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar II in the late 7th and early 6th centuries BCE. Nebuchadnezzar's inscriptions speak of raising its top "to rival the heavens," language strikingly close to 11:4. A stele now in the Schøyen Collection, published by Andrew George, depicts Nebuchadnezzar beside the tower. Herodotus (1.181) describes a tower of eight stages at Babylon. Judean exiles in Babylon would have seen the restored ziggurat. Many scholars think the story was shaped, or at least sharpened, in that setting as a satire on Babylonian imperial pride.

### Babel and "confusion"

The name *Babel* means "gate of god" in Akkadian (*bāb-ili*). The link with Hebrew *balal*, "to confuse," is a pun, not an etymology.

### A Sumerian parallel on languages

The Sumerian epic **Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta** (early 2nd millennium BCE) contains a passage, the "spell of Nudimmud," describing a time when all peoples addressed the god Enlil "in one tongue," and the god Enki then changed the speech in their mouths. Its interpretation is debated (it may describe a future unity rather than a past one), but it shows that Mesopotamian literature also reflected on the origin of language diversity.

### Linguistics

Historical linguistics shows that languages diversify gradually from common ancestors over thousands of years. Language families such as Afro-Asiatic, Indo-European, and Sino-Tibetan are far older than any biblical date for Babel, and some, like Sumerian, are unrelated to any other known language. No evidence supports a single sudden diversification.

### The line of Shem and the Harran region

Several names in the genealogy correspond to towns in the region of **Harran** in northern Mesopotamia, known from Assyrian and earlier texts: **Serug** (Assyrian *Sarugi*), **Nahor** (*Nahur*, attested in the Mari letters of the 18th century BCE), and **Terah** (perhaps *Til-ša-turaḫi*). **Harran** itself was an important city on the trade route, and was attested from the 3rd millennium onward. Harran and Ur were both major centres of the moon god Sin. Nabonidus, the last Neo-Babylonian king, restored Sin's temple at Harran in the 6th century BCE. Some scholars suggest that the connection between Ur and Harran in Genesis reflects this cult link.

### "Ur of the Chaldeans"

Ur (Tell el-Muqayyar), excavated by Leonard Woolley in 1922–1934, was one of the great cities of Sumer, famous for its royal cemetery (c. 2600–2400 BCE) and its Ur III dynasty (c. 2112–2004 BCE). However, the **Chaldeans** (*Kaldu*) first appear in Assyrian records in the **9th century BCE**, and they became the ruling dynasty of Babylon only in the late 7th century BCE. Calling Ur "of the Chaldeans" is therefore an anachronism for any 2nd-millennium Abraham. The Septuagint reads "the land of the Chaldeans." Cyrus Gordon proposed a northern "Ur" near Harran, but this has not won wide acceptance.

## Points of Agreement

- Accurate description of Mesopotamian building materials.
- Real ziggurats, including a colossal one at Babylon, matching the story's image.
- Names in Shem's genealogy correspond to genuine towns in the Harran region.
- Ur and Harran were real cities linked by trade and the cult of Sin.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 11 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| One human language until Babel | Languages diversified gradually over tens of thousands of years |
| "Babel" from "confuse" | The Akkadian name means "gate of god" |
| Ur "of the Chaldeans" | The Chaldeans appear only in the 9th century BCE |
| Lifespans of several hundred years after the flood | Biologically impossible |

## Assessment

Genesis 11 marks the transition from myth to ancestral legend. The Babel story is an etiology for linguistic diversity and a critique of Babylonian imperial ambition, probably shaped against the background of Etemenanki. The genealogy preserves real north Mesopotamian toponyms, which may reflect old traditions of Aramean origins. The description of Ur as Chaldean points to a writer of the Neo-Babylonian period or later.
