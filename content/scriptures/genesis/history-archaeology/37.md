## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Chapter 37 opens the Joseph story, which most scholars read as a crafted court novella rather than a historical report. Its trade caravan and its double account of who sold Joseph point to a literary work of the Iron Age, not a record from the Middle Bronze Age.

## What the Text Claims

- Jacob favours Joseph and gives him a special robe (*ketonet passim*, 37:3). Joseph dreams that his family will bow to him (37:5–11).
- His brothers, pasturing near Shechem and Dothan, throw him into a dry cistern (37:12–24).
- A caravan of **Ishmaelites** from Gilead, with camels carrying gum, balm and resin to Egypt, passes by. Joseph is sold for **twenty pieces of silver** (37:25–28).
- In the same verses **Midianites** pull him from the pit, and in 37:36 the Midianites sell him to Potiphar in Egypt (contrast 39:1, where the Ishmaelites sell him).
- The brothers dip the robe in goat's blood, and Jacob mourns (37:31–35).

## External Evidence

### Camels and the incense caravan

The caravan carries *nekhot*, *tsori* and *lot* (gum tragacanth, balm and labdanum). The overland trade in such aromatics, using domesticated camels, is best documented from the 8th–7th centuries BCE under Assyrian control of the Arabian routes. Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef's study of camel bones in the Aravah Valley places the introduction of domesticated camels into the southern Levant around the late 10th century BCE. Finkelstein and Silberman and Redford take the caravan as a sign of Iron Age composition. Conservative scholars reply that camels were known in small numbers earlier.

### The price of a slave

Kenneth Kitchen argues that twenty shekels matches the average price of a slave in the early second millennium (as in the Laws of Hammurabi and Mari texts). He notes that prices rose to about thirty shekels later in the second millennium and higher in the first. This is the strongest conservative argument for an early setting. Critics answer that prices varied by age, sex and region, and that twenty shekels also matches the valuation of a boy aged 5–20 in Leviticus 27:5, a text written for Israelite readers.

### Dothan and the robe

Tell Dothan, excavated by Joseph Free in 1953–1964, was occupied in both the Bronze and Iron Ages, so the place name does not fix a date. The *ketonet passim* appears elsewhere only for royal daughters (2 Sam 13:18). The Beni Hasan tomb painting of Khnumhotep II (c. 1890 BCE) shows Asiatics in brightly patterned clothes, which shows that striking garments were associated with Levantine people. It does not illustrate Joseph specifically.

## Points of Agreement

- Shechem, Dothan and the route from Gilead down the coast to Egypt are realistic geography. The Dothan valley sits on a natural trade route.
- Dry cisterns, herding far from home and selling people into slavery in Egypt are all plausible in many periods. Papyrus Brooklyn 35.1446 lists many Asiatic household servants in a 13th-dynasty Egyptian estate.
- Twenty shekels is a realistic price, if not a decisive date marker.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 37 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| Ishmaelites buy Joseph (37:25–28; 39:1) | Midianites sell him to Potiphar (37:28, 36). Two versions are interwoven, usually assigned to J and E |
| Reuben rescues him (37:21–22, 29) | Judah proposes the sale (37:26–27). Rival heroes from different tribal perspectives |
| Camel caravans with incense | Best fit for the Iron II Arabian trade, not the patriarchal era |
| Ishmaelites and Midianites as established peoples | Both are eponymous descendants of Abraham, born only two generations earlier in the story's own chronology |

## Assessment

The Joseph story has a unity and suspense unusual in Genesis, and Gerhard von Rad and Claus Westermann saw it as a wisdom-style novella. The doublets in chapter 37, the camel caravan and the anachronistic peoples all suggest a story composed or reworked in the Israelite monarchy or later, possibly with a northern (Joseph-tribe) origin. A real memory of Semitic people sold into Egypt is plausible in a general sense, since such movement is well attested in many periods. But nothing in the chapter can be tied to a specific historical event.
