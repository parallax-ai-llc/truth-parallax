## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Mixed.** The chapter is an archive-like collection of Edomite genealogies, clan chiefs and a king list. Some names may preserve genuine Edomite tradition, but the material is arranged from an Israelite viewpoint, and verse 31 shows that it was written after kings ruled in Israel.

## What the Text Claims

- Esau marries Canaanite and Ishmaelite wives and moves to the hill country of Seir (36:1–8).
- His sons and grandsons, including Teman, Kenaz and Amalek, become "chiefs" (*alluphim*) of Edom (36:9–19, 40–43).
- The Horite chiefs, "the inhabitants of the land," are listed separately (36:20–30).
- Eight kings "reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31–39). None succeeds his father, and each has a different capital, including Bela son of Beor of Dinhabah, Jobab of Bozrah and Hadad son of Bedad, who "defeated Midian in the country of Moab."

## External Evidence

### Edom in Egyptian and Assyrian sources

- **Papyrus Anastasi VI** (late 13th century BCE) records Shasu tribes of Edom being let past an Egyptian fortress in the Wadi Tumilat to water their flocks. Edom then appears as a land of pastoral groups, not a kingdom.
- **Assyrian inscriptions** name kings of Edom from the late 8th century onward: Qaus-malaku paid tribute to Tiglath-pileser III, Aya-ramu to Sennacherib and Qaus-gabri to Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal. None of the Genesis names appears in these lists. Their theophoric element, the god Qaus, is also absent from Genesis 36.

### Archaeology of Edom

- **Bozrah (Buseirah)**, excavated by Crystal-M. Bennett, became a major fortified capital only in the late 8th–6th centuries BCE.
- **Faynan / Khirbat en-Nahas.** Thomas Levy and Mohammad Najjar's radiocarbon dates show large-scale copper production in lowland Edom in the 12th–9th centuries BCE. Erez Ben-Yosef's work at Timna points the same way. This has revived the debate: some argue for an early, mostly nomadic Edomite polity, while others hold that a true kingdom arose only under Assyrian influence.

### "Bela son of Beor"

The first king, Bela son of Beor (36:32), recalls Balaam son of Beor (Num 22–24). Balaam son of Beor is also named in the plaster inscription from Deir Alla in the Jordan Valley (8th century BCE). This shows that "son of Beor" was a figure of Transjordanian tradition, but it does not confirm a king Bela.

## Points of Agreement

- Teman, Bozrah and Seir are genuine Edomite place names. "Teman" also appears in the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions ("Yahweh of Teman").
- The non-dynastic succession may reflect a loose tribal society with chiefs or local rulers rather than an invented royal line.
- The close link between Edom and the Horites/Seir and the inclusion of Amalek and Kenaz match other biblical memories of southern clans being absorbed or related.
- Recent copper-industry evidence makes some form of organised Edomite society before the 8th century more plausible than it seemed in the 1980s.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 36 | Historical record |
|------------|-------------------|
| Kings rule Edom before Israel's monarchy | Most excavated Edomite towns and fortresses, including Bozrah, are 8th–6th century |
| Eight named kings | No Genesis king appears in Egyptian or Assyrian records |
| "Before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31) | Presupposes Saul or David. The text was written in the monarchy or later |
| Horites as earlier inhabitants of Seir | "Horite" is often linked with Hurrians, who are not known in southern Transjordan. Others read it as "cave-dwellers" |

## Assessment

The Edomite lists are some of the most document-like material in Genesis. They may draw on Edomite or Judahite records, and the absence of father-to-son succession gives them a certain authenticity. Still, verse 31 dates the composition to the monarchy at the earliest, and the archaeological peak of Edomite statehood is in the Assyrian period. Critical scholars such as John Bartlett think the lists reflect 8th–6th century knowledge projected back into the past. The Faynan copper evidence leaves open the possibility that some names echo earlier chiefs. The chapter as a whole serves Israel's claim that Edom was its "brother" nation, older yet second in God's favour.
