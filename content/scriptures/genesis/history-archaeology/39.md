## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The story of Potiphar's wife is a well-known folk motif with a close Egyptian literary parallel. Its Egyptian setting is broadly plausible, but the name Potiphar and the story pattern point to literary composition, not to a remembered event.

## What the Text Claims

- Joseph is bought by Potiphar, "an officer (*saris*) of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard, an Egyptian" (39:1).
- Joseph prospers and is put in charge of all Potiphar's house (39:2–6).
- Potiphar's wife tries to seduce him, and when he refuses she accuses him of attempted rape, keeping his garment as evidence (39:7–18).
- Potiphar puts Joseph in the royal prison, where the keeper puts him in charge of the prisoners (39:19–23).

## External Evidence

### The Tale of Two Brothers

The Egyptian *Tale of Two Brothers*, preserved in Papyrus D'Orbiney (British Museum EA 10183), was copied in the 19th dynasty, around 1200 BCE. In it, the wife of the elder brother Anubis tries to seduce the younger brother Bata. When he refuses, she pretends to have been assaulted, and Anubis tries to kill Bata. The parallel with Genesis 39 is close in structure: a trusted young man, a married woman's proposition, refusal, a false accusation and the husband's anger. Similar stories appear in Greek myth (Phaedra and Hippolytus, Bellerophon), so the motif is international. It shows that the Joseph writer used a familiar story pattern with Egyptian roots.

### The name Potiphar

Potiphar (and Potiphera in 41:45) is generally explained as Egyptian *Pa-di-pa-Ra*, "the one whom Ra has given." Names of the form *Pa-di-*[god] are rare before the Third Intermediate Period and become common from the 21st–22nd dynasties and especially in the Saite period (7th–6th centuries BCE). The exact form *Pa-di-pa-Ra* is known only from late sources. Redford used this as a key argument for a late date. Kitchen and Hoffmeier respond that the name might have been updated in transmission, which is possible, but it means the name cannot support an early date either.

### Titles and household slaves

- *Saris* later means "eunuch," but it comes from Akkadian *ša rēši*, "royal official." Its use for an Egyptian officer reflects Assyrian-era Hebrew usage.
- Joseph's role "over the house" corresponds to the common Egyptian title *imy-r pr*, steward or overseer of the house.
- **Papyrus Brooklyn 35.1446** (13th dynasty, c. 18th century BCE) lists nearly 80 servants in an Egyptian household, many of them Asiatics with Semitic names given Egyptian names. This confirms that Semitic household slaves were a real feature of Middle Kingdom Egypt.

## Points of Agreement

- Semitic slaves in elite Egyptian households are well documented, especially in the late Middle Kingdom.
- A trusted slave rising to manage a household is realistic. The title of steward is genuinely Egyptian.
- The story's Egyptian setting is coloured by authentic customs at a general level.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 39 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| The name Potiphar | Name type typical of 1st-millennium, especially Saite, Egypt |
| *Saris* "officer/eunuch" | Assyrian-derived Hebrew term |
| Seduction and false accusation | A widespread literary motif with a close Egyptian parallel |
| Royal prison under the "captain of the guard" | No Egyptian source describes an arrangement like this, though prisons and labour institutions existed |

## Assessment

Genesis 39 shows how the Joseph writer drew on Egyptian colour and international story patterns. The *Tale of Two Brothers* proves that the motif existed in Egypt centuries before most dates proposed for the composition of Genesis. The writer may have known it directly or through common oral tradition. The name Potiphar fits best in the 1st millennium BCE, in line with Redford's Saite-to-Persian dating. Conservative scholars such as Hoffmeier stress the authentic Middle Kingdom background of Asiatic servants, but this background is general and does not establish a historical Joseph.
