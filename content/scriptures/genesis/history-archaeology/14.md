## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** Genesis 14 is the only chapter that places Abram in international politics, naming four eastern kings who invade Transjordan. Despite more than a century of searching, none of the kings has been identified in Mesopotamian or Elamite records, and the campaign it describes has no historical parallel. The chapter's names have an authentic ancient flavour, but most critical scholars regard it as a late literary composition.

## What the Text Claims

- **Amraphel** king of Shinar, **Arioch** king of Ellasar, **Chedorlaomer** king of Elam, and **Tidal** king of Goiim make war on five kings of the cities of the plain, including Sodom and Gomorrah (14:1–2).
- After twelve years of subjection to Chedorlaomer, the five kings rebel; the eastern kings march through Transjordan, defeating the Rephaim, Zuzim, Emim, and Horites, and then the Amalekites and Amorites (14:4–7).
- They defeat the five kings in the Valley of Siddim, "full of bitumen pits," and capture Lot (14:8–12).
- "Abram the **Hebrew**" pursues them with **318** trained men as far as **Dan**, defeats them, and rescues Lot (14:13–16).
- **Melchizedek**, king of **Salem** and priest of **El Elyon** ("God Most High"), blesses Abram, who gives him a tenth of everything (14:18–20).

## External Evidence

### The four kings

| Genesis 14 | Proposed identification | Status |
|------------|-------------------------|--------|
| Amraphel of Shinar | Hammurabi of Babylon (18th century BCE) | Abandoned; the names do not match linguistically |
| Arioch of Ellasar | *Arriwuk*, a name attested at Mari and Nuzi; Ellasar perhaps Larsa | Name type authentic, king unknown |
| Chedorlaomer of Elam | Elamite-type name *Kutir-Lagamar* ("servant of the god Lagamar") | Name type authentic, no such king known |
| Tidal of Goiim | Hittite royal name *Tudhaliya*; "Goiim" means "nations" | Several Hittite kings bore the name, none fits |

The names are plausible for the 2nd millennium BCE, and conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that coalitions of eastern kings fit the politics of the Old Babylonian (Mari) period, c. 2000–1700 BCE. Critical scholars reply that no known period saw Elam lead a coalition including Babylon and the Hittites, and that Elam never controlled Transjordan.

### The "Chedorlaomer texts"

Late Babylonian tablets in the British Museum (the "Spartoli tablets"), copied in the Persian or Hellenistic period, mention figures with names that early scholars read as resembling Chedorlaomer, Arioch, and Tidal. Michael Astour argued that both these texts and Genesis 14 drew on legends about early enemies of Babylon. The links are disputed and do not provide historical confirmation of the campaign.

### Anachronisms

- **Dan** (14:14): the city was named Dan only after the tribe of Dan captured Laish (Judg 18:29; Josh 19:47). Before that it was called Laish, a name known from Egyptian execration texts and the Mari archives.
- **"Abram the Hebrew"** (14:13): this is how foreigners refer to Israelites elsewhere in the Bible. Comparisons with the *ḫabiru* of 2nd-millennium texts are possible but not straightforward.
- **Explanatory glosses** such as "Bela (that is, Zoar)" and "the Valley of Siddim (that is, the Salt Sea)" (14:2–3, 7, 8, 17) update old names for a later audience.

### The number 318

Abram's 318 men match the number of servants in Eliezer's name: in gematria, where Hebrew letters have numerical values, the letters of *Eliezer* (15:2) total 318. Many scholars see this as a sign of scribal learning rather than an eyewitness figure.

### Melchizedek and El Elyon

**Salem** is identified with Jerusalem in Psalm 76:2. Jerusalem existed in the Middle Bronze Age (it is named in Egyptian execration texts) and was ruled by **Abdi-Heba** in the Amarna period (14th century BCE). **El Elyon** combines two divine names. The 8th-century BCE Aramaic **Sefire treaty** lists "El and Elyon" among the divine witnesses, which shows these were once distinct deities. Genesis 14:22 identifies El Elyon with YHWH. Many scholars see the Melchizedek scene (14:18–20) as a later insertion that legitimises the Jerusalem priesthood and the tithe, linking David's capital to Abraham.

### Literary character

The chapter stands apart from the surrounding narratives in style, presenting Abram as a military leader. Scholars such as J. A. Emerton have argued for several layers, while Van Seters and others view it as a late composition, perhaps of the Persian period, using archaic names to create an ancient atmosphere.

## Points of Agreement

- Personal names of the eastern kings have authentic 2nd-millennium forms.
- The bitumen pits (14:10) match the Dead Sea region, where bitumen was collected in antiquity.
- Jerusalem existed as a city-state before Israel controlled it.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 14 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Four eastern kings campaign in Transjordan | No such campaign or kings are known |
| An Elamite overlord of the cities of the plain | Elam never controlled this region |
| Pursuit to Dan | The name Dan postdates the Israelite settlement |
| Abram defeats imperial armies with 318 men | Militarily implausible; the number matches the gematria of Eliezer |

## Assessment

Genesis 14 is a literary composition that uses ancient-sounding names, perhaps drawn from Mesopotamian traditions, to portray Abram as a hero among kings and to connect him with Jerusalem's priesthood. The authentic name forms show that the writer had access to old onomastic material, but the campaign itself has no historical support and contains clear anachronisms.
