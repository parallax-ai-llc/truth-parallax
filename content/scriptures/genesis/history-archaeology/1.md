## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (non-historical genre: cosmogonic myth).** Genesis 1 is a carefully structured account of origins, not a record of observed events. Read literally, it conflicts with modern cosmology, geology, and biology. Read in its ancient setting, it is a theological response to Mesopotamian and Egyptian creation traditions.

## What the Text Claims

- In the beginning the earth is formless, darkness covers the **deep** (*tehom*), and God's spirit or wind moves over the waters (1:1–2).
- God creates by command over six days: light (1:3–5), a **firmament** (*raqia*) separating waters above from waters below (1:6–8), dry land and plants (1:9–13), sun, moon, and stars (1:14–19), sea creatures and birds (1:20–23), land animals and humankind (1:24–31).
- Humans are made "in the image of God," male and female, and given dominion (1:26–28).
- The seventh day of rest follows in 2:1–3, completing the scheme.

## External Evidence

### Composition: the Priestly source

Genesis 1:1–2:4a is the classic example of the **Priestly (P)** source. Its vocabulary ("be fruitful and multiply," "according to their kinds," "these are the generations of"), its interest in order and classification, and its climax in the Sabbath are all typical of P. Most critical scholars date P to the exile or early Persian period (6th–5th centuries BCE), when the Sabbath became a central marker of Jewish identity. The chapter is therefore younger than the second creation account in 2:4b–3:24.

### Mesopotamian parallels: Enuma Elish

The Babylonian epic **Enuma Elish**, recited at the New Year festival in Babylon, describes how Marduk defeats Tiamat, the salt-water deep, splits her body in two, and sets half above as the sky to hold back the waters. He then fixes the stars and the moon, and humans are created to serve the gods. Points of contact with Genesis 1 include:

- The primeval watery deep: Hebrew *tehom* is cognate with Akkadian *tiamtu* / Tiamat.
- Creation by dividing the waters into upper and lower parts.
- Heavenly bodies set in place to mark times and seasons.
- The creation of humankind as the climax.

The differences are equally important. In Genesis there is no battle and no rival god; the deep is impersonal, and the sun and moon are not even named, perhaps to avoid their divine names. Many scholars read Genesis 1 as a deliberate polemic against Babylonian religion, written by exiles who knew it.

### Egyptian parallels

The **Memphite Theology** (preserved on the Shabaka Stone, c. 700 BCE, possibly copying an older text) describes the god Ptah creating through his heart and tongue, that is, by thought and word. Egyptian cosmogonies also begin with a primeval watery chaos (Nun) from which land emerges. Creation by divine speech in Genesis 1 has its closest analogue here.

### The Bible's own echoes of a combat myth

Other biblical texts preserve a version in which God defeats a sea monster at creation: Psalm 74:13–17, Psalm 89:9–10, Isaiah 51:9, and Job 26:12–13 name Rahab or Leviathan. Genesis 1:21 mentions the "great sea monsters" (*tanninim*) only as creatures God made. Hermann Gunkel (1895) argued that Genesis 1 deliberately demythologised this older combat tradition, which is also known from the Ugaritic Baal cycle.

### The ancient cosmology

The *raqia* (1:6–8) is a solid vault holding back the waters above, with windows through which rain falls (7:11; 8:2). This three-tiered universe of heavens, earth, and underworld waters is the standard cosmology of the ancient Near East. It is not compatible with modern astronomy.

## Points of Agreement

- The chapter accurately reflects the cosmology and religious debates of its own time and region.
- Its literary shape (days 1–3 forming realms, days 4–6 filling them) is a recognisable ancient technique of ordered composition.
- Some readers note a loose correspondence between Genesis and science in that the universe had a beginning and life appeared in water before land. These are general resemblances rather than evidence.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 1 | Scientific evidence |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Creation in six days | Universe c. 13.8 billion years old; earth c. 4.5 billion years |
| Light and "days" before the sun (1:3–5, 14–19) | The sun predates the earth and its day–night cycle |
| Plants (day 3) before the sun (day 4) | Photosynthetic life depends on sunlight and appears long after the sun formed |
| Birds and sea creatures (day 5) before land animals (day 6) | Land animals predate birds by over 100 million years |
| Each kind created separately | Common descent through evolution is supported by fossil, genetic, and anatomical evidence |
| A solid firmament holding back waters | No such structure exists |

## Assessment

Genesis 1 cannot be assessed as history because it does not belong to the genre of history. It is an exilic-era Priestly composition that takes up the shared Near Eastern picture of a world formed from primeval waters and reshapes it around a single God who creates without conflict and rests on the seventh day. Attempts to read it as a scientific record (young-earth or "day-age" concordism) are rejected by mainstream biblical scholarship and science alike. Its real historical value lies in what it reveals about the Judean community that wrote it and the Babylonian world it was answering.
