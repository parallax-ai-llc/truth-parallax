## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (Priestly covenant text).** Genesis 17 is a theological and legal text that grounds the practice of circumcision in a covenant with Abraham. It has no narrative events that archaeology could test. Its language and concerns are those of the Priestly source, and most scholars date it to the exile or early Persian period, when circumcision became a key marker of Jewish identity. The practice itself is much older and was shared with other peoples.

## What the Text Claims

- When Abram is 99, God appears as **El Shaddai** and makes an "everlasting covenant" with him (17:1–7).
- Abram's name is changed to **Abraham**, "for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations" (17:5).
- God promises the land of Canaan as a perpetual possession (17:8).
- Every male, including slaves, is to be **circumcised** on the eighth day as the sign of the covenant; an uncircumcised male "shall be cut off from his people" (17:9–14).
- Sarai's name becomes **Sarah**; she will bear a son, **Isaac**, at 90 (17:15–19). Abraham laughs (17:17), a play on Isaac's name (*yitsḥaq*, "he laughs").
- Ishmael will become a great nation with twelve princes, but the covenant passes through Isaac (17:20–21).
- Abraham, Ishmael (13 years old), and all the men of his household are circumcised that day (17:23–27).

## External Evidence

### Circumcision in the ancient Near East

Circumcision was practised by several of Israel's neighbours and was not unique to Israel:

- **Egypt**: a relief in the tomb of **Ankhmahor** at Saqqara (6th Dynasty, c. 2300 BCE) depicts the circumcision of young men. Herodotus (2.104) later claimed the Egyptians, Colchians, and Ethiopians practised it from earliest times and that the Phoenicians and "Syrians of Palestine" learned it from Egypt.
- **The Levant**: Jeremiah 9:25–26 lists Egypt, Judah, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and desert-dwelling Arabs as circumcised peoples.
- **Uncircumcised peoples** known to Israel included the **Philistines** (Judg 14:3; 1 Sam 18:25–27) and, as far as the evidence shows, the Mesopotamian peoples among whom the exiles later lived.

The distinctive Israelite feature is circumcision on the **eighth day** after birth rather than at puberty or marriage, as among most other peoples.

### Why circumcision became central: the exile

During the Babylonian exile, Judeans lived among peoples who did not practise circumcision. Many scholars argue that circumcision and Sabbath observance therefore became defining signs of identity in this period. Genesis 17 is the foundational text for this. Its strict penalty ("cut off from his people") and its inclusion of foreign slaves match the Priestly concern for the boundaries of the community. Later, under Antiochus IV (1 Macc 1:48, 60–61), circumcision became a point of fierce conflict.

### The Priestly source

Genesis 17 is a classic Priestly chapter. Its markers include the divine name **El Shaddai** (compare Exod 6:3, where God says he appeared to the patriarchs as El Shaddai), the "everlasting covenant" (*berit olam*), "be fruitful and multiply," precise ages, and the formula "to be God to you and to your offspring." It parallels the non-Priestly covenant of chapter 15 and the separate announcement of Isaac's birth in 18:1–15, where Sarah rather than Abraham laughs. This duplication is a standard example of multiple sources.

### El Shaddai

The meaning of *Shaddai* is uncertain. Proposals include a link with Akkadian *šadû* ("mountain"), giving "God of the mountain," or with the Hebrew word for breasts (*shadayim*), suggesting fertility (as in Gen 49:25). The **Deir Alla** inscription (Transjordan, c. 800 BCE) mentions *šdyn*, divine beings associated with the gods of the seer Balaam, which shows the term was in use in the region in the Iron Age.

### The names Abram and Abraham

*Abram* ("the father is exalted") belongs to a common West Semitic name type. Names of the form *Abi-ram* or similar are attested in Akkadian texts of both the 2nd and 1st millennia BCE, so the name itself cannot date Abraham. *Abraham* is probably a dialectal variant of the same name; the explanation "father of a multitude" (*av hamon*) is a folk etymology. *Sarai* and *Sarah* are both forms meaning "princess" (compare Akkadian *šarratu*, "queen"). The renaming theme is theological: a new name marks a new status.

### Ishmael's twelve princes

The promise of twelve princes for Ishmael (17:20) anticipates the list of Ishmaelite tribes in 25:13–16, which, as discussed for Genesis 16, reflects North Arabian groups known from Assyrian sources of the 8th–7th centuries BCE.

## Points of Agreement

- Circumcision was a genuine ancient practice in Egypt and the southern Levant.
- The covenant and name-change themes are consistent with ancient Near Eastern ideas of royal grant and status change.
- The name Abram belongs to a well-attested Semitic name type.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 17 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Circumcision begins with Abraham | Practised in Egypt by the late 3rd millennium BCE and shared by several Levantine peoples |
| "Abraham" means "father of a multitude" | A folk etymology; probably a dialectal variant of Abram |
| One announcement of Isaac's birth | Another version in 18:1–15 (non-Priestly) |
| A 90-year-old woman gives birth | Biologically impossible; a theological motif |

## Assessment

Genesis 17 is best read as a Priestly charter for a central identity practice. It projects the significance of circumcision, which became especially important during and after the exile, back onto the founding ancestor. The custom itself is ancient and well attested among Israel's neighbours, but the chapter's language, theology, and legal concerns belong to the 6th–5th centuries BCE.
