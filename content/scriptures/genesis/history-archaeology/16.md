## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (unverifiable family story with an ethnic etiology).** The custom of a childless wife providing a slave woman to bear children is well attested in ancient Near Eastern documents across many centuries. The story itself cannot be verified. Its depiction of Ishmael as the ancestor of a wild, desert-dwelling people reflects the Arab tribes known to Israel in the 1st millennium BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Sarai, childless, gives her Egyptian slave **Hagar** to Abram as a wife, saying, "it may be that I shall obtain children by her" (16:1–3).
- When Hagar conceives, she looks on her mistress with contempt; Sarai treats her harshly and Hagar flees (16:4–6).
- The angel of the LORD finds her by a spring on the way to **Shur** and tells her to return; her son will be called **Ishmael** ("God hears") and will be "a wild ass of a man," living in conflict with his kin (16:7–12).
- Hagar names God *El-roi* ("God of seeing"), and the well is called **Beer-lahai-roi**, between **Kadesh and Bered** (16:13–14).
- Ishmael is born when Abram is 86 (16:15–16).

## External Evidence

### Surrogate motherhood in ancient law and contracts

Several ancient texts describe arrangements like Sarai's:

- **Code of Hammurabi** (c. 1750 BCE), §§144–147: if a *nadītu* priestess, who was not permitted to bear children, gives her husband a slave woman who bears children, the husband may not take a second wife. If the slave then claims equality with her mistress, she may be marked as a slave again but not sold. The parallel with Hagar's contempt and Sarai's harsh treatment is close.
- **Old Assyrian marriage contracts** (19th century BCE) from Kanesh in Anatolia include clauses that if the wife does not bear a child within a set period, she will buy a slave woman for her husband.
- **Nuzi** (15th–14th centuries BCE): a marriage contract stipulates that if the wife does not bear children, she must provide a slave woman for her husband, and the children will belong to her.
- A **Neo-Assyrian marriage contract** from Nimrud (7th century BCE) contains a similar provision.

Mid-20th-century scholars treated the Nuzi parallel as evidence that the patriarchal stories came from the 2nd millennium. John Van Seters showed that the same custom appears in 1st-millennium documents, including the Neo-Assyrian contract. The custom therefore cannot date the story.

### Hagar the Egyptian

Egyptian slaves and servants in Levantine households are plausible in many periods. The name *Hagar* is often linked to the Arabic root *hjr* ("to flee," "to migrate"), and later Arab tradition connected it with the *Hagarites* (Hagrites) of 1 Chronicles 5:10, 19–20 and Psalm 83:6, a group in the eastern desert. Paul later used Hagar allegorically (Gal 4:24–25). No extra-biblical source mentions her.

### Ishmael and the Ishmaelites

Ishmael is presented as the ancestor of Arabian tribes. Genesis 25:13–15 lists his sons, including **Nebaioth**, **Kedar**, **Adbeel**, **Dumah**, and **Tema**. These names correspond to North Arabian peoples and oases attested in Assyrian sources of the **8th–7th centuries BCE**:

- **Qedar** (Kedar) appears frequently in the annals of Tiglath-pileser III, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Ashurbanipal as a powerful tribal confederation.
- **Nabayati** (Nebaioth) and **Idiba'il** (Adbeel) also appear in Assyrian texts.
- **Adummatu** (Dumah, modern Dumat al-Jandal) and **Tayma** (Tema) were major oases.

The 2nd-millennium sources do not mention any of these groups. Ernst Axel Knauf and Israel Eph'al concluded that the Ishmaelite genealogy reflects the Arab world as it was known in the Assyrian period. The portrait of Ishmael as "a wild ass of a man" living "over against all his kin" fits Israelite perceptions of desert nomads.

### Geography

**Shur** ("wall") is the desert on Egypt's north-eastern frontier, possibly named after the line of Egyptian border fortresses. **Kadesh** is Kadesh-barnea, usually identified with Tell el-Qudeirat in north-eastern Sinai. Excavations there found fortresses of the Iron Age (10th–6th centuries BCE) but nothing earlier. **Bered** is unknown. The geography points to the southern route between Canaan and Egypt.

### The story's duplicate

Hagar's expulsion is told again in Genesis 21:8–21, where she and the boy are sent away after Isaac's birth. Source critics see two versions of one tradition: the non-Priestly chapter 16 and a variant in chapter 21 often attributed to E. The Priestly verses 16:1a, 3, 15–16 provide dates and ages.

## Points of Agreement

- The surrogate-mother custom is genuinely ancient and widely documented.
- The Code of Hammurabi addresses almost exactly the conflict between a slave woman who has borne children and her mistress.
- The Ishmaelite genealogy corresponds to real Arab tribes and oases.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 16 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Ishmael as ancestor of a people | An ethnic etiology; the corresponding tribes appear only in 1st-millennium sources |
| Customs presumed to date the story to the 2nd millennium | Same customs attested in the 1st millennium |
| Kadesh as a known place | Archaeological remains there begin in the Iron Age |
| Hagar's flight told once | Retold differently in Genesis 21 |

## Assessment

Genesis 16 depicts a credible ancient domestic situation, one that legal texts from Hammurabi to Neo-Assyrian Nimrud addressed in similar terms. The custom is too widespread in time to date the patriarchs. The story's main historical value lies in its portrait of Ishmael, which reflects how Judeans of the Assyrian period and later understood their relationship to the Arab tribes of the desert.
