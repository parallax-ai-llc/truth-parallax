## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** This chapter is family drama with a few realistic touches of trade goods and Egyptian manners. There is no external evidence for its events, and its comment on Egyptian dining taboos matches Greek-era descriptions of Egypt as much as any earlier source.

## What the Text Claims

- The famine continues. Judah persuades Jacob to send Benjamin, offering himself as surety (43:1–14).
- Jacob sends a gift of "the choice fruits of the land": balm, honey, gum, resin, pistachio nuts and almonds, with double silver (43:11–15).
- Joseph's steward brings the brothers into the house and returns Simeon (43:16–25).
- Joseph eats separately from his brothers, and the Egyptians eat separately from both, "for the Egyptians could not eat with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians" (43:32).
- The brothers are seated in order of age, and Benjamin's portion is five times larger (43:33–34).

## External Evidence

### Gifts from Canaan

The gift list is a realistic sample of highland Canaanite produce. Pistachios (*botnim*) and almonds grow wild in the Levant, and "honey" (*devash*) may mean date or grape syrup as well as bees' honey. Balm and resins also appear in the caravan of 37:25. The goods show the writer's knowledge of the region's exports, not a specific period.

### Egyptians eating apart from foreigners

No Egyptian text sets out a general ban on eating with Semites. The closest parallel is **Herodotus** (*Histories* 2.41, 5th century BCE). He reports that Egyptians would not kiss a Greek, use a Greek's knife, spit or cooking pot, or eat meat cut with a Greek knife, because Greeks ate cattle sacred to Isis. Egyptian purity rules for priests are well documented, and foreigners were often viewed as impure in Egyptian ideology. Redford saw the remark in 43:32 as reflecting the late period, when Egyptian–foreign separation is best described.

### The steward and household

Large Egyptian households with a steward (*imy-r pr*) are well documented in every period. Washing feet and feeding donkeys on arrival (43:24) were normal acts of hospitality across the region.

## Points of Agreement

- The gift of Canaanite produce is realistic and the items match the hill country's natural products.
- Egyptian ideas of ritual purity and contempt for foreigners provide a general background for the separate tables.
- Hospitality customs and the role of the steward fit the setting.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 43 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| Egyptians will not eat with Hebrews | No specific Egyptian evidence. The closest parallel is Herodotus on Greeks |
| "Hebrews" as an ethnic term used by Egyptians | Its relation to the *ꜥapiru/ḫabiru* of 2nd-millennium texts is debated. Most scholars doubt a direct equation |
| Judah as surety (43:8–10) | Duplicates Reuben's offer in 42:37, suggesting combined sources |

## Assessment

Chapter 43 adds little that can be tested historically. Its interest lies in the writer's portrayal of Egypt: a land of plenty, strict purity and separation from foreigners. That picture matches what Greek observers recorded in the Persian period, which supports the view that the Joseph story took its final form in the 1st millennium BCE. The story's real concern is the reconciliation of the brothers and Judah's growing leadership, a theme that would have mattered to a Judahite audience.
