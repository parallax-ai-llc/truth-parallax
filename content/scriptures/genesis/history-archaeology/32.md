## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (etiological legend).** Jacob's night struggle with a mysterious figure is a legend explaining the names Israel and Penuel and a dietary custom. The places it mentions, Mahanaim and Penuel in Gilead, are real Iron Age towns, attested outside the Bible in the 10th century BCE. The story ties the ancestor of Israel to sites that mattered to the northern kingdom.

*Verse numbers follow the English and Catholic numbering (32:1–32), one lower than the Hebrew (32:2–33).*

## What the Text Claims

- Angels meet Jacob, and he names the place **Mahanaim**, "two camps" (32:1–2).
- Jacob sends messengers to Esau "in the land of Seir, the country of Edom," and divides his people into two camps out of fear (32:3–21).
- At the ford of the **Jabbok**, a "man" wrestles with Jacob until dawn and dislocates his hip (32:22–25).
- Jacob's name is changed to **Israel**, "for you have striven with God and with men and have prevailed" (32:28).
- Jacob names the place **Peniel/Penuel**, "face of God" (32:30).
- Therefore Israelites do not eat the sinew of the hip (32:32).

## External Evidence

### Mahanaim and Penuel

- The topographical list of the Egyptian pharaoh **Sheshonq I** (biblical Shishak) at **Karnak**, recording his campaign of about 925 BCE, includes both **Mahanaim** and **Penuel** among towns in the Jordan valley and Gilead. This confirms that both existed as significant places in the 10th century BCE.
- According to the Bible, Mahanaim was the capital of Ishbaal son of Saul (2 Sam 2:8) and David's refuge during Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 17:24), and **Jeroboam I** fortified Penuel (1 Kgs 12:25).
- The exact sites are debated. Twin hills by the Jabbok (Nahr ez-Zarqa), **Tell edh-Dhahab el-Gharbi** and **Tell edh-Dhahab esh-Sharqi**, are often proposed for Mahanaim and Penuel respectively.

### The name Israel

The earliest external mention of **Israel** is the **Merneptah Stele** (c. 1208 BCE), which names Israel as a people in Canaan. The **Kurkh Monolith** of Shalmaneser III (853 BCE) names Ahab "the Israelite," and the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE) mentions Israel and its god YHWH. The name contains the divine element *El*. Its original meaning was probably "El rules" or "El strives," and the explanation in 32:28 is a popular etymology.

### Hosea's version

The prophet Hosea (8th century BCE) refers to this tradition: Jacob "strove with God; he strove with the angel and prevailed, he wept and sought his favour" (Hos 12:3–4). Hosea's version differs in detail, which shows that several forms of the story circulated in the northern kingdom.

### Folklore

A river spirit or night demon who must leave before dawn and who guards a crossing is a familiar folk motif. Hermann Gunkel argued that the story was originally about such a local numen of the Jabbok, later identified with Israel's God.

### Esau in Seir

Jacob's message to Esau in "Seir, the country of Edom" assumes that Esau already ruled Edom. Chapter 36 later lists kings who reigned in Edom "before any king reigned over the Israelites" (36:31), a phrase written from the perspective of the monarchy.

## Points of Agreement

- Mahanaim and Penuel are real towns in Gilead, confirmed by the Sheshonq I list.
- The Jabbok is a real river with fords on the route between Gilead and the Jordan valley.
- The name Israel is ancient, attested from the late 13th century BCE.

## Points of Conflict

- The wrestling story is an etiology for a name, a place and a food taboo, not a historical report.
- The name Israel's real etymology differs from the one given in the story.
- The sites' prominence fits the monarchic period (10th–8th centuries BCE), when they were towns of the northern kingdom.

## Assessment

Genesis 32 shows Jacob crossing into the land through places that mattered to the kingdom of Israel. The Sheshonq I list confirms that Mahanaim and Penuel were real places by the 10th century BCE. The story's main concerns, explaining Israel's name and a dietary custom, belong to the realm of etiological legend. Its historical value lies in what it shows about northern Israelite tradition, which Hosea confirms was alive in the 8th century.
