## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (tribal poetry); valuable as a source for tribal history.** The "Blessing of Jacob" is a collection of sayings about the tribes, placed in Jacob's mouth. It is not a deathbed speech, but several sayings preserve memories of tribal situations that can be compared with other evidence.

## What the Text Claims

- Jacob gathers his sons to tell them "what will happen to you in days to come" (49:1–2).
- Reuben loses his rank for defiling his father's bed. Simeon and Levi are cursed to be "scattered in Israel" for their violence (49:3–7).
- Judah receives praise and dominion: "the sceptre shall not depart from Judah… until Shiloh comes" (49:8–12).
- Zebulun lives by the sea toward Sidon; Issachar becomes "a slave at forced labour"; Dan judges and is a serpent; Gad is raided and raids back; Asher has rich food; Naphtali is a doe let loose (49:13–21).
- Joseph receives the longest blessing, invoking the Mighty One of Jacob, Shaddai, and "blessings of the breasts and of the womb"; Benjamin is a ravenous wolf (49:22–27).
- Jacob asks to be buried at Machpelah and dies (49:29–33).

## External Evidence

### Archaic poetry

Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman argued that the poem's grammar, spelling and parallelism place it among the oldest Hebrew poetry, along with Exodus 15, Deuteronomy 33 and Judges 5. Many scholars date the core sayings to the period of the judges or the early monarchy. Others argue that archaic features can be imitated and date the collection later. The text is often very difficult. "Until Shiloh comes" (49:10) is one of the most disputed phrases in the Hebrew Bible.

### Comparison with other tribal poems

- **Judges 5** (Song of Deborah) names ten groups, including Machir and Gilead instead of Manasseh and Gad, and omits Judah, Simeon and Levi. Reuben "stays among the sheepfolds" there as well.
- **Deuteronomy 33** (Blessing of Moses) omits Simeon entirely and praises Levi as a priestly tribe, whereas Genesis 49 condemns Levi.

These differences show that tribal lists and rankings changed over time.

### Specific sayings and external data

- **Gad.** The Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE) states that "the men of Gad had dwelt in the land of Ataroth from of old." It confirms Gad as a real group in Transjordan, exposed to Moabite pressure, as the saying "raiders shall raid Gad" suggests.
- **Issachar.** Amarna letter EA 365, from Biridiya of Megiddo, mentions providing corvée workers to cultivate fields at Shunem in the Jezreel Valley. Albrecht Alt and later Nadav Na'aman suggested that the saying about Issachar as forced labourers reflects such conditions in the area the tribe later occupied.
- **Simeon and Levi.** Simeon's towns are listed inside Judah (Josh 19:1–9), and Simeon disappears as an independent tribe. Levi has no territory. The curse explains both facts after the event.
- **Dan.** Tel Dan, excavated by Avraham Biran, was an important Iron Age city in the far north, where the tribe settled after migrating (Judg 18). The Tel Dan inscription was found there.
- **Asher.** A place or group name that may correspond to Asher appears in some Ramesside texts, but the identification is disputed.

### Judah's sceptre

The royal language for Judah presupposes the Davidic dynasty. Most critics therefore date at least this saying to the monarchy or later.

## Points of Agreement

- The tribes named were real social and geographical groups in Iron Age Israel and Transjordan.
- The sayings on Gad, Simeon, Levi and possibly Issachar match independent evidence about these groups.
- The poem's archaic language supports an early date for at least some of its material.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 49 | Problem |
|------------|---------|
| Spoken by Jacob before the tribes existed | A collection of tribal sayings, presented as prophecy after the event |
| Zebulun "at the shore of the sea," near Sidon (49:13) | Joshua 19:10–16 gives Zebulun an inland territory |
| Judah holds the royal sceptre | Presupposes the Davidic monarchy |
| Levi cursed and scattered | Deuteronomy 33 praises Levi as the priestly tribe |

## Assessment

Genesis 49 is best read as an anthology of tribal sayings from different times, gathered and framed as Jacob's last words. Some sayings may go back to the pre-monarchic period, while the praise of Judah and Joseph reflects the two kingdoms. External sources such as the Mesha Stele and the Amarna letters shed light on individual sayings, which makes the poem one of the more useful texts in Genesis for social history. It is not historical reporting about Jacob.
