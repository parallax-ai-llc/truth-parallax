## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The chapter is a carefully constructed legal narrative, usually assigned to the Priestly (P) source, that establishes Israel's ancestral claim to a burial site at Hebron. Its legal form is realistic, but the closest parallels come from the 1st millennium BCE, not the 2nd. The "Hittites" of Hebron are an anachronistic or symbolic label, and the tomb cannot be verified archaeologically.

## What the Text Claims

- Sarah dies at **Kiriath-arba, that is, Hebron** (23:2).
- Abraham, "a stranger and sojourner," negotiates with the **sons of Heth** (Hittites) at the city gate (23:3–16).
- **Ephron the Hittite** sells the field and cave of **Machpelah**, east of Mamre, for **400 shekels of silver**, weighed out "according to the merchants' standard" (23:16).
- The sale is formally witnessed, and the boundaries and trees of the field are specified (23:17–20).

## External Evidence

### Hittites in Hebron?

The Hittite empire was centred in Anatolia and, at its height in the 14th–13th centuries BCE, extended into northern Syria. It never ruled southern Canaan. Hittite texts do not refer to Hebron, and no Hittite presence there has been found. In the Bible, "Hittites" often appears as one of the stock pre-Israelite peoples of Canaan (Gen 15:20; Deut 7:1). After the fall of the Hittite empire, Assyrian sources applied "Hatti" broadly to the whole of Syria-Palestine, which may explain the biblical usage. Ephron and Zohar are Semitic names, not Hittite ones.

### The legal form: Hittite or Neo-Babylonian?

In 1953 Manfred Lehmann argued that the purchase reflected Hittite land law, with obligations attached to holding a whole field. This was influential for a time as evidence of 2nd-millennium accuracy. In 1966 Gene Tucker showed that the closest parallels to Genesis 23's form, with a dialogue between buyer and seller followed by a formal record of transfer, are **Neo-Babylonian "dialogue documents"** of the 7th–5th centuries BCE. Most scholars now follow Tucker and Van Seters in seeing the chapter as reflecting 1st-millennium legal practice, consistent with a Priestly author of the exilic or Persian period.

### Silver by weight

Payment in weighed silver, before coinage, is attested throughout the ancient Near East from the 3rd millennium onward and continued until the Persian period. The phrase "current with the merchant" suggests a recognised weight standard. This detail does not narrow the date.

### Hebron and the Tomb of the Patriarchs

Ancient Hebron (Tel Rumeida) had massive Middle Bronze Age fortifications and was a significant Judahite town in Iron II. The traditional **Cave of Machpelah** lies beneath the monumental enclosure of the **Haram el-Khalil**, built in the Herodian period (late 1st century BCE). The cave below has not been excavated scientifically, and no pre-Roman evidence links the site to the tradition.

## Points of Agreement

- Hebron was an important town in both the Middle Bronze and Iron Ages.
- Cave tombs for family burial were standard in Bronze and Iron Age Canaan and Judah.
- Formal land sales witnessed at the city gate match ancient Near Eastern practice (compare Ruth 4; Jer 32:6–15).

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 23 | Historical view |
|------------|-----------------|
| Hittites own land at Hebron | No Hittite presence in southern Canaan; a stereotyped or late label |
| Contract reflects patriarchal-age law | Closest parallels are Neo-Babylonian (7th–5th centuries BCE) |
| The cave is the Herodian-era site | Identification first visible in the Roman period |

## Assessment

Genesis 23 is a legal charter. For a Priestly writer in the exilic or post-exilic period, when Judeans had lost their land, the story affirmed that the ancestors had legally owned a piece of Canaan, paid for in full and witnessed by its inhabitants. The chapter's realism lies in its 1st-millennium legal form, not in the 2nd-millennium setting it describes. The once-popular argument from Hittite law has been abandoned by most scholars.
