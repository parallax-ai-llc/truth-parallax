## Overview

This page does not read Genesis as any faith community does. It asks a different question: **what do archaeology, ancient Near Eastern texts, the natural sciences, and independent historical sources say about the events Genesis describes, and how far can the book be trusted as history?**

Genesis falls into two very different parts, and the answer differs for each.

- **The primeval history (chapters 1–11)** tells of creation, the first humans, the flood, and the tower of Babel. Mainstream scholarship reads it as **myth and legend**: an Israelite reworking of stories that were already old in Mesopotamia, retold to express Israel's own theology. Measured as a record of events, it is not history, and it should not be graded as if it were.
- **The ancestral narratives (chapters 12–50)** tell of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph. They read like family history, but no extra-biblical source mentions any of the patriarchs, and the stories contain details that fit the 1st millennium BCE better than the 2nd. Most critical scholars now regard them as traditions shaped, and largely composed, long after the period they describe.

## Composition and Date

### The documentary hypothesis and its successors

From the 18th century onward, readers noticed that Genesis tells several stories twice, with different names for God and different vocabulary. There are two creation accounts (1:1–2:4a and 2:4b–3:24), two interwoven flood stories, and three versions of a patriarch passing off his wife as his sister (chapters 12, 20, 26). Julius Wellhausen's classic synthesis (1878/1883) explained this with four sources: J (Yahwist), E (Elohist), D (Deuteronomist), and P (Priestly), combined in stages.

The four-source model has been heavily revised. Most scholars still recognise a **Priestly (P)** layer, usually dated to the Babylonian exile or early Persian period (6th–5th centuries BCE). P includes Genesis 1, the genealogies of chapters 5 and 11, much of the flood story, and chapter 17. The existence and date of a continuous "J" and especially "E" are now disputed. Many European scholars (following Rendtorff, Blum, and others) see separate blocks of tradition, such as a primeval story and an Abraham cycle, joined only late. J. Van Seters and others date the non-Priestly material to the exile as well, rather than to the 10th century as older scholarship held.

What nearly all critical scholars agree on is that Genesis is a **composite work** whose final form is no earlier than the 6th century BCE, even if it contains older traditions.

### Clues to the date of writing

- **"Ur of the Chaldeans"** (11:28, 31; 15:7). The Chaldeans (*Kaldu*) first appear in Assyrian records in the 9th century BCE and dominated southern Babylonia only in the 7th–6th centuries.
- **Domesticated camels** (12:16; 24; 37:25). Camels were not used as pack animals in the southern Levant before about the 10th–9th centuries BCE.
- **Philistines** in Gerar (chapters 21 and 26). The Philistines settled the coast in the 12th century BCE, long after any proposed patriarchal age.
- **Edomite king list** (36:31) is introduced as reigning "before any king reigned over the Israelites," a phrase that presupposes the Israelite monarchy.
- **Arameans, Ishmaelites, Kedar, Nebaioth**, and other Arab groups appear in the forms known from Assyrian sources of the 8th–7th centuries BCE.
- **Table of Nations** (chapter 10) reflects the political map of the Iron Age, including Cimmerians and Scythians, who entered the Near East only in the late 8th–7th centuries BCE.

## The Primeval History and Mesopotamia

Genesis 1–11 shares its structure and many details with Mesopotamian literature that is centuries older than any part of the Bible:

- **Enuma Elish** (Babylonian creation epic, probably late 2nd millennium BCE): creation begins from a watery deep, Tiamat (compare Hebrew *tehom*, 1:2), whose body is split to form sky and earth.
- **Atrahasis** (Old Babylonian, c. 1700 BCE): humans are made from clay; after a long period they multiply; the gods send a flood; one man survives in a boat. The sequence creation–growth–flood matches Genesis 1–9.
- **Gilgamesh Tablet XI**: the flood hero Utnapishtim builds a boat, takes animals aboard, lands on a mountain, sends out birds, and offers a sacrifice whose smell draws the gods. The parallels to Genesis 8 are close and detailed.
- **Sumerian King List**: kings with reigns of tens of thousands of years before the flood, followed by shorter reigns after it, parallels the long-lived patriarchs of Genesis 5 and 11.

The Israelite writers did not copy these texts mechanically. They reshaped them around one God, removed divine conflict, and gave humans a higher status. But the dependence on Mesopotamian tradition is not seriously disputed. The natural reading is that Genesis 1–11 belongs to the same genre: stories about origins, not records of observed events.

## The Patriarchal Age Debate

### The Albright consensus

In the mid-20th century, W. F. Albright, John Bright, Cyrus Gordon, E. A. Speiser, and others argued that the patriarchs fitted the Middle Bronze Age (c. 2000–1550 BCE). They pointed to Amorite-type personal names in the Mari archives, pastoral movement in the Fertile Crescent, and especially family customs in the **Nuzi tablets** (15th–14th centuries BCE) that seemed to match Genesis: adoption of a servant as heir, a barren wife supplying a slave woman, and the importance of household gods.

### The collapse of the consensus

In the 1970s, **Thomas L. Thompson** (*The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives*, 1974) and **John Van Seters** (*Abraham in History and Tradition*, 1975) independently dismantled these arguments. They showed that:

- Many alleged Nuzi parallels were misread, or rested on doubtful translations.
- Where genuine parallels exist, the same customs are also attested in the 1st millennium BCE, so they cannot fix a 2nd-millennium date.
- The names, place names, and peoples in Genesis fit the Iron Age at least as well as the Bronze Age.
- No archaeological "patriarchal age" can be identified.

Their work is now the starting point for critical discussion. William Dever, though more open to some early memories, concedes that the attempt to place Abraham in a specific archaeological period has failed. Finkelstein and Silberman argue that the stories reflect the world of the 8th–7th centuries BCE.

## Ancient and Modern Debate

- **Ancient readers** assumed Mosaic authorship. Already in the 12th century, Abraham ibn Ezra hinted that some verses (for example 12:6, "the Canaanites were then in the land") were written after Moses. Spinoza (1670) and Richard Simon developed the point.
- **Critical scholarship** sees Genesis as a theological prehistory of Israel, composed and compiled largely between the late monarchy and the Persian period. It treats chapters 1–11 as myth and chapters 12–50 as legends that may preserve memories of pastoral groups, tribal relations, and sanctuaries, but not reliable biographies.
- **Conservative scholarship**, represented by K. A. Kitchen, G. J. Wenham, J. K. Hoffmeier, and A. R. Millard, argues that Genesis 12–50 preserves genuine 2nd-millennium features. Examples include Amorite-type names, the price of a slave in Genesis 37:28 (20 shekels, matching Old Babylonian prices), treaty forms, and the plausibility of Asiatic migrants entering Egypt. Conservatives usually accept that Genesis 1–11 uses ancient Near Eastern imagery while maintaining that it conveys real events in some form.
- **The middle ground** taken by many scholars is that Genesis contains old traditions (for example, the Aramean connections of Jacob, or pastoral life in the hill country) that cannot be dated with confidence and cannot be tested archaeologically.

## Reliability Assessment

As a record of the **origin of the universe, life, and humanity**, Genesis 1–11 is **not history**. It is myth in the technical sense: a story of origins using the cosmology and literary inheritance of the ancient Near East. Its claims conflict with cosmology, geology, biology, linguistics, and the continuous archaeological record of ancient civilisations, including the absence of any global flood.

As a record of the **patriarchs**, Genesis 12–50 is **historically unverifiable** and contains clear anachronisms. The individuals cannot be located in any period, and the cultural details that once seemed to anchor them in the Middle Bronze Age are no longer considered decisive.

As evidence for the **world of its authors**, Genesis is valuable. Its geography, ethnography, and political assumptions reveal much about Judah and Israel in the Iron Age and the exile, and about how they related to Mesopotamia, Egypt, Aram, Edom, and Arabia.
