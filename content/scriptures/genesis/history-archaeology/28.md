## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (sanctuary foundation legend).** Jacob's dream is a cult legend explaining why Bethel was a holy place and why Israelites paid tithes there. Bethel was a real and important sanctuary, especially in the 8th century BCE, but the story's purpose is to legitimate that shrine, not to record an event.

## What the Text Claims

- Isaac sends Jacob to Paddan-aram to marry a daughter of Laban; Esau marries a daughter of Ishmael (28:1–9).
- At a place called **Luz**, Jacob dreams of a *sullam* (stairway or ramp) reaching heaven, with angels going up and down (28:10–12).
- YHWH renews the promises of land and descendants (28:13–15).
- Jacob sets up the stone he slept on as a **pillar** (*maṣṣēbâ*), anoints it with oil and names the place **Bethel**, "house of God" (28:18–19).
- He vows that the stone will be God's house and that he will give a tenth of everything (28:20–22).

## External Evidence

### Bethel

Bethel is usually identified with **Beitin**, north of Jerusalem, excavated by W. F. Albright and James Kelso. A 2009 reassessment of the finds by Israel Finkelstein and Lily Singer-Avitz concluded that Bethel was occupied in the Middle Bronze Age and in Iron I, was sparsely settled in the 10th–9th centuries BCE, and flourished especially in the **8th century BCE**. It declined sharply in the Babylonian and Persian periods.

According to 1 Kings 12:28–33, **Jeroboam I** made Bethel one of the two royal sanctuaries of the northern kingdom. Amos (7:13) calls it "the king's sanctuary and a temple of the kingdom" in the 8th century. Genesis 28 gives this sanctuary a founding story reaching back to the ancestor of Israel himself, which would have been especially valuable in that period.

### Standing stones

Standing stones (*maṣṣēbôt*) are common in Canaanite and Israelite cult places of the Bronze and Iron Ages. Examples include the row of stelae at **Gezer**, small stelae in a shrine at **Hazor**, and the standing stone in the shrine of the Judahite fortress at **Arad**. Later Deuteronomic law forbids them (Deut 16:22), so their positive role here suggests that the story predates, or at least does not share, Deuteronomic reforms.

### The stairway to heaven

The Hebrew *sullam* appears only here. Many scholars connect the image with the stepped temple-towers (ziggurats) of Mesopotamia, which were thought to link heaven and earth (compare Gen 11). Others relate it to a ramp or ladder of the kind used in siege warfare or construction. The image is mythic and does not provide historical information.

### Luz

The note that Bethel was formerly called Luz (28:19; Judges 1:23) may preserve a memory of an older Canaanite town name. No inscription confirms it.

## Points of Agreement

- Bethel was a real cult centre with a long history, and it was a royal northern sanctuary in the monarchy.
- Anointed standing stones and vows with tithes fit Iron Age religious practice (Amos 4:4 mentions tithes at Bethel).
- The route north from Beersheba through the hill country to Bethel is realistic.

## Points of Conflict

- The story serves to legitimate a sanctuary, and its divine appearance cannot be investigated historically.
- Bethel's main period of prosperity was the 8th century BCE, when the northern kingdom had most reason to tell this story.
- Name etiologies (Luz/Bethel) are typical of legend.

## Assessment

Genesis 28 belongs to a group of Jacob stories rooted in the northern kingdom of Israel, linking the ancestor to Bethel, Shechem, Mahanaim and Penuel. Finkelstein and others suggest that these traditions took shape in the northern kingdom and were adopted in Judah after Israel fell in 722 BCE. The archaeology of Bethel fits such a scenario. The chapter is valuable evidence for Israelite religion, including standing stones and tithes, but not for a historical Jacob.
