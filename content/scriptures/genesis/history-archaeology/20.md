## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** This is the second of three versions of the same story, in which a patriarch passes his wife off as his sister to a foreign king (12:10–20; 20; 26:1–11). The repetition shows that the episode is a traditional narrative pattern, retold in different sources, rather than a record of an event. Gerar is a real place, but nothing in the chapter can be verified.

## What the Text Claims

- Abraham settles in **Gerar** between Kadesh and Shur and says of Sarah, "She is my sister" (20:1–2).
- **Abimelech king of Gerar** takes Sarah, but God warns him in a dream before he touches her (20:3–7).
- Abraham explains that Sarah is his half-sister, the daughter of his father (20:12).
- Abimelech restores Sarah with gifts of livestock, slaves and silver, and Abraham prays for the healing of Abimelech's household (20:14–18).

## External Evidence

### Three versions of one story

| | Genesis 12 | Genesis 20 | Genesis 26 |
|-|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Patriarch | Abraham | Abraham | Isaac |
| Foreign ruler | Pharaoh of Egypt | Abimelech of Gerar | Abimelech, "king of the Philistines," at Gerar |
| How truth comes out | Plagues | A dream from God | Abimelech sees Isaac caressing Rebekah |
| Usual source attribution | J | E | J |

Source critics since Wellhausen have seen chapter 20 as the clearest example of the **Elohist (E)** strand: God is called *Elohim*, he speaks through dreams, and the story is concerned with moral scruples, explaining that Abraham did not strictly lie. John Van Seters argued instead that chapter 20 is a later literary revision of chapter 12, not a parallel oral variant. Either way, scholars agree it is a reworking of a shared motif.

### Gerar

Gerar is usually identified with **Tel Haror** (Tell Abu Hureira) in the western Negev, excavated by Eliezer Oren. The site was a large fortified town in the Middle Bronze Age and was also occupied in the Late Bronze and Iron Ages. The place itself therefore fits several periods, from the time often proposed for the patriarchs to the monarchy.

### The name Abimelech

*Abimelech* ("my father is king") is a good West Semitic name. A ruler of Tyre called **Abi-milki** wrote several of the **Amarna letters** (14th century BCE). The name also appears for a son of Gideon (Judges 9). It was common enough over a long period that it cannot date the story.

### Marriage to a half-sister

Abraham's claim that Sarah is his father's daughter contradicts later Israelite law, which forbids marriage to a half-sister (Lev 18:9, 11; Deut 27:22). Some scholars see this as an archaic memory; others see it as a narrator's excuse for Abraham. E. A. Speiser proposed that a Hurrian "wife-sistership" legal status at **Nuzi** lay behind these stories. That theory was later rejected by Thompson, Van Seters and others, who showed that the Nuzi texts do not support it.

## Points of Agreement

- Gerar was a real town in the western Negev, occupied over a long period.
- Abimelech is an authentic West Semitic personal name.
- Gifts of livestock, slaves and weighed silver (20:14, 16) fit ancient Near Eastern compensation customs before coinage.

## Points of Conflict

- The story is told three times with different characters, which is typical of folklore.
- Sarah is said to be desirable to a king, although according to Genesis 17:17 she is about 90.
- The Nuzi "wife-sister" explanation, once used to argue for a 2nd-millennium date, is no longer accepted.

## Assessment

Genesis 20 tells us a good deal about how the patriarchal stories were composed and edited, and little about history. A popular tale of the ancestress in danger was retold in at least three forms and given a more theological and morally careful colouring in this version. The geographical setting is realistic, but the episode itself should be classed as legend.
