## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** This long and polished story of finding Rebekah is a literary composition with clear signs of late date. Its domesticated camels, its "Aram-naharaim," and its Aramean kinsmen all fit the Iron Age far better than the early 2nd millennium BCE. Some place names in Upper Mesopotamia are authentic, which shows real geographical knowledge, but not a historical event.

## What the Text Claims

- Abraham makes his servant swear not to take a Canaanite wife for Isaac (24:1–9).
- The servant travels with **ten camels** to **Aram-naharaim**, to "the city of Nahor" (24:10).
- At a well, **Rebekah** waters his camels, fulfilling the sign he had asked for (24:12–27).
- Her brother **Laban** and father **Bethuel** agree to the marriage; she is given a gold nose ring and bracelets (24:22, 28–53).
- Rebekah travels to Canaan and marries Isaac (24:61–67).

## External Evidence

### Camels

The domestication of the dromedary camel is usually placed in the Arabian Peninsula toward the end of the 2nd millennium BCE. In the southern Levant, domestic camels become common only in the Iron Age. In 2013 Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef dated the earliest domestic camel bones from the copper-smelting sites of the Aravah valley to the last third of the 10th century BCE. Camels as beasts of burden and caravan transport are well attested in Assyrian sources from the 9th century onward.

A handful of earlier finds (figurines, isolated bones) suggest that camels were known before this, but not that they were widely used for long-distance travel in the early 2nd millennium. Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that limited early use is enough to explain the text. Most critical scholars, however, see the camels of Genesis 24 as an anachronism reflecting the time of the writers.

### Aram-naharaim and the Arameans

*Aram-naharaim* ("Aram of the two rivers") refers to the region of the upper Euphrates and the Habur. The name *Naharina* for this region appears in Egyptian New Kingdom texts and *Nahrima* in the Amarna letters. However, the **Arameans** as a people first appear in the annals of the Assyrian king **Tiglath-pileser I** (c. 1114–1076 BCE), who fought the "Ahlamu-Arameans." Calling Abraham's kin in this region "Arameans" (25:20) therefore reflects conditions after about 1100 BCE.

### The city of Nahor

Remarkably, a town called **Nahur** is mentioned in the **Mari letters** (18th century BCE) and in later Assyrian texts in the region of Harran. Other names in Terah's family, **Serug** and **Terah**, correspond to towns near Harran (Sarugi, Til-Turahi) known from Neo-Assyrian records. These correspondences show that the authors knew real place names of the Harran area, which was prominent in the 1st millennium as a centre of the moon god Sin. They do not prove an early date, because most of the names were still in use in the Neo-Assyrian period.

### Literary features

Genesis 24 is the longest chapter in Genesis and is highly repetitive and carefully crafted. The meeting at a well is a recurring "betrothal type-scene" (compare Jacob and Rachel, 29:1–14; Moses and Zipporah, Exod 2:15–21). Several scholars, notably Alexander Rofé, have argued from its language and its concern with marriage outside the local population that it was composed late, perhaps in the Persian period.

## Points of Agreement

- Upper Mesopotamian toponyms (Nahor, Haran) are authentic.
- Bride-price and gifts of gold jewellery, and the involvement of a brother in arranging a sister's marriage, fit ancient Near Eastern custom.
- Wells as meeting places for shepherds and women drawing water are realistic.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 24 | Historical view |
|------------|-----------------|
| Caravan of ten camels in the patriarchal age | Domestic camels widespread in the Levant from c. 10th century BCE |
| Rebekah's family are Arameans | Arameans first attested c. 1100 BCE |
| A precise, dated episode | A literary type-scene, possibly very late |

## Assessment

Genesis 24 is a charming and theologically rich story about divine guidance and the importance of marrying within the family. Its details, especially the camels and the Aramean identity of Rebekah's family, place its composition in the Iron Age or later. The authentic place names around Harran show real knowledge of Upper Mesopotamia, most likely gained in the Neo-Assyrian or Neo-Babylonian period.
