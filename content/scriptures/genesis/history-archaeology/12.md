## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low (historically unverifiable, with anachronisms).** No source outside the Bible mentions Abram, and the chapter cannot be placed in any archaeological period. Its picture of migrants moving from Mesopotamia to Canaan and of Asiatics seeking food in Egypt is plausible for many periods. But the domesticated camels in 12:16 point to a writer of the 1st millennium BCE, and the wife-sister story is one of three variants of the same tale.

## What the Text Claims

- The LORD tells Abram to leave his country and kindred for a land he will be shown, promising to make him a great nation (12:1–3).
- Abram, aged 75, leaves **Harran** with Sarai and Lot (12:4–5).
- He passes through **Shechem** to the oak of Moreh; "at that time the Canaanites were in the land" (12:6). He builds altars there and between **Bethel and Ai** (12:7–8), then journeys towards the **Negev** (12:9).
- Famine drives him to **Egypt**, where he passes Sarai off as his sister; Pharaoh takes her and gives Abram "sheep, oxen, male donkeys, male and female slaves, female donkeys, and **camels**" (12:10–16).
- Plagues strike Pharaoh's house, and Abram is expelled with his possessions (12:17–20).

## External Evidence

### When might Abraham have lived?

The biblical chronology (1 Kgs 6:1; Exod 12:40; Gen 21:5; 25:26; 47:9) would place Abraham's migration around **2100–1900 BCE**. Mid-20th-century scholars such as W. F. Albright tried to fit him into the Middle Bronze Age, picturing Abraham as a donkey caravaneer. Thompson (1974) and Van Seters (1975) argued that none of the supposed correlations required a 2nd-millennium date. Most critical scholars now hold that the patriarchs cannot be assigned to any specific period.

### Camels

Genesis 12:16 lists camels among Abram's livestock in Egypt. Camels appear repeatedly in the patriarchal stories (Gen 24; 31; 37:25).

- In 2013, **Lidar Sapir-Hen and Erez Ben-Yosef** analysed bones from copper-smelting sites in the Aravah Valley and found that domesticated camels appear there **no earlier than the last third of the 10th century BCE**, probably associated with the copper trade.
- In Egypt, the camel is very rare in the archaeological and textual record before the 1st millennium BCE. It became common only in the Persian period.
- Assyrian inscriptions of the 9th–7th centuries BCE regularly mention camels in connection with Arab tribes, which is the world reflected in Genesis 24 and 37.

Conservative scholars such as Kitchen point to scattered earlier evidence of camels, including figurines and bones. Even granting this, there is no evidence that Egyptians kept or gave camels as wealth in the early 2nd millennium BCE. The detail is best explained as the anachronism of a later writer.

### Asiatics in Egypt

Movement of Semitic-speaking pastoralists into Egypt is well attested:

- The tomb of **Khnumhotep II at Beni Hasan** (c. 1890 BCE) shows a group of 37 "Aamu" (Asiatics) led by a chief named Abisha, arriving with donkeys, weapons, and colourful garments. There are no camels.
- Asiatics settled in large numbers in the eastern Delta in the Middle Bronze Age, culminating in the **Hyksos** rule from **Avaris** (Tell el-Dab'a), c. 1640–1530 BCE.
- **Papyrus Anastasi VI** (late 13th century BCE) records an Egyptian border official allowing Shasu pastoralists from Edom to pass a fortress in the Wadi Tumilat to keep themselves and their herds alive.

The picture of a hungry herder seeking pasture in Egypt is therefore realistic, but it is realistic for many centuries and does not date Abram.

### Shechem, Bethel, and Ai

- **Shechem** (Tell Balata) was a large fortified city in the Middle Bronze Age and remained important later; it is mentioned in Egyptian execration texts and the Amarna letters.
- **Bethel** (usually identified with Beitin) was occupied in several periods, though its archaeological history has been debated.
- **Ai** (et-Tell) was a large city in the Early Bronze Age, but it lay **abandoned** throughout the Middle and Late Bronze Ages and was resettled only as a small village in Iron Age I. A patriarch camping "between Bethel and Ai" in the 2nd millennium would have seen only ruins at Ai.

### "The Canaanites were then in the land"

Already in the 12th century, the Jewish commentator **Abraham ibn Ezra** noted that 12:6 implies the Canaanites were no longer in the land at the time of writing, a hint that the text postdates Moses.

### The wife-sister story

The same plot appears three times: Abram and Sarai in Egypt (12:10–20), Abraham and Sarah in Gerar (20:1–18), and Isaac and Rebekah in Gerar (26:6–11). Source critics see three versions of one folk tale. E. A. Speiser proposed that it reflected a Hurrian custom from **Nuzi** of a wife adopted as a "sister" with elevated status. Later studies showed that the Nuzi documents had been misread and that no such custom existed.

## Points of Agreement

- Movement between Mesopotamia, Canaan, and Egypt along known routes.
- Shechem and the central hill country as important places.
- Asiatics entering Egypt in times of famine.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 12 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Camels as Abram's livestock | Camel domestication in the region c. 10th century BCE or later |
| Altar between Bethel and Ai | Ai was unoccupied in the Middle and Late Bronze Ages |
| Wife-sister episode | One of three versions of the same tale |
| "The Canaanites were then in the land" | Implies a writer looking back from later times |

## Assessment

Genesis 12 presents a plausible general picture of pastoral migration but provides nothing that can anchor Abram in time. The camels and the wording of 12:6 point to an Iron Age writer. The chapter is best read as the opening of a national legend of origins, perhaps built on old traditions of ancestors linked with Harran and the hill-country sanctuaries of Shechem and Bethel.
