## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The chapter combines a birth story, a family conflict and an etiology of the name Beersheba. Its reference to "the land of the Philistines" is an anachronism, and Tel Beersheba was not a settlement until the Iron Age. The stories reflect the self-understanding of Iron Age Israel and its relations with neighbouring peoples rather than events of the 2nd millennium BCE.

## What the Text Claims

- Sarah gives birth to Isaac at Abraham's age of 100; she says "God has made laughter for me" (21:1–7).
- At Sarah's demand, Abraham sends away **Hagar** and **Ishmael**; God rescues them in the wilderness and promises that Ishmael will become a great nation (21:8–21).
- Ishmael lives in the **wilderness of Paran**, becomes an archer and marries an Egyptian woman (21:20–21).
- Abraham and **Abimelech** make a covenant over a well, and the place is named **Beersheba**, "well of seven" or "well of the oath" (21:22–31).
- Abimelech and his commander Phicol return "to the land of the Philistines," and Abraham sojourns there "many days" (21:32–34).

## External Evidence

### Beersheba

**Tel Beersheba** (Tell es-Sabaʿ), excavated by Yohanan Aharoni and Ze'ev Herzog, was first settled in the Iron I period (12th–11th centuries BCE). It became a fortified royal Judahite town in Iron II, until its destruction around the time of Sennacherib's campaign (701 BCE). There is no evidence of a Middle or Late Bronze Age settlement on the tell. Wells in the area could, of course, have existed earlier, and a large well was found at the city gate. But the story's picture of Beersheba as a named, important place reflects Iron Age conditions.

### Philistines

The Philistines appear in Egyptian sources only from the reign of **Ramesses III** (early 12th century BCE), whose reliefs at **Medinet Habu** depict his battles with the "Sea Peoples," among them the *Peleset*. Their settlement in the southern coastal plain (Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron, Gath, Gaza) is documented archaeologically from about 1175 BCE onward. A "land of the Philistines" in Abraham's day, conventionally dated in the early 2nd millennium, is therefore several centuries too early. See also chapter 26.

### Ishmael and the Arabs

Ishmael is presented as the ancestor of desert peoples east and south of Judah. The Ishmaelite tribes listed in Genesis 25:13–15 are attested in Assyrian sources of the 8th–7th centuries BCE (see chapter 25). The portrait of Ishmael as an archer in the wilderness fits Israel's view of its nomadic neighbours in that period.

### Three explanations of Isaac's name

The name *Yiṣḥāq* ("he laughs") is explained three times: Abraham laughs (17:17), Sarah laughs (18:12), and here Sarah says God has made laughter (21:6). This multiplication is typical of combined sources (P, J and E) each offering its own wordplay on a name.

## Points of Agreement

- The Negev around Beersheba is accurately described as wilderness pasture, dependent on wells.
- Disputes and treaties over wells were a real feature of pastoral life in the region.
- Concubinage and the status of a slave woman's son as an heir are well attested in ancient Near Eastern law (for example, Code of Hammurabi §§170–171 on children of a slave woman).

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 21 | Historical record |
|------------|-------------------|
| Philistines living in the Gerar region | Philistines arrive c. 1175 BCE |
| Beersheba named by Abraham | Tel Beersheba founded in Iron I |
| Abraham fathers a son at 100 | Not credible biologically; a recurring motif of the promised child |
| Two etiologies for "Beersheba" (21:31; 26:33) | Doublet attributed to different sources |

Conservative scholars such as K. A. Kitchen argue that "Philistine" here is an updating of an older name for Aegean settlers present earlier on the coast. No archaeological evidence currently supports such an early Aegean presence at Gerar.

## Assessment

Genesis 21 is a set of etiologies: it explains the name Isaac, the existence and character of the Ishmaelite peoples, and the name and sanctity of Beersheba. The anachronism of the Philistines and the late foundation of Beersheba point to Iron Age storytellers who projected their own world back into the ancestral age.
