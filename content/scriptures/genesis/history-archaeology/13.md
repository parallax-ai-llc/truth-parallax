## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low to mixed.** The story of two herding families separating because the land cannot support both reflects a realistic pattern of pastoral life in the southern Levant. But the people involved cannot be verified, the chapter looks back on Sodom's destruction from a later vantage point, and the location of Sodom and the "cities of the plain" remains unknown. Lot functions in Genesis as the ancestor of Moab and Ammon, which suggests an ethnic origin story.

## What the Text Claims

- Abram returns from Egypt to the Negev and then to his earlier altar between Bethel and Ai (13:1–4).
- Abram and Lot have so many flocks that "the land could not support both of them"; their herders quarrel, "and at that time the Canaanites and the Perizzites lived in the land" (13:5–7).
- Lot chooses the **plain of the Jordan**, "well watered everywhere like the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt," and settles near **Sodom**. The narrator adds that this was "before the LORD had destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah" (13:10–13).
- God promises Abram all the land he can see, to the north, south, east, and west, and offspring as numerous as the dust (13:14–17).
- Abram settles by the oaks of **Mamre** at **Hebron** (13:18).

## External Evidence

### Pastoralism in the hill country

Archaeological surveys of the central hill country, led by Israel Finkelstein and others, show long cycles in which the population alternated between settled farming and pastoral nomadism. Pastoral groups moved seasonally between the hills, the Jordan Valley, and the Negev, and disputes over grazing and water were common. Ethnographic studies of Bedouin herding support the realism of 13:5–7. This is accurate local colour, but it fits many centuries, including the Iron Age.

### The plain of the Jordan and the location of Sodom

The Hebrew phrase *kikkar ha-yarden*, "circle of the Jordan," normally refers to the lower Jordan Valley north of the Dead Sea. Two main proposals locate Sodom and its sister cities:

- **The southern Dead Sea**: Early Bronze Age towns at **Bab edh-Dhra** and **Numeira**, excavated by Walter Rast and R. Thomas Schaub, were destroyed and abandoned in the later 3rd millennium BCE. Some writers have linked them with Sodom and Gomorrah. Their destruction is too early for any biblical chronology of Abraham, and the excavators did not claim an identification.
- **The northern Jordan Valley**: Steven Collins has argued for **Tall el-Hammam**, a large Middle Bronze Age site north-east of the Dead Sea. A 2021 paper in *Scientific Reports* proposed that a cosmic airburst destroyed the site around 1650 BCE. After strong criticism from other scientists, the journal retracted the paper in April 2025 (the authors dispute the retraction). The identification with Sodom is not accepted by most archaeologists.

No inscription from any site names Sodom or Gomorrah, and no extra-biblical text mentions them.

### "Like the land of Egypt"

The comparison to Egypt (13:10) presupposes an audience familiar with the irrigated Nile Valley. The narrator's aside about Sodom's destruction and the reference to Zoar show that the story was written after the tradition of Sodom's destruction was already established.

### Lot as an ethnic ancestor

Genesis 19:30–38 makes Lot the father of **Moab** and **Ammon**. These peoples emerged as kingdoms in Transjordan during the Iron Age, and are attested in Assyrian records and in the **Mesha Stele** (c. 840 BCE). Lot's choice of the eastern plain and Abram's of the hill country map the later division between Israel and its Transjordanian neighbours. Many scholars read the story as a political etiology of that relationship.

### Hebron and Mamre

**Hebron** (Tel Rumeida) was a fortified city in the Middle Bronze Age and again in the Iron Age. The traditional site of **Mamre** at Ramat el-Khalil, north of Hebron, has remains of a large enclosure from the Herodian period, built when the site was already venerated. Nothing at either place has been linked directly to the patriarchs. The tradition of Abraham's tomb at Hebron (Gen 23) is first monumentalised in the Herodian enclosure still standing today.

## Points of Agreement

- Realistic picture of conflicts over pasture between herding groups.
- Correct general geography of the hill country, the Jordan Valley, the Negev, and Hebron.
- Lot's association with Transjordan reflects real Iron Age political geography.

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 13 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| Sodom and Gomorrah as identifiable cities of the plain | No site securely identified; no extra-biblical mention |
| Narrator's aside "before the LORD destroyed Sodom" | Written from a later vantage point |
| Perizzites as a people of the land | Not attested in any extra-biblical source |
| Lot as the ancestor of Moab and Ammon | An ethnic etiology; Moab and Ammon appear as polities only in the Iron Age |

## Assessment

Genesis 13 combines realistic pastoral detail with a theological message about the promise of land. Its narrative maps later political relationships between Israel and its eastern neighbours onto the story of two kinsmen. Archaeology offers no way to confirm the episode, and claims linking Sodom to specific excavated sites remain speculative.
