## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Low.** The reconciliation of Jacob and Esau is a narrative resolution to the family conflict, with no external support. Jacob's purchase of land at Shechem and his altar there serve to establish Israel's claim to an important northern city and cult place. Shechem and Succoth are real sites, but the episode is a legitimating tradition rather than a historical record.

## What the Text Claims

- Jacob bows seven times before Esau, who embraces him; Jacob presses gifts on him (33:1–11).
- Esau returns to **Seir**; Jacob goes to **Succoth**, builds a house and booths for his cattle, explaining the name ("booths") (33:16–17).
- Jacob arrives safely at the city of **Shechem** and buys a plot of land from the sons of **Hamor** for **100 qesitah** (33:18–19).
- He erects an altar called **El-Elohe-Israel**, "El, the God of Israel" (33:20).

## External Evidence

### Shechem (Tell Balata)

Shechem, at **Tell Balata** near modern Nablus, was excavated by Ernst Sellin and later by G. Ernest Wright's American expedition. It was a major fortified city in the Middle Bronze Age, with massive walls, gates and a large temple-fortress. In the 14th century BCE it was the centre of a powerful kingdom led by **Labayu**, who appears in the **Amarna letters** (EA 252–254 and references in letters of other rulers) as a troublesome local ruler expanding his territory. Shechem was later an important Israelite city and briefly the first capital of the northern kingdom (1 Kgs 12:1, 25).

The story's picture of Shechem as an established non-Israelite city ruled by a local family fits the Bronze Age situation reasonably well, and it is also consistent with the Bible's own account of Shechem's mixed population in Judges 9.

### Succoth

Succoth is usually placed at or near **Tell Deir ʿAlla** in the Jordan valley, near the Jabbok. The site is best known for the **Balaam inscription** (8th century BCE), an Aramaic or Gileadite text on plaster that mentions "Balaam son of Beor," the seer of Numbers 22–24. The Succoth name etiology ("booths") is typical of the Jacob cycle's interest in place names.

### The qesitah

The unit of value *qesitah* appears only here, in Joshua 24:32 (which repeats this purchase) and in Job 42:11. Its exact meaning is unknown; the ancient versions translate it as "lambs." Its rarity has led some scholars to regard it as an archaic term, but it does not by itself establish a date.

### Joseph's tomb

Joshua 24:32 says that Joseph's bones were buried in this plot. The traditional "Joseph's Tomb" near Nablus is a structure of much later date; no ancient evidence connects it with an Israelite burial.

### El, the God of Israel

The altar's name identifies the god of Israel with **El**, the head of the Canaanite pantheon known from the Ugaritic texts. This reflects an early stage of Israelite religion in which YHWH and El were identified, as many scholars, including Mark S. Smith, have argued.

## Points of Agreement

- Shechem was a major city in both the Bronze and Iron Ages and an early centre of Israelite tradition.
- Succoth's location near the Jabbok is consistent with Jacob's route from Penuel to Shechem.
- El was a widely worshipped high god in Canaan, and the name Israel contains his name.

## Points of Conflict

- The reconciliation scene is narrative closure with no historical controls.
- Purchase of land at Shechem, like the purchase at Hebron (chapter 23), serves to legitimate Israelite claims.
- Esau's return to Seir assumes Edom's identity with Seir, attested mainly in the 1st millennium BCE.

## Assessment

Genesis 33 connects the patriarch with Shechem, one of the oldest and most important cities in the central hill country. Archaeology confirms Shechem's prominence, and the Amarna letters show that it was ruled by local dynasts long before Israel's monarchy. The story, however, is best understood as northern Israelite tradition claiming an ancestral link to the city and its sanctuary, not as a historical report.
