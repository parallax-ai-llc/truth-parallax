## Historical Verdict

**Reliability: Not applicable (theological vision narrative).** Genesis 15 is a night vision and covenant ceremony, not a report of public events. It can be assessed only for what its legal and ritual details reveal about its date and background. The animal-cutting rite has genuine ancient Near Eastern parallels in both the 2nd and 1st millennia, while the language of the land promise and the forecast of slavery in Egypt fit a writer reflecting on Israel's later history.

## What the Text Claims

- Abram complains that he is childless and that his heir will be **Eliezer of Damascus**, a servant of his household (15:2–3).
- God promises a son and descendants as numerous as the stars; Abram believes, and "it was reckoned to him as righteousness" (15:4–6).
- God says, "I am the LORD who brought you from **Ur of the Chaldeans**" (15:7).
- Abram cuts a heifer, goat, and ram in half; a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch pass between the pieces (15:9–17).
- God foretells that Abram's offspring will be oppressed in a foreign land for **400 years** and will return "in the fourth generation" (15:13–16).
- The land is promised "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the Euphrates," the land of ten peoples (15:18–21).

## External Evidence

### Cutting animals to make a covenant

The Hebrew idiom for making a covenant is literally "to **cut** a covenant." Several ancient texts describe rituals involving slaughtered animals:

- **Mari** (18th century BCE): letters refer to concluding an alliance by "killing a donkey foal" (*ḫayaram qatālum*).
- **Alalakh** (18th–17th centuries BCE): a treaty records that the king cut the throat of a sheep while swearing.
- The **Sefire treaties** (Aramaic, mid-8th century BCE) include curses comparing the fate of a violator to animals cut up in the ritual.
- The treaty between Ashurnirari V of Assyria and Mati'ilu of Arpad (mid-8th century BCE) has a ram decapitated as an image of the punishment awaiting the vassal.
- **Jeremiah 34:18–20** describes the people of Jerusalem making a covenant by passing between the halves of a calf, around 588 BCE.

The rite in Genesis 15 is thus well attested, but it spans more than a millennium. Its closest biblical parallel is Jeremiah, which suggests a 7th–6th-century context for the chapter's present form.

### A covenant of grant

Moshe Weinfeld (1970) compared God's unconditional promise to Abram with royal **land grants** in the ancient Near East, in which a king rewards a loyal servant with land guaranteed to his descendants. Examples include Hittite grants and Babylonian *kudurru* boundary stones. In Genesis 15, God alone passes between the pieces, taking the obligation on himself. The parallel is widely accepted as illuminating the form but does not date the text.

### A slave as heir: the Nuzi argument

Cyrus Gordon and E. A. Speiser argued that Eliezer's status reflected a custom attested in the **Nuzi** tablets (15th–14th centuries BCE), whereby a childless couple adopted someone, sometimes a servant, as heir, who would be displaced if a natural son was born. Thompson (1974) and Van Seters (1975) countered that the Hebrew text does not describe adoption, and that the relevant inheritance practices are also attested in Old Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian documents. The supposed parallel cannot fix the story in the Late Bronze Age.

### The land promised

The boundaries "from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates" (15:18) correspond to the idealised extent of David and Solomon's empire (1 Kgs 4:21) and to the Assyrian and Persian province called "Beyond the River" (*Abar-Nahara*). No Israelite state ever ruled so large an area, according to archaeology. The list of ten peoples (15:19–21), including the Kenites, Kenizzites, and Rephaim, is the longest such list in the Bible and appears to be a literary expansion.

### "Ur of the Chaldeans" and the 400 years

As in Genesis 11, "Ur of the Chaldeans" (15:7) uses a term for the Chaldeans not attested before the 9th century BCE. The prediction of oppression and return (15:13–16) presupposes the Exodus story and the conquest. Many scholars regard 15:13–16 as a later addition harmonising the patriarchal and Exodus traditions. The figure of 400 years conflicts with the 430 years of Exodus 12:40 and with "the fourth generation" (15:16).

### Composition

Genesis 15 contains many phrases typical of Deuteronomistic writing: "I am the LORD who brought you out" (compare Exod 20:2), the list of peoples, and the theme of faith. Van Seters, John Ha, and others have dated the chapter to the exile. Others see an older core.

## Points of Agreement

- The covenant ceremony matches genuine ancient Near Eastern practice.
- The form of an unconditional grant of land is paralleled in royal documents.
- Damascus was a known city in the 2nd millennium (it appears in Egyptian topographical lists of Thutmose III).

## Points of Conflict

| Genesis 15 | Evidence |
|------------|----------|
| "Ur of the Chaldeans" | Chaldeans attested only from the 9th century BCE |
| Oppression for 400 years | Conflicts with 430 years (Exod 12:40) and "fourth generation" (15:16) |
| Land from the Nile to the Euphrates | No Israelite kingdom ever held such territory |
| Nuzi adoption custom | The parallel is weak and not period-specific |

## Assessment

Genesis 15 is a theological text that grounds Israel's claim to the land in a solemn divine covenant. Its ritual is authentic for the ancient Near East, but the closest parallels, its anachronistic reference to the Chaldeans, its Deuteronomistic language, and its knowledge of the Exodus all point to a writer of the late monarchy or exile. It tells historians more about Judah's theology of land in that period than about Abram.
